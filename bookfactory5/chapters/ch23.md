# The Perfect Basic Bowl

This is the most important recipe in the book. Not because you will drink plain usucha every day — maybe you will, maybe you won't — but because every latte, cooler, smoothie, and cake in Parts Five and Six begins with the ninety seconds on this page. Learn this bowl and you have learned them all.

Give it three quiet attempts before you judge yourself. The first is usually a little clumsy, the second surprisingly better, and somewhere around the third your wrist discovers the speed on its own.

### Usucha — the everyday bowl

*Serves one. Takes about three minutes, ninety seconds of which is waiting for water.*

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon, or 2 scoops of a chashaku)
- 70–80 ml hot water, off the boil (70–80°C — boil, then wait two minutes)
- A wide bowl, a sifter, and a whisk or frother

**Steps:**

1. Boil the kettle. While it cools, pour a splash of the hot water into your bowl, swirl to warm it, and pour it away. Dry the bottom of the bowl with a flick.
2. Sift the matcha into the warm, dry bowl. Watch it fall like green snow. Do not skip this — Chapter 22 explains why, but the short version is: sifting is the difference between silk and gravel.
3. Pour in the water — 70 to 80 ml, which is less than you think. A bowl of usucha is small on purpose.
4. Whisk fast and light in an M or W zigzag, tines just off the bottom, wrist loose, for 15–20 seconds. You are aiming for a layer of fine, pale-green foam with no large bubbles.
5. Lift the whisk slowly out of the center. Drink within a couple of minutes, while the suspension and the foam are alive — matcha does not wait politely.

**The tricky part:** the whisking speed. Almost everyone whisks too slowly at first, gets thin foam, and blames the tea. Think of it as flicking water off your fingertips — the energy comes from the wrist, not the arm. If your arm is tired after twenty seconds, you used the wrong muscles; if your tea has fine foam, you didn't.

**One variation:** in summer, make the same bowl with cold water — sift, add cold water, whisk about twice as long. Cold-whisked usucha is brisker and greener-tasting, with softer bitterness, and it is the base for half of Part Five's iced drinks.

### Koicha — the thick tea, for when you are ready

*Serves one or two sharing. Use the best matcha you own.*

- 4 g matcha (double), 30–40 ml water (half), same temperature
- Sift, add *half* the water, and **knead** slowly with the whisk — no zigzag, no speed — until you have a smooth, glossy paste with no dry pockets
- Add the rest of the water and fold gently until it flows like warm honey
- No foam is the goal. Sip slowly. This is matcha at maximum honesty — sweet, deep, and intense — and it is why the old tea masters saved their finest leaf for it.

## When it goes wrong: the honest troubleshooting table

| What you notice | Most likely cause | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Bitter, harsh | Water too hot | Boil, then wait two full minutes before pouring |
| Bitter even with cooler water | The matcha itself (old, or low grade sold as high) | See Chapter 10; try a smaller dose (1.5 g) meanwhile |
| Little green lumps | Didn't sift, or humid powder | Sift every time; store the tin sealed and cool |
| No foam, thin body | Whisking too slow or too short | 15–20 seconds, genuinely fast, zigzag not circles |
| Big soapy bubbles | Whisked hard but in circles | Switch to the M/W motion; finish with a slow lift |
| Tastes flat, smells like hay | Tin has been open too long | Matcha fades in weeks, not years — Chapter 12 |
| Gritty last sip | Powder settled | Normal; swirl the bowl gently as you finish |
| Whisk shedding tines | Dry bamboo forced into the bowl | Soak chasen tips in warm water for a minute first |

## The habit worth keeping

However you make it, the bowl asks for about ninety seconds of your full attention — water, sift, whisk, lift. You cannot rush it, because rushing it visibly ruins it, and that makes this recipe a small daily argument against doing everything at once. The tea masters built an entire philosophy on that observation. You do not need the philosophy to enjoy the tea — but do not be surprised if, some quiet morning around your thirtieth bowl, you begin to see their point.
