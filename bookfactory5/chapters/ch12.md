# Buying and Keeping the Green

At the back of a kitchen cupboard, behind the soy sauce and a bag of dried shiitake, there is a small tin. You remember it the moment your fingers touch it: a gift, or a souvenir, or an ambitious purchase from a year ago — matcha, real matcha, bought with real intentions. You pry off the lid, and the powder inside is the color of dried grass. Khaki, maybe olive at its most generous. You lean in for the aroma and get something faint and flat, like hay in a warm barn. Somewhere in your memory is the green this tin used to hold — that lit-from-inside spring green from the first chapter of this book — and what you are looking at now is not it. Nothing went "bad" in the way milk goes bad. Nothing in this tin will hurt you. But the thing you paid for, the thing a farmer shaded and a picker selected and a stone mill ground at thirty grams an hour, has quietly left. What is left is the shell of a tea.

This chapter exists so that this never happens to you again. It is the practical close of Part Two: everything we have learned about how matcha is made, folded into two humble skills — how to buy it, and how to keep it. Neither requires expertise. Both require knowing one thing that most buyers do not: matcha is not a pantry staple. It is closer to fresh produce that happens to be dry.

## The calm buyer's checklist

The previous chapters gave you the judgment: you know that "ceremonial grade" is a marketing phrase with no legal referee behind it, you know why color and aroma tell the truth faster than labels do, and you know which regions have spent centuries earning their reputations. This section is the shopping list version — the few decisions that matter at the moment of purchase, when you are standing in a shop or scrolling a listing and the essay parts of your brain have gone quiet.

- **Buy small.** Twenty to forty grams — the size of the classic little tin — is the right first purchase, and honestly the right tenth purchase too. This feels stingy until you remember the arithmetic of a bowl: a couple of grams per serving means even a 30-gram tin holds a dozen or more bowls. A large bag is not a bargain if half of it fades before you reach the bottom; it is simply more matcha than you can drink at its best. The habit to build is small tins, finished briskly, replaced often.
- **Buy recent.** Look for a harvest date or at minimum a packing date, and prefer the seller who prints one to the seller who does not. Matcha is a seasonal, perishable product pretending to be a shelf-stable one; a visible date is the seller admitting that, which is exactly the honesty you want. If the only date on offer is a "best before" stamped two years out, treat it as what it is — a legal formality, not a description of when the tea will taste alive.
- **Buy from sellers who tell you where and when.** A seller who names the region, the harvest, and ideally the cultivar is showing you their supply chain because they are proud of it. A seller who tells you only that the matcha is "premium," "authentic," and "ceremonial" is showing you their marketing department. You do not need to memorize regions to use this test. You only need to notice whether the label answers questions or avoids them.
- **Match the grade to the job.** For drinking straight — whisked with only water — buy the best young-harvest tea you are comfortable paying for, because there is nothing to hide behind. For lattes, a step down is not a compromise but a correct choice: milk mutes the delicate top notes you paid for, while a slightly more robust, more bitter tea has the spine to speak through it. For baking, robust and affordable is genuinely ideal — the oven is coming for the subtleties anyway, as Part Six will explain. Buying one expensive tin and using it for everything is the most common way beginners waste money.
- **Expect a fair floor.** After the chapters on shading, hand-plucking, and stone mills, you can reason about price from first principles: land, labor, weeks of reduced photosynthesis, and a grinding process measured in grams per hour put a floor under what real drinking-quality matcha can cost. Prices vary too much across countries and years for this book to print a number that would stay true, but the logic travels: if a tin is priced like a commodity spice, somewhere along the line it was made like one. Suspiciously cheap "ceremonial" matcha is not a loophole you discovered. It is a label doing the work the leaf cannot.

That is the whole checklist: small, recent, traceable, matched to purpose, fairly priced. Five decisions, none of them requiring a trained palate. And then, the moment the tin is yours, the responsibility changes hands — because from here on, nothing can improve this tea, and almost everything in your kitchen is trying to ruin it.

## The most perishable "dry good" you own

Here is the uncomfortable truth the storage aisle never mentions: of all the dry things in your kitchen — flour, spices, coffee, dried mushrooms, loose-leaf tea — matcha declines the fastest. Faster than whole-bean coffee. Much faster than the sencha in the next cupboard. The reason is not that the leaf is weak. It is geometry.

Think about what the stone mill actually did. It took a leaf and broke it into particles a few microns across — fine enough to suspend in water, fine enough to feel like pressed silk. Every one of those breaks created new surface. A single intact leaf exposes only its skin to the air; grind it to powder and you have unfolded it into an enormous field of exposed surfaces, every one of them raw and reactive. This is exactly why matcha is wonderful — all that surface is why the flavor releases so completely into water — and exactly why it is fragile. The same fineness that lets water reach everything lets oxygen reach everything too.

A whole tea leaf is a sealed envelope. Matcha is that envelope opened, unfolded, and left face-up on the table. Everything that wants to react with the leaf — and several things do — now has total access.

## The four enemies

Four ordinary things degrade matcha, and it is worth knowing what each one actually does, because the storage advice stops being a list of rules and becomes obvious once the mechanisms are clear.

**Oxygen.** The air itself slowly reacts with the leaf's most delicate contents — the chlorophyll that gives matcha its color, the volatile aroma compounds, the catechins. Oxidation is the same family of chemistry that browns a cut apple, just running in slow motion across a billion tiny particles. It never sleeps and it cannot be reversed; every opening of the lid replaces the tired air in the tin with a fresh, hungry batch.

**Light.** Chlorophyll's whole job in the living plant is to absorb light, and it goes on absorbing it after the leaf is dead — except now the captured energy has nowhere useful to go and instead breaks the pigment down. This is why matcha exposed to light fades from green toward olive and yellow-brown, the exact reverse of everything those weeks of shading accomplished. It is also why serious tins are opaque metal and why a clear jar on a sunny shelf is the fastest way to un-do a farmer's work.

**Heat.** Warmth does not attack the powder by itself so much as it speeds up everything else. Chemical reactions run faster in a warm tin than a cool one, so oxidation that would take months at cellar temperature takes weeks above the stove. Heat also drives off the volatile aroma compounds — the fresh, sweet, faintly marine top notes that are the first thing to leave and the thing you miss most when they are gone.

**Humidity.** Matcha powder is desperately hygroscopic — those micron-fine particles pull moisture out of the air the way sugar clumps in a damp jar. Absorbed water makes the powder cake and clump, dulls the aroma, and, worse, water is the medium chemistry happens in: a slightly damp powder degrades faster on every other front at once. In a tropical kitchen — and this book is written in one — humidity is arguably enemy number one.

Notice that all four enemies are simply "an ordinary kitchen." Air, a window, a warm stove, a humid afternoon. Nothing exotic is attacking your tea. That is precisely why storage has to be deliberate.

## What decline looks like

You do not need lab equipment to know where a matcha is in its life. The tin tells you, in two languages.

The first is color. Fresh matcha is vividly, almost implausibly green — the electric spring green that shading built. As oxidation and light do their work, that green dulls by degrees: first it loses its brightness and goes flat, then it drifts toward olive, and finally it arrives at the khaki of the tin in the cupboard — the color of the chlorophyll gone, the color of hay. The slide is gradual, which is why it helps to really look at your matcha when you first open it, the way you looked at that first tin in chapter one. Fix the starting color in your memory and you will notice the drift.

The second is aroma. Open a fresh tin and the smell rises to meet you: sweet, green, vegetal, a hint of the seashore. As the volatile compounds escape and oxidize, that voice gets quieter and duller until what is left smells like dried grass — hay, old paper, a barn in summer. Stale matcha rarely smells *bad*, which is what makes it easy to keep using long past its best. It just smells like less and less, and then like nothing in particular.

Taste follows the nose: the umami thins out, the natural sweetness fades, and bitterness — which was always there, previously in balance — steps forward unopposed. If your usual matcha suddenly seems harsher than you remember and no change in your whisking explains it, the tea is usually telling you its age.

To be clear about the stakes: old matcha is disappointing, not dangerous. Kept dry, it does not spoil in any way that threatens you, and the end of this chapter will offer the tired tin an honorable retirement. But "safe" and "worth drinking straight" are different standards, and this book cares about the second one.

## The honest timeline

So how long does matcha actually last? Sellers are vague about this for an understandable reason: the true answer is unflattering to shelf-stable commerce. Here it is anyway, in honest ranges rather than false precision, because the speed of decline depends on the tea, the packaging, and your kitchen.

**Sealed and unopened**, a well-packed tin — filled by the producer, often flushed with nitrogen instead of ordinary air — is protected from three enemies out of four. Only heat still reaches it. Kept cool and dark, an unopened tin holds its quality for months; kept refrigerated or frozen, longer still. This is why buying ahead is fine *if* the tins stay sealed: an unopened tin is matcha in suspended animation.

**Opened**, the clock changes character entirely. The seal is broken, fresh air enters at every use, and the countdown that matters begins. Expect roughly four to eight weeks of peak character — the window in which the tea tastes the way its maker intended — before the decline becomes something you can plainly see and smell. Careful storage stretches toward the far end of that range and careless storage collapses it, but no storage stops it. An opened tin is not a possession. It is an appointment.

Put those two facts together and the buying advice from earlier in this chapter stops being a preference and becomes arithmetic. A 30-gram tin at a bowl or two a day is gone comfortably inside the peak window. A 100-gram bag on the same schedule is not. Buy the amount you will finish in six or eight weeks, and you will spend your matcha life drinking tea at its best; buy "value" sizes and you guarantee that the second half of every bag is a lesser tea than the first. Small, often, finished — that is the whole strategy.

## Where to keep it

The good news: having understood the four enemies, you already know the countermeasures. Storage is just the enemies list, inverted.

**Airtight** answers oxygen and humidity at once. Keep matcha in the most genuinely sealed container you own — the original tin if it closes well, or a small airtight canister if it does not. Smaller is better as the tea runs down, because less headspace means less air resting on the powder between uses. Open the tin only to take tea out, close it promptly, and do not leave it gaping on the counter while the kettle works.

**Dark** answers light. An opaque tin inside a cupboard is fully protected; a clear jar is acceptable only inside a closed drawer and never on an open shelf. If your matcha came in a resealable foil pouch, that pouch is doing honest work — squeeze the air out, seal it well, and stand it somewhere dark, or drop the whole pouch into a tin for armor.

**Cool** answers heat. The worst places in any kitchen are the ones we reach for out of convenience: the shelf above the stove, the counter by the window, the top of the refrigerator where the motor's warmth gathers. A low cupboard away from appliances is the honest minimum. Which raises, inevitably, the question of the coldest cupboard in the house.

**And away from smells** — the quiet fourth rule that follows from the same geometry as everything else. All that surface area absorbs aromas as eagerly as it absorbs moisture. A tin that shares shelf space with belacan, curry powder, or ground coffee will, over weeks, take quiet notes from its neighbors, and a faintly shrimp-scented usucha is an experience you only need once. Give matcha its own corner, and make sure the container seals against smells as well as air — they travel together.

## The refrigerator question

Should matcha live in the fridge? You will find confident voices on both sides, and the honest answer is that both are partly right, because they are talking about different situations.

The case for cold is real: refrigeration slows every chemical process that ages the tea, and for **sealed, unopened** tins it is close to a free win. If you have bought ahead — a tin for next month, a spare for a gift — the refrigerator is a good home for them, and the freezer is a defensible one for genuinely long storage, since a factory-sealed, nitrogen-flushed tin has almost no internal moisture to cause trouble. Producers themselves store tencha cold for exactly this reason.

The case against is also real, and it turns on a small piece of physics: condensation. Take a cold tin out of the refrigerator and open it immediately in warm, humid room air, and moisture condenses on every cold surface it touches — the tin, and the powder itself. You have personally delivered enemy number four directly onto the leaf, and in a humid climate you have delivered a lot of it. Do this daily with an opened tin and refrigeration can end up aging the tea faster than the cupboard would have.

The resolution is a single habit: **let the tin come fully to room temperature before you open it.** For a sealed tin coming out of long cold storage, take it out hours ahead — overnight is easiest — and only then break the seal. For a tin in daily use, most people are better served by the simpler regime: keep the working tin airtight in a cool, dark cupboard, drink it within its window, and let the refrigerator hold only the sealed reserves. Cold storage is a tool for the unopened; the cupboard, plus discipline about quantities, is the tool for the tin you are actually drinking.

And the year-old khaki tin from the top of this chapter? Do not pour it out. Its top notes are gone and its color would embarrass a farmer, but robust flavor remains, and Part Six of this book is full of places for it — cookies, pancakes, an ice cream — where sugar, butter, and heat will meet it more than halfway. Faded matcha makes a poor bowl and a perfectly respectable shortbread. Waste is the only unforgivable ending.

## The last link

Step back and look at what Part Two has actually described, from its first chapter to this one. Weeks of shade cloth, unrolled so the leaves would build chlorophyll and hold their sweetness. A harvest confined to the youngest leaves and a steamer waiting within hours to stop time inside them. Cold, careful storage of the tencha; granite turning at a patient rhythm so the powder would never taste heat. Sealed tins, flushed and dated. Every single stage is the same act performed by different hands: protecting a delicate leaf from air, light, warmth, and hurry. Matcha is not so much manufactured as *escorted* — passed down a chain of people who each keep it safe for the next.

When you carry a tin home, you take the last position in that chain. No skill is asked of you that a farmer would recognize as difficult: buy modestly, check a date, close a lid, choose a dark shelf, drink it while it is alive. But the chain is only as strong as its final link, and a century of craft can be undone in a month on a sunny windowsill. Keep the green, and everything the leaf was given in the shade arrives intact in your bowl.

Which raises the natural next question: arrives *what*, exactly? We have spent this whole part of the book protecting the contents of the leaf — it is time to open it up and see what those contents are. Because you drink matcha whole, you consume everything the leaf carries: its caffeine, its chlorophyll, an unusual amino acid the shade helped preserve, a family of compounds the wellness industry cannot stop shouting about, and a good deal of quiet fiber. Part Three walks into that chemistry with the same rule that governs the rest of this book — plain language, honest uncertainty, and no promises a leaf cannot keep.
