# Hot Lattes

Somewhere in the world right now, someone is standing at a cafe counter watching a barista pour steamed milk into a bowl of whisked green, and that person is thinking: *I could learn to make this.* This chapter is for that person — which, statistically, is most of the people holding this book. The matcha latte is how the leaf conquered the world, and it is almost certainly the drink you will make more often than any other in Part Five. The good news is that it is genuinely easy. The better news is that the version you make at home, once you know one rule, can be smoother than most of the ones you have paid for.

The rule is this: **paste first, always.** Nine out of ten disappointing lattes — the gritty ones, the lumpy ones, the ones with a sad archipelago of green clumps floating on top — fail for the same reason: someone poured milk onto dry powder, or dumped powder into a full mug, and hoped. Matcha will not disperse into milk. Milk is thicker than water, its proteins and fats get in the way, and the powder responds by huddling into clumps and sulking. So every recipe in this chapter begins the same way: sift the matcha, add a small amount of hot water, and work it into a smooth, glossy paste — exactly the koicha kneading motion you met in Chapter 23 — *before* any milk comes near it. The paste stage takes twenty seconds. It fixes ninety percent of everything.

One more thing before the recipes, and it may save you money: you do not need your best tin for lattes. As Chapter 10 explained, "ceremonial" and "culinary" are marketing words with no legal definition — but the honest logic behind them applies here. Milk softens, sweetens, and slightly muffles matcha, so the delicate top notes you pay a premium for in a fine sipping matcha are mostly lost under 180 ml of steamed milk. What survives is color, body, and backbone. A decent everyday or culinary matcha — fresh, reasonably green, honestly priced — is not a compromise in a latte; it is the *correct* tool, the way a sturdy cooking wine belongs in the pot and the good bottle belongs in the glass. Save the special tin for the plain bowl, where you can actually taste what you paid for.

### Classic Matcha Latte

This is the reference recipe — the one to learn thoroughly, because the other five in this chapter are variations on its skeleton. It is also, not coincidentally, the drink that carried matcha out of Japan and into every cafe on earth: the milk rounds off the bitterness, the sweetness is optional and under your control, and the color still looks like spring. Make it three times and the sequence will live in your hands.

*Serves one. About five minutes. Any milk works; whole dairy milk froths most forgivingly.*

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon) — everyday/culinary grade is right here
- 60 ml (¼ cup) hot water, off the boil (70–80°C — boil, then wait two minutes)
- 180 ml (¾ cup) milk
- Optional: 1–2 teaspoons sugar, honey, or maple syrup
- A sifter, a small bowl or wide mug, and a whisk or milk frother
- A small saucepan or steam wand for the milk

**Steps:**

1. Sift the matcha into your bowl or a wide mug. Yes, every time. Sifting is thirty seconds; picking clumps off your tongue is forever.
2. Pour in about a third of the hot water and knead with the whisk — slow, pressing strokes, no speed — until you have a smooth, glossy paste with no dry pockets. This is the step that decides the whole drink.
3. Add the rest of the water and whisk fast in the M/W zigzag from Chapter 23 for ten to fifteen seconds, until the surface is evenly foamed. You have essentially made a small, strong usucha. If you are sweetening, stir the sweetener in now, while the liquid is hot and can dissolve it.
4. Heat the milk to steaming but not boiling — around 55–65°C, which in practice means small bubbles at the pan's edge and a surface that shivers but never rolls. Boiled milk tastes flat and cooked, and it will scald the matcha's aroma too.
5. Froth the milk: a steam wand if you have one, a handheld frother, or the low-tech classic — a lidded jar, filled halfway, shaken hard for thirty seconds, then microwaved briefly to set the foam. You want wet, glossy microfoam, not stiff bubble-bath peaks.
6. Pour the milk into the matcha in a slow, steady stream, holding the foam back with a spoon at first, then spooning it on top. Taste. Adjust the sweetness — milks and matchas vary, and your palate outranks my numbers.

**The tricky part:** the milk temperature. Nearly everyone overheats it the first time, because "steamed" sounds like "very hot," and boiled milk quietly ruins a latte — the sweetness cooks out, a skin forms, and the foam turns coarse. If you have no thermometer, use your ears and eyes: take the pan off the heat at the first whisper of bubbles around the rim. Milk that you could comfortably sip immediately is right. Milk that would make you wince is too far.

**One variation:** a stronger cup for people who came from coffee — keep everything the same but use 3 g of matcha and only 150 ml (⅔ cup) of milk. The result stands up the way a double-shot latte does: greener, more assertive, and much closer to what you actually taste in a good cafe version.

### Oat-Milk Silk Latte

If you make lattes with oat milk, matcha may be the drink oat milk was waiting for. Oats bring their own gentle sweetness, which flatters matcha's grassy side, and a good barista-style oat milk froths into a dense, silky microfoam that plain dairy has to work hard to match. Because the milk is doing more of the flavor work, you can ease off the powder slightly — this is the rare latte that gets *better* when you use a little less.

*Serves one. About five minutes.*

**You need:**

- 1.5–2 g matcha (a level to heaped half-teaspoon)
- 60 ml (¼ cup) hot water, off the boil
- 180 ml (¾ cup) oat milk — a "barista" style froths best, but any works
- Optional: ½–1 teaspoon maple syrup, which agrees with oats remarkably well
- Sifter, bowl or wide mug, whisk or frother

**Steps:**

1. Sift the matcha and make your paste with a splash of the hot water — slow, glossy, no dry pockets — then whisk in the rest of the water until evenly foamed.
2. Heat the oat milk gently to steaming, not boiling. Oat milk thickens slightly as it heats, which is part of its charm; boiling pushes it past silky into gluey.
3. Froth generously. Oat foam is durable and forgiving — this is a good milk to practice your frothing on.
4. Pour into the matcha, taste before you sweeten (many oat milks are already noticeably sweet), and adjust.

**The tricky part:** oat milks vary more from brand to brand than dairy does — in sweetness, thickness, and how they froth — so your first cup with a new carton is a calibration, not a verdict. If the latte tastes muddy or heavy, the fix is usually less milk or slightly more matcha, not more sweetener.

**One variation:** the toasted-oat version — warm the oat milk with a tablespoon of rolled oats in the pan, let it sit five minutes off the heat, and strain before frothing. It deepens the cereal sweetness into something like warm porridge in the best sense, and it costs almost nothing to try.

### Honey Cloud Latte

Honey and matcha are old friends — the floral sweetness rounds off the leaf's bitter edge more gracefully than plain sugar does — but honey has a logistical problem: stirred into a finished latte, it sinks, sulks at the bottom of the cup, and greets you as a syrupy surprise in the last sip. The fix is to dissolve it at the paste stage, where the small amount of hot water melts it completely into the matcha itself. Then the whole drink is gently honeyed from first sip to last, and the spooned-on foam sits above it like the cloud in the name.

*Serves one. About five minutes.*

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 1–2 teaspoons honey — a mild one; strongly resinous honeys can bully the tea
- 60 ml (¼ cup) hot water, off the boil
- 180 ml (¾ cup) milk of your choice
- Sifter, bowl or wide mug, whisk or frother, and a spoon for the cloud

**Steps:**

1. Sift the matcha into your bowl and add the honey directly on top of the powder.
2. Add a third of the hot water and knead into a paste. The honey will melt into it as you work — keep going until the paste is smooth, glossy, and uniformly sweet-smelling.
3. Whisk in the remaining water until foamed. Taste the base now: it should be slightly sweeter than you want the final drink, because the milk will dilute it.
4. Heat and froth the milk, aiming for the thickest foam you can manage.
5. Pour in the warm milk while holding back the foam with a spoon — then spoon the foam on top in a generous heap, so the drink wears a white cloud over green. Drink through it; do not stir the cloud away.

**The tricky part:** the honey dose. Honeys differ wildly in strength, and matcha is easily drowned. Start at one teaspoon even if you suspect you want two — you can always drizzle a little extra over the foam cloud, which looks lovely and lets each sip start sweet.

**One variation:** the salted honey cloud — add the smallest pinch of fine salt to the paste along with the honey. Salt sharpens both the sweetness and the matcha's umami, the same trick that makes salted caramel work, and people will not be able to name what you did.

### Spiced Ginger-Cardamom Latte

This is the chapter's cold-weather recipe, and the one that treats milk as an ingredient rather than a backdrop. Fresh ginger and crushed cardamom steep directly in the warming milk — the technique behind every good chai — and the infused milk then meets the matcha paste. Green tea and warm spice may sound like an odd match, but cardamom in particular has a citrus-pine brightness that sits beside matcha's grassiness surprisingly comfortably; the ginger brings warmth from below. On a grey evening it is the most comforting drink in this book that still contains caffeine.

*Serves one. About ten minutes, most of it steeping.*

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 60 ml (¼ cup) hot water, off the boil
- 200 ml (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon) milk — a little extra, because straining takes a toll
- 3–4 thin coins of fresh ginger, no need to peel
- 3–4 green cardamom pods, crushed open with the flat of a knife
- 1–2 teaspoons honey or sugar — spiced lattes genuinely want some sweetness
- A small saucepan, a fine strainer, sifter, bowl or wide mug, whisk or frother

**Steps:**

1. Put the milk, ginger coins, and crushed cardamom pods in the small saucepan. Bring slowly to steaming — not boiling — then take off the heat, cover, and let it steep for five to eight minutes. Longer steep, louder spice.
2. While it steeps, sift the matcha, make your paste with a splash of the hot water, then whisk in the rest until foamed. Stir the sweetener into the hot base.
3. Strain the milk into a jug, pressing lightly on the solids, and discard the spices. Reheat for a moment if it has cooled, then froth.
4. Pour the spiced milk into the matcha, cap with foam, and dust the top with the faintest pinch of ground cardamom if you like your drinks to announce themselves.

**The tricky part:** restraint with the ginger. Cardamom fades politely, but ginger keeps extracting heat the longer it sits, and a latte that bites the back of your throat has stopped being cozy. Start with three coins and a five-minute steep; you can always go further next time. And do strain properly — a stray cardamom husk is nobody's favorite last sip.

**One variation:** a lazy weeknight shortcut — skip the steeping entirely and work a quarter-teaspoon of ground ginger and a pinch of ground cardamom into the matcha paste itself. It is blunter and a little dustier than the infused version, but it turns a ten-minute recipe into a five-minute one, and on a Tuesday that argument usually wins.

### White-Chocolate Matcha

Let us be honest about what this one is: dessert wearing a latte's clothes. White chocolate melted into hot milk makes the drink rich, sweet, and rounded, and matcha — whose slight bitterness is exactly what white chocolate lacks — cuts through the sweetness the way espresso cuts through a mocha. The pairing is a modern classic for good reason; you will meet it again in the cookie chapter. But it earns its place by being made deliberately, in a small cup, and enjoyed as the treat it is, not mistaken for a daily driver.

*Serves one, in a small cup on purpose. About six minutes.*

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 40 ml (2½ tablespoons) hot water, off the boil — less than usual; the drink is already rich
- 150 ml (⅔ cup) milk
- 20–25 g white chocolate (about 1 heaped tablespoon, chopped, or 4–5 small squares)
- No added sugar — the chocolate brings more than enough
- A small saucepan, sifter, bowl or small mug, whisk or frother

**Steps:**

1. Warm the milk gently in the saucepan. When it is hot but nowhere near boiling, drop in the chopped white chocolate and stir until it melts completely into the milk. Keep the heat low — white chocolate scorches easily and sulks when it does.
2. Sift the matcha, paste it with a splash of the hot water, and whisk in the rest until smooth and foamed.
3. Froth the white-chocolate milk briefly. It will not build tall foam — the extra fat weighs it down — but it turns glossy and thick, which suits the drink.
4. Pour the chocolate milk into the matcha, stir once from the bottom, and serve small. A little more matcha dusted on top signals, correctly, that this is not a subtle beverage.

**The tricky part:** the temptation to make it big. In a large mug this drink turns cloying by the halfway mark, and the matcha drowns. The small cup is not a garnish decision; it is the recipe working as designed — rich things want small portions and company.

**One variation:** for balance-seekers, push the matcha to 3 g and add a pinch of salt to the melting chocolate. The extra tea and the salt pull the drink back from candy toward something closer to a green-tea ganache in a cup — noticeably more adult, no less indulgent.

### Hōjicha-Matcha Half-and-Half

Hōjicha (ほうじ茶) is green tea's mellow cousin: the same leaf, but roasted over high heat until it turns russet-brown and trades its grassiness for notes of toast, caramel, and roasted nuts. A latte made from strong-brewed hōjicha layered with matcha paste gives you both characters in one cup — toasty warmth below, green freshness above — and because hōjicha is generally a lower-caffeine tea — it is often made from later-harvest leaves and stems — while this drink also uses less matcha than a full latte, the cup as a whole leans gentler on caffeine than the classic recipe. It has become a cafe favorite in Japan and beyond, and it is the recipe in this chapter most likely to convert someone who claims not to like matcha.

*Serves one. About eight minutes, including brewing the hōjicha.*

**You need:**

- 1.5 g matcha (a level half-teaspoon)
- 40 ml (2½ tablespoons) hot water, off the boil, for the paste
- 2 teaspoons loose hōjicha (or 1 teabag) brewed strong in 90 ml (⅓ cup plus 1 tablespoon) just-boiled water for 2–3 minutes — hōjicha forgives hot water; that is the roast's gift
- 120 ml (½ cup) milk
- Optional: 1 teaspoon brown sugar, which echoes the roast
- A strainer, sifter, two small vessels, whisk or frother

**Steps:**

1. Brew the hōjicha strong: full-temperature water, two to three minutes, then strain into your serving cup or glass. Stir in the sugar now if using.
2. In a separate small bowl, sift the matcha and make a slightly thick paste-and-a-little version with the 40 ml of water: paste first, then whisk the remainder in. You want it concentrated, since it is only half the tea in the drink.
3. Heat and froth the milk, and pour most of it into the hōjicha in the cup.
4. Pour the matcha slowly over the back of a spoon onto the milky hōjicha, so it settles as a green layer over the tan one, and finish with the last of the foam. Photograph it if you must — then stir the layers together, which is where the actual drink lives: toast and grass, roasted and fresh, each covering the other's gaps.

**The tricky part:** brewing the hōjicha strong enough. Brewed at the polite strength you would drink it plain, it vanishes under milk and matcha, and the drink tastes like a slightly dusty latte. Double-strength looks too dark in the cup and is exactly right in the blend — the same logic as espresso.

**One variation:** the evening lean — shift the ratio to two-thirds hōjicha, one-third matcha (1 g), keeping everything else the same. The cup gets toastier and gentler still, and it points straight at Chapter 30, where the low-caffeine hours get a whole chapter of their own.

Six hot lattes, one skeleton: sift, paste, whisk, milk. Once your hands know that sequence, you can improvise beyond this chapter with confidence — swap the milk, move the sweetener, steep something interesting in the pan — because you understand which part of the drink each decision touches. And when the weather turns and a hot cup stops sounding like comfort, the same skeleton simply gets colder: cold-whisked matcha over ice, shaken jars, cold foam floated on cold milk. That is Chapter 25, and the paste rule — you may be pleased to hear — mostly gives way there to something even easier.
