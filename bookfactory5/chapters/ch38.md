# What to Serve Beside the Bowl

On the paper in front of you sits a sweet the size of a golf ball, and it is pretending to be a flower.

This is *nerikiri* (練り切り), one of the great families of wagashi (和菓子) — the Japanese confections of bean paste and rice flour that have kept matcha company for centuries. The one in front of you has been tinted the pink of early plum blossom and shaped, by someone's thumb and a small wooden tool, into five soft petals. In a month, the same shop will be making them green and leaf-shaped; in autumn, persimmon-orange. You eat it first, before the tea is even whisked, in two or three bites off a little wooden pick. It is sweeter than you expect — frankly, honestly sweet, no apology — and just as the sweetness settles over your tongue, the bowl arrives, and the first sip of matcha lands on a palate that has been *prepared*.

You know the logic already; we met it in Part One, so one paragraph of recap will do. In the tea ceremony the sweet always comes before the bowl, not beside it and never after, because sweetness and matcha's bitterness are call and response. The sugar primes the palate; the tea's pleasant bitterness answers it, cuts it, resolves it; and the finish you are left with is cleaner and rounder than either would have managed alone. This is not etiquette for its own sake. It is a piece of taste engineering so sound that once you notice it, you find it hiding everywhere — in the chocolate on the coffee saucer, in dessert wine after dinner, in every culture that ever put something sweet next to something bracing.

This chapter is about that "something sweet" — and, occasionally, something salty or rich — in full breadth: what the Japanese tradition serves, what works from the Western pantry, and what works from mine, because I live in Malaysia and my tea moments happen a short walk from some of the best kuih on earth. None of what follows is a rulebook. Pairing is taste logic plus your own mouth, and your mouth outranks me. But taste logic is real, so let us start there.

## What the bowl wants

Strip away the poetry and matcha brings two dominant cards to the table: a clean, green bitterness and a deep savory umami. Both are strong flavors, and strong flavors pair the way strong personalities do — beautifully with a complement, badly with a rival.

What matcha wants beside it is one of three things. **Sweetness**, first and most classically: sugar is bitterness's oldest dance partner, and everything in the wagashi tradition is built on that pairing. **Fat**, second: butter, coconut milk, cream, chocolate — fat carries matcha's aroma, cushions its edge, and lingers with the umami in a way that plain sugar cannot. (This is, in miniature, why the latte conquered the world.) **A little salt**, third and least obvious: a good salted butter biscuit does something to a bowl of matcha that an unsalted one does not, the same trick salt plays on caramel and dark chocolate.

And what does the bowl fight? Two things, mainly. **Competing bitterness** — a very dark coffee dessert, a burnt caramel, a bitter-forward chocolate at the highest percentages — because two bitternesses do not resolve each other; they argue. And **sharp acidity** — anything vinegary, aggressively citric, or sour-cream tangy — which collides with matcha's vegetal side and can push the whole experience toward "grassy pond." These are not laws. They are the failure modes to know about before you experiment, and we will come back to them at the end.

One structural note, borrowed straight from the tearoom: serve the accompaniment *before* or at the very start, not as a constant back-and-forth. A bowl of usucha is small and cools quickly; it deserves a stretch of your attention on its own. Sweet first, then tea, then perhaps a last bite — a rhythm, not a buffet.

## The Japanese answers

The wagashi tradition has been solving this pairing for a very long time, and its answers are worth knowing even if you never taste half of them, because each one demonstrates the principle in a different material.

The tradition broadly divides its sweets into moist ones and dry ones. The moist sweets — *namagashi* (生菓子), of which nerikiri is the aristocrat — accompany a proper bowl at a gathering: soft, sculptural, sweet bean paste at their heart, changed with the season. The small dry sweets — *higashi* (干菓子), pressed sugar shapes that dissolve on the tongue — traditionally partner the thin tea. You do not need to memorize the taxonomy. You need the pattern: bean paste and sugar, in some soft or crisp vehicle, in a portion of two or three bites.

From the wider everyday family, four are worth seeking out. *Yōkan* (羊羹) is a firm jelly of red bean paste, sold in blocks and eaten in thin slices; it is dense, quietly sweet, and one of the cleanest partners a bitter bowl can have. *Daifuku* (大福) is a ball of soft mochi wrapped around bean paste — the chewy one, and the most beginner-friendly of the lot; the strawberry version, with fresh fruit inside, is frankly wonderful with matcha. *Dorayaki* (どら焼き) is bean paste sandwiched between two small honeyed pancakes — the most Western-shaped of the wagashi, and a generous, casual pairing rather than a refined one. *Monaka* (最中) is bean paste pressed between two shatteringly crisp wafers; the pleasure is textural, the wafer against the tea's froth.

And two everyday Japanese answers that are not wagashi at all. *Kasutera* (カステラ) — castella — is the honey sponge cake that arrived with Portuguese traders centuries ago and became thoroughly Japanese; its dense crumb and dark, sweet top edge sit beside a bowl of matcha as comfortably as anything ever has. And chocolate: Japan pairs matcha with white and milk chocolate constantly, and the logic holds at home. A single square of good chocolate before the bowl — milk, or a dark somewhere in the middle percentages rather than the punishing ones — is possibly the easiest excellent pairing in this chapter, which is exactly why it will reappear when you host someone in the next chapter.

## The Malaysian answers

Now for the section this chapter exists for, because nobody handed me these pairings; they came from living where I live and refusing to believe that a Japanese bowl needed a Japanese biscuit.

Here is the thing about Malaysian kuih: it is already built on the same chassis as wagashi. Small portions. Rice flour and starch bodies. Sweetness from palm sugar and coconut. Steamed, soft, seasonal, bought fresh in the morning and gone by afternoon. The materials differ — coconut milk where Japan uses bean paste, pandan where Japan uses sakura — but the *format* of "small sweet thing beside a bitter drink" needed no translation at all. Malaysians have been eating kuih beside bitter black coffee for generations. The bowl of matcha slots into that seat like it was measured for it.

Three kuih have earned permanent rotation at my table.

**Ondeh-ondeh.** Little green glutinous rice balls rolled in grated coconut, with a core of *gula melaka* — palm sugar — that bursts when you bite. This is, I will say it plainly, the best pairing in this chapter. Gula melaka is not plain sweetness; it is dark, smoky, almost caramel-burnt in the best way, and that depth meets matcha's bitterness the way dark honey meets it — two complex flavors that resolve instead of arguing. Eat one whole (this is structurally necessary, as anyone who has bitten one in half over a white shirt knows), let the palm sugar flood out, then lift the bowl. The tea's clean bitter finish cuts through the coconut richness and leaves you ready for — well, for the second ondeh-ondeh. There are usually five in a packet. I make no further comment.

**Kuih lapis.** The steamed layer kuih — rose-red and white in its classic form — gently sweet, springy, faintly coconut. Children famously peel it apart layer by layer, and I will not tell you to stop. It is the mildest of the three, which makes it the right choice beside a better, more delicate matcha whose subtleties you actually want to taste: enough sweetness to do the wagashi job, not enough personality to talk over the tea.

**Kuih talam.** Two steamed layers: pandan-green custard below, salted coconut-milk white above. Read that again — *salted*. The tradition already knew the sweet-fat-salt triangle this chapter opened with and built it into one two-toned square. Talam beside matcha covers every base the bowl wants at once, and the salted santan layer does for the tea what salted butter shortbread does, which we are coming to.

A word on **pandan**, because the question is obvious: pandan and matcha are both green, both aromatic, both beloved — do they work together? My honest answer: sometimes. They are two green aromatics, and in the same mouthful they compete more than they combine; pandan's sweet, almost vanilla-like fragrance and matcha's marine-vegetal one are similar enough in register to blur each other. *Beside* each other, though — a slice of pandan chiffon cake, then the bowl — the sequence works, because the cake's sweetness and softness do the priming and the pandan fades before the tea arrives. In the same recipe, I mostly leave the marriage alone. Your mileage, as with everything here, may vary, and testing it is a pleasant afternoon.

Then there is the calendar, because Malaysian pairing is seasonal the way tearoom pairing is seasonal — we just call the seasons festivals. At Mid-Autumn, a thin wedge of **mooncake** beside matcha is outstanding: lotus-seed paste is dense and sweet in almost exactly the way fine bean paste is, and a mooncake's richness genuinely needs a bitter partner — which is precisely why Chinese tradition serves it with tea in the first place. Matcha is not the traditional tea for the job, and I will not pretend it is; it is merely a very good one. At Chinese New Year, **pineapple tarts** present a harder case: buttery pastry says yes, but the jam's caramelized tang brings real acidity, which the bowl fights. My finding, offered as a finding and not a ruling: the open-faced tarts with darker, slow-cooked jam, where the sugar has gone deep and the sharpness has mellowed, sit well beside matcha; the brighter, tangier ones sit better beside something else on the CNY table.

And do not overlook the humble **teatime biscuit** tier — the marie biscuit, the butter-sugar biscuits of every Malaysian childhood tin. They are cheap, plain, and completely correct: sweetness, a little fat, no argument with the tea. The bowl does not check receipts.

## The Western answers

The Western pantry pairs with matcha more easily than people expect, for one reason: the West is extremely good at exactly the things the bowl wants — butter, sugar, and fruit.

**Shortbread** first, and not only because you may have a batch of the matcha version from the cookies chapter. Plain shortbread — butter, sugar, flour, salt — is almost a laboratory demonstration of this chapter's principle: sweetness and fat and salt in one crumbly rectangle, with no acid and no competing bitterness anywhere in sight. If you want one Western pairing to start with tonight, it is this. **Butter cakes** generalize the same logic: pound cake, madeira cake, a plain sponge — the denser and more buttery, the better the bowl answers it. This is also, not coincidentally, the castella logic wearing a different accent.

**Fruit** is the fresher road, with one rule: choose sweet-fleshed fruit, not sharp. **Mango** is the star — its perfumed sweetness against matcha's green bitterness is one of the finest pairings I know, and the reason a slice of it appears in the next chapter's hosting menu. Ripe **strawberries** work beautifully (Japan agrees; see daifuku, above). **Stone fruit** — peaches, lychees if we may smuggle them in under a Western heading, ripe plums more than sour ones — all sit well. What you are avoiding is the aggressively acidic end: citrus segments, pineapple, green apple, kiwi. Lovely fruits; wrong seat.

Now, **cheese**, because someone always asks, and honesty is the house rule: mostly, no. Aged cheeses — cheddar, parmesan, blues — bring salt, funk, and sharpness that flatten matcha's aroma and clash with its vegetal side; this is wine's territory, and matcha should not pretend otherwise. The exceptions live at the mild, fresh, creamy end: a little mascarpone (the tiramisu chapter already knew), fresh ricotta with honey, a mild cream cheese on something plain. What those have in common is that they are barely "cheese" in the sharp sense at all — they are essentially dairy fat, and dairy fat is a friend of the bowl.

## What does not work, honestly

Every pairing chapter should end its recommendations with its failures, so here are mine, found the ordinary way.

**Competing bitters.** A dark coffee dessert — tiramisu made the classic way, an espresso brownie, anything mocha — next to a bowl of matcha is two strong bitter voices at once, and neither gets to finish a sentence. Save the coffee desserts for coffee.

**Serious acid.** Lemon tart, vinegared anything, sharp yogurt, sour fruit. The collision pushes matcha's green side toward harshness. If a meal has been acidic, a sip of water and a few minutes are enough to reset before the bowl.

**Chili heat directly before.** This one matters where I live, because the odds that your afternoon began with laksa or nasi lemak are not low. Capsaicin numbs and floods the palate; a delicate bowl of usucha straight after sambal is largely wasted — you paid for subtlety your tongue cannot currently receive. The fix is not abstinence (this is Malaysia; be serious) but sequence: let half an hour and something coconut-sweet — kuih exists for this — stand between the chili and the tea.

None of these will hurt you or the tea. They just waste a good bowl, and by this point in the book you know what a good bowl costs in shade, stone, and attention.

## Setting the plate

Which brings us to the small practical habit that turns pairing into hospitality: set the sweets out *before* you whisk. Two or three bites per person, on the plainest plate you own, arranged with the thirty seconds of care you would give a guest — because that is the tearoom's real teaching hiding inside all this taste logic. The sweet before the bowl is not just palate engineering. It is the host saying, in edible form, *I thought about you before you arrived.* Even when the guest is only you.

And if an actual guest is coming — if you want to turn a plate of ondeh-ondeh and a bowl of green into a small ceremony of your own, phones away, fifteen unhurried minutes — then you are ready for the next chapter, which is entirely about that. Bring the mango.
