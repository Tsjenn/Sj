# Smoothies and Shakes

Here is a small confession that would scandalize a tea master: for every recipe in this chapter, you may put your sifter away. Twenty chapters of this book have insisted that sifting is non-negotiable — silk versus gravel, remember — and every word of that stands for whisked drinks, where your wrist is the only machine in the room. But a blender spinning at twenty thousand revolutions per minute does to a clump of matcha what your chasen could only dream of. The blades shear apart anything the humidity has stuck together, and they do it in the first two seconds. This is the one corner of the kitchen where the powder can go in straight from the tin, and I want you to enjoy that freedom, because you have earned it.

Two more permissions before we start. First: use culinary grade here, without guilt. Chapter 20 made the argument in full, but the short version is that a smoothie is a crowd, and a crowd needs a loud voice. The bright, slightly assertive edge of a good culinary matcha cuts through banana and yogurt in a way that a delicate, expensive tin simply cannot — blending your best ceremonial powder into mango is not luxury, it is waste. Second: notice that almost nothing in this chapter contains added sugar, and where sweetness appears it is fruit doing the work, or a spoon of honey or palm sugar that you can see and count. That is not because sugar is sinful — Chapter 28 is entirely devoted to dessert drinks and makes no apologies. It is because ripe fruit genuinely carries these recipes, and pretending otherwise would just be marketing in reverse. I will not tell you a smoothie is medicine because it is green. These drinks are here because they are delicious. That is the entire pitch.

The one skill this chapter asks of you is dosing. Matcha's job in a blender is not to dominate — fruit will always out-shout it on flavor — but to supply what fruit cannot: aroma, a gentle bitter edge that keeps sweetness from turning flat, and that color. Too little and you have painted a banana smoothie green for a photograph, which is the kind of dishonesty this book keeps complaining about in other people. Too much and you get chalk. The working range is 2 to 4 grams per serving — one to two teaspoons — starting at 2 and moving up only when a loud fruit demands it. Each recipe below tells you where in that range to stand.

### Banana Matcha Smoothie

This is the reference smoothie — the one to make first, the one to measure every other green blend against, and very possibly the one you will still be making years from now. Banana and matcha are one of those pairings that feels pre-arranged: the fruit brings sweetness, body, and a soft custard roundness, and the matcha brings the grassy lift that keeps all that roundness from becoming baby food. It is also the recipe that teaches this chapter's single most important technique, which has nothing to do with tea at all: the banana must be frozen.

*Serves one. Takes five minutes, plus freezing bananas the night before.*

**You need:**

- 1 ripe banana, peeled, sliced, and frozen solid (about 100–120 g / 1 cup slices)
- 200 ml milk of your choice (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon) — dairy, oat, and soy all work
- 2 g matcha (1 teaspoon), culinary grade
- 1–2 ice cubes only if your banana is not fully frozen
- Optional: 1 teaspoon honey, tasted for first, not assumed

**Steps:**

1. The night before — or whenever you have overripe bananas — peel them, slice into coins, and freeze flat on a plate or in a bag. Make this a standing habit; a freezer with banana coins in it is a freezer that is always four minutes from a smoothie.
2. Pour the milk into the blender first, then add the matcha directly onto the liquid. No sifting needed — the blades will handle it.
3. Add the frozen banana on top.
4. Blend on high for 45–60 seconds, until completely smooth and slightly thickened, with a pale foam on top. Stop, taste, and only then decide whether it needs the honey. A properly ripe banana usually says no.
5. Pour and drink soon — like every matcha drink in this book, it is best in the first few minutes, before the green settles and the foam fades.

**The tricky part:** the banana, not the matcha. A fresh, unfrozen banana makes a thin, room-temperature drink that no quantity of ice can rescue — ice dilutes as it chills, while frozen fruit chills and thickens at once, which is the entire texture secret of this chapter. And ripeness matters as much as temperature: freeze bananas at the leopard-spot stage, when the peel is well freckled, because freezing stops ripening exactly where it stands. A green banana frozen is a green banana forever.

**One variation:** add a tablespoon of natural peanut butter (about 16 g) with the banana. Peanut, banana, and matcha sounds like a collision and tastes like a milkshake with a diploma — the roasted saltiness makes the green note ring clearer, not quieter. Nudge the matcha to 3 g if the peanut flavor is a loud one.

### Mango-Matcha Lassi

If you live anywhere near the equator, as I do, mango season is not an ingredient, it is an event — and this is what to do with it. A lassi is the yogurt drink of the Indian subcontinent, adopted with enthusiasm across Southeast Asia, and it turns out that its tangy, cooling frame takes to matcha beautifully: the yogurt's sourness and the tea's bitterness lean on each other, and the mango sits sweet and perfumed on top of both. One honest warning before you start, though. Mango is the loudest fruit in this chapter, and a properly fragrant one will happily shout the matcha into the wings. That is why this recipe doses higher than the others — you are not making the tea stronger for strength's sake; you are giving it a fair chance to be heard.

*Serves one generously, or two small glasses. Takes ten minutes.*

**You need:**

- 200 g ripe mango flesh (about 1¼ cups cubed) — fresh in season, frozen cubes otherwise
- 150 g plain yogurt (⅔ cup) — regular, not Greek, for a drinkable lassi
- 60 ml cold water or milk (¼ cup)
- 3 g matcha (1½ teaspoons)
- 4–5 ice cubes if using fresh mango; none needed with frozen
- A small pinch of salt

**Steps:**

1. If working with a fresh mango, cube the flesh and taste a piece. If it is disappointing, this is the moment to stop and make the banana smoothie instead — a lassi cannot outrun a bland mango.
2. Add the water or milk to the blender, then the matcha, then the yogurt, then the mango, then the ice and the pinch of salt.
3. Blend on high for about 45 seconds until smooth and pourable. A lassi should be thinner than a smoothie — it is a drink for heat, not a spoonable dessert.
4. Taste. What you want is three flavors taking turns: mango first, yogurt tang second, and a green, faintly bitter matcha finish that arrives last and makes you take the next sip. If the finish never arrives, blend in another half teaspoon of matcha.
5. Pour into a tall glass and drink cold, ideally somewhere with a ceiling fan.

**The tricky part:** accepting what the mango does to the color. Yogurt plus mango plus matcha makes a muted, khaki-leaning green — nothing like the neon of a pure matcha drink — and no honest quantity of tea will fix that, because orange and green were always going to negotiate. Judge this drink with your mouth, not your camera. If you catch yourself adding matcha purely to improve the photograph, put the spoon down and reread Chapter 20.

**One variation:** a salted lassi lean — skip any thought of sweetener, double the pinch of salt, and add a few torn mint leaves before blending. It sounds austere and drinks like air conditioning. This version lets the matcha through more clearly, so you can drop back to 2 g.

### Avocado Matcha Shake

Everywhere I grew up, the avocado was never toast — it was a drink. Across Malaysia and Indonesia, kopitiams and street stalls blend avocado with milk, ice, and palm sugar or condensed milk into a shake so thick and silky it blurs the line between beverage and dessert. That tradition deserves to be better known on its own merits, and it happens to be the single best home matcha ever finds in a blender. Avocado has almost no flavor agenda of its own — what it contributes is fat and an impossibly smooth body — which means the matcha, for once in this chapter, gets to be the loudest voice in the glass. This is the richest recipe of the five, and the one to serve to someone who thinks they already know what a smoothie is.

*Serves one as a meal-adjacent treat, or two small glasses. Takes ten minutes.*

**You need:**

- ½ ripe avocado (about 80–100 g flesh / ½ cup scooped)
- 200 ml cold milk (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon) — full-cream dairy or coconut-blend milk suit the tradition best
- 2 g matcha (1 teaspoon)
- 2 teaspoons palm sugar syrup (gula melaka, melted) or honey — about 15 ml
- 5–6 ice cubes
- A pinch of salt

**Steps:**

1. If using palm sugar, shave about 20 g from the block and melt it with a teaspoon of water over low heat into a dark syrup. (Make extra; it keeps for weeks in the fridge and you will find uses.)
2. Check your avocado the way you would for any avocado purpose: it should yield to a gentle press. Stringy or rock-hard fruit makes a stringy or gritty shake.
3. Add the milk, then the matcha, then the avocado, syrup, salt, and ice.
4. Blend on high for a full 60 seconds. This drink needs longer than the others — you are asking the blades to shatter ice into a dense base, and stopping early leaves crunchy shrapnel in the silk.
5. Taste for sweetness — avocado brings none of its own, so unlike the rest of this chapter, the sweetener here is structural, not optional. Adjust by the half teaspoon.
6. Pour into a glass you can get a spoon into. The first inch disappears fast; the last inch is why the spoon is there.

**The tricky part:** believing the quantity of avocado is enough. Half a fruit looks stingy going into the blender and comes out having thickened the entire drink to soft-serve territory; a whole avocado makes something you would need to eat with a fork. Trust the half. If the finished shake is still too thick to pull through a straw, blend in cold milk a splash at a time — you can always thin a shake, but you cannot un-thin one.

**One variation:** the kopitiam deluxe — swap the palm sugar for a tablespoon of sweetened condensed milk, drizzled partly into the blender and partly down the inside of the glass before pouring, the way the street stalls do. It is unapologetically a dessert. Chapter 28 will feel validated.

### Berry Matcha Smoothie Bowl

Here is the honest truth about smoothie bowls: they are smoothies you eat with a spoon, and the internet has spent a decade pretending they are more mystical than that. What genuinely is different is the engineering — a bowl must be thick enough to hold toppings on its surface, which means less liquid and more frozen fruit than any drinkable blend — and the aesthetics, which for once I will defend without irony. Deep berry purple and matcha green sit almost opposite each other on the color wheel, and a green-dusted, green-drizzled purple bowl is legitimately beautiful in a way no filter needs to help. This is the one recipe in the chapter where matcha works the surface as much as the blend: dusted on top, its aroma reaches your nose a full second before the first spoonful lands.

*Serves one. Takes ten minutes.*

**You need:**

- 150 g frozen mixed berries (about 1 cup) — strawberries, blueberries, blackberries in any proportion
- ½ frozen banana (about 50 g / ½ cup slices) for body and sweetness
- 60–80 ml milk or yogurt (¼ to ⅓ cup) — start at the low end
- 2 g matcha (1 teaspoon) in the blend, plus ½ teaspoon more for dusting
- Toppings: a small handful of fresh berries, 2 tablespoons granola, 1 teaspoon toasted coconut flakes, or whatever your shelf offers

**Steps:**

1. Add the smaller amount of liquid to the blender, then the matcha, then all the frozen fruit.
2. Blend in pulses, stopping to scrape down the sides with a spatula between rounds. This is deliberately awkward — the mixture should be so thick the blades struggle. Add liquid only a tablespoon at a time, only when the blender truly stalls.
3. You are done when the texture resembles soft-serve ice cream: a spatula dragged across the top should leave a trench that keeps its shape.
4. Scrape into a shallow bowl and smooth the surface.
5. Arrange the toppings in rows or clusters rather than scattering — not for the photograph, or not only, but because it keeps crunchy things crunchy and lets every spoonful be composed instead of random.
6. Just before serving, dust the remaining half teaspoon of matcha over one side of the bowl through a small sieve. Yes, the sifter is back for the finale — a duster's job, not a declumper's. Eat immediately, before the toppings begin to sink.

**The tricky part:** resisting the liquid. Every instinct from the rest of this chapter tells you to pour until the blender runs smoothly, and that instinct produces soup with granola sinking in it. A bowl blend should fight you. If you overshoot, the rescue is more frozen banana, not patience.

**One variation:** swap the berries for 150 g frozen pineapple and keep everything else. The color scheme trades its drama for sunshine, and pineapple's acidity gives the matcha a sharper, brighter finish — dose up to 3 g in the blend, because pineapple, like mango, talks over quiet company.

### Protein Matcha Shake

This recipe earns its place by being useful rather than beautiful. Plenty of people already end a run or a gym session with a protein shake, and plenty of those shakes taste like sweetened drywall; a teaspoon of matcha is one of the cheapest upgrades that exists, cutting through the artificial-vanilla flatness with something green and actually plant-shaped. Let me be precise about what is on offer, because this is the corner of the smoothie world where marketing gets loudest: matcha will not improve your recovery, your performance, or your physique, and this book will not pretend otherwise. What it will improve is the flavor of a drink you were going to have anyway, and it happens to bring its caffeine along — welcome after a morning workout, worth remembering after an evening one (Chapter 14 has the numbers, Chapter 30 the alternatives). No blender required; this one is built for a gym bag.

*Serves one. Takes two minutes, which is the point.*

**You need:**

- 250 ml cold milk or water (1 cup) — milk for a rounder shake, water for a lighter one
- 1 serving vanilla or unflavored protein powder (typically 25–30 g — follow your tub's scoop)
- 2–3 g matcha (1 to 1½ teaspoons): 2 g against unflavored powder, 3 g to cut through a sweet vanilla one
- 4–5 ice cubes
- A shaker bottle with a mixing ball or grid

**Steps:**

1. Liquid first, always. Pour the milk or water into the shaker before any powder touches it. This one habit prevents the concrete layer at the bottom that has ruined a million gym shakes.
2. Add the matcha directly onto the liquid, then the protein powder on top of that.
3. Drop in the ice, cap the bottle, and check the cap. Genuinely check it.
4. Shake hard for a slow count of twenty — long enough for the mixing ball to break up both powders, and considerably more agitation than matcha usually gets from a whisk.
5. Rest it for thirty seconds, then give five more shakes; matcha foams enthusiastically, and the pause lets the first wave settle so the second pass smooths it. Drink cold, within a few minutes.

**The tricky part:** the order of operations. Powder poured in first glues itself to the dry bottom of the shaker the instant liquid hits it, and a mixing ball cannot scrape corners. Liquid, matcha, protein, ice — in that order, every time, and this becomes the most reliable recipe in the chapter. It is also the least forgiving of stale matcha: with no fruit to hide behind and cold liquid muting everything, a tired, hay-scented tin shows itself immediately. If your shake tastes of nothing, revisit Chapter 12 before blaming the recipe.

**One variation:** make it a full smoothie on non-rushed days — same ingredients into a blender with half a frozen banana, blended for 45 seconds. Rounder, colder, breakfast-shaped, and a natural bridge back to where this chapter began.

Five glasses, one machine, and not a single sifter troubled except for a decorative dusting. You now hold the two levers that matter in a blender: frozen fruit controls the texture, and the matcha dose controls whether the tea sings lead or harmony. What we have not yet done is let matcha be dessert on purpose — no fruit alibi, no protein pretext, just ice cream, chocolate, and condensed milk meeting the green leaf with the lights on. That is the next chapter, and I promise this book's honesty extends to enjoying it thoroughly.
