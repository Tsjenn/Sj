# Tools, Water, and the Whisk

Walk into a tea shop and you can spend a frightening amount of money on matcha equipment. Walk out with the wrong three items and you will make bitter, lumpy tea with them. So before a single recipe, let us rank every tool by how much it actually matters — and be honest about what you can skip.

## The tools, ranked honestly

**Essential: a sifter.** Not glamorous, and the single biggest upgrade to your matcha. Matcha powder is so fine that it clumps from humidity and static — those clumps are the bitter little bombs in badly made bowls, because the outside of a clump wets while the inside stays dry powder. Any small fine-mesh strainer works. Sift every time, for every recipe in this book, including the cakes. This is the closest thing to a magic trick I can teach you.

**Essential: something to whisk with.** The traditional tool is the *chasen* (茶筅), a whisk carved from a single piece of bamboo into eighty or more springy tines. It is genuinely better at the job than anything else — the tines flex at exactly the speed that folds air into suspended powder — and a decent one is not expensive. If you are not ready for one: a small electric milk frother makes a respectable everyday bowl and an excellent latte. A metal balloon whisk is the worst option; it is built for volume, not fineness. Fork? No.

**Genuinely useful: a wide bowl.** The *chawan* (茶碗) is wide for a working reason: a whisk needs room to move fast. Any bowl wider than your spread hand and deeper than your thumb will do — a cereal bowl is honestly fine to start. What does not work is a mug: the walls trap your whisk and your knuckles will report the problem immediately.

**Nice to have: the bamboo scoop.** The *chashaku* (茶杓) measures roughly one gram per scoop and feels wonderful in the hand. A half-teaspoon measure does the same job. Buy the chashaku when you want to, not because you must.

**Skip for now:** the linen cloth, the water ladle, the ceramic rest, the special canister spoon. They belong to the full ceremony, which is a beautiful thing you may grow into — Part Seven returns to it — but none of them changes what is in your bowl.

## Water: the ingredient everyone ignores

Two things about water matter more than any tool.

**Temperature.** Boiling water is the most common way good matcha is ruined. At 100°C the leaf's bitter catechins shout and its sweetness goes silent. The kind range is roughly **70–80°C**. No thermometer needed: boil your kettle, then pour the water into your empty bowl or a spare cup and let it sit for about two minutes — or pour it between two cups a few times. Warming the bowl this way has a bonus: a warm bowl whisks better and keeps your tea pleasant longer.

**Taste.** Matcha is mostly water, and it hides nothing. If your tap water tastes strongly of chlorine, your tea will too; filtered water is a cheap fix. There is no need for anything fancier — if the water tastes clean to you, it will make clean tea.

## The two teas: usucha and koicha

Traditional matcha comes in two strengths, and knowing both makes every menu and recipe in this book make sense.

**Usucha (薄茶) — "thin tea."** About 2 grams of matcha to 70–80 ml of water, whisked briskly until a fine foam forms. Bright, brisk, light-bodied, with a gentle cream of bubbles on top. This is the everyday bowl, the one the rest of this book builds on, and the one the next chapter teaches step by step.

**Koicha (濃茶) — "thick tea."** Double the matcha, half the water — and no foam at all. Instead of whisking briskly you *knead* the paste slowly until it flows like warm honey. It is intense, deeply umami, and unforgiving: koicha made from cheap matcha is punishment, which is why tradition reserves the best leaf for it. Try it when you have a tin you love. It is matcha with nowhere to hide, and on the right afternoon it is extraordinary.

## The motion

Whichever tool you hold, the motion is the same idea: **fast, light, and through the liquid — not against the bowl.**

With a chasen, the classic instruction is to whisk in a quick **M or W shape** — straight lines, wrist loose, tines just barely off the bottom of the bowl. Not circles: circles stir, but the zigzag aerates, and aeration is what turns a gritty slurry into something silky with a pale green foam. Fifteen to twenty seconds of honest speed is usually enough. Finish by lifting the whisk slowly from the center of the foam, which leaves the surface smooth with a small peak — the traditional sign of a bowl made with attention.

With a milk frother, hold it low in the bowl at an angle for ten seconds, then near the surface for five to build foam. With any tool, the failure mode is the same: too slow, too timid, too short. Matcha rewards brief, confident effort — a theme, you will find, that extends past the bowl.

## Caring for the tools

A chasen wants a rinse in warm water — never soap — a gentle shake, and drying somewhere airy, ideally standing on its handle or on a little whisk stand so the tines keep their curve. Its tines will slowly lose their spring over months of use; this is normal, not a defect. The bowl and scoop want nothing special. The sifter wants a dry brush-off, because wet matcha in a mesh becomes green cement.

That is the whole kit: a strainer, a whisk of some kind, a wide bowl, decent water off the boil. Perhaps twenty minutes of learning.
