# Glossary, FAQ, and Where to Go Deeper

Every well-used kitchen has one drawer that holds the things you reach for without ceremony — the string, the spare batteries, the instructions for the machine that beeps. This chapter is that drawer. No scenes, no arguments, no slow pours: just every term this book has used, laid out with its kanji; the twenty-five questions people actually ask across a tea counter; and honest directions for the day one book stops being enough. Reach in whenever you need to.

## The terms

**bancha (番茶)** — The humble late-harvest Japanese green tea: leaves picked after the prized spring flush, made into an everyday, inexpensive, robust brew. Not matcha's raw material, but part of its family tree on the steamed road.

***Camellia sinensis*** — The single evergreen species behind every true tea on earth: green, black, oolong, white, pu-erh, and matcha alike. Everything that distinguishes one tea from another happens after the leaf is picked; tea is one plant and a thousand years of human decisions.

**catechins** — The family of polyphenols that gives green tea much of its bitterness and astringency, and most of its "antioxidant" reputation. The most abundant and most studied member is EGCG. They are real, well-documented compounds; the disease-prevention promises attached to them in marketing are not, and this book declines to make any.

**"ceremonial grade"** — A marketing term with no legal definition anywhere — no certifying body, no inspection, no standard a tin must meet before the word goes on. Serious producers use it to gesture at real practices (first flush, full shading, fine grinding, a flavor built to drink straight); careless ones use it as decoration. Judge the tea by color, aroma, texture, transparency, price logic, and a plain bowl — never by the word.

**chanoyu (茶の湯)** — Literally "hot water for tea": the Japanese tea ceremony, a discipline built over four centuries around the preparation and sharing of a bowl of matcha. Its true subject was never really tea but attention — what happens to an ordinary act when a person decides to do one thing completely.

**chasen (茶筅)** — The tea whisk, carved from a single piece of bamboo into eighty or more springy tines. Genuinely the best tool for whisking matcha, because the tines flex at exactly the speed that folds air into suspended powder. Soak the tips in warm water for a minute before use, and it will last.

**chashaku (茶杓)** — The slender bamboo scoop used to measure matcha. One scoop carries about a gram; the standard bowl of thin tea takes two.

**chawan (茶碗)** — The tea bowl: wide enough to whisk in, comfortable to hold in both hands. In the ceremony it is often the most treasured object in the room; in your kitchen, any wide bowl that gives the whisk room to work will do.

**culinary grade** — Matcha made for cooking rather than drinking straight: usually later-harvest leaf, shorter or no shading, a more robust and bitter character. Like "ceremonial," the term is unregulated — but the category is honorable. Its loud, durable core is exactly what survives milk and ovens, which makes it the correct tool, not the cheap compromise, for lattes and bakes.

**first flush / shincha (新茶)** — The first harvest of spring, when the plant's winter-built reserves of sweetness and umami are at their peak; the leaf that becomes the finest tencha. In Japan the season's first tea, sold fresh, is celebrated as *shincha* — "new tea."

**genmaicha (玄米茶)** — Green tea blended with toasted rice: nutty, comforting, and gentle, since the rice stretches the leaf. A friendly neighbor on matcha's family tree, not an ingredient in it.

**gyokuro (玉露)** — The shaded luxury of Japanese leaf teas. Grown under cover like tencha, so it develops the same sweetness and umami — but then rolled and brewed as whole leaf rather than dried flat and ground. Think of it as matcha's closest cousin that stayed a leaf.

**hōjicha (ほうじ茶)** — Japanese green tea roasted until it turns amber: toasty, cozy, and low on the bitter notes, which is why it appears in this book's evening chapters and in gentler lattes. Roasting is its whole personality.

**ichigo ichie (一期一会)** — "One time, one meeting": the tea ceremony's reminder that this gathering, with these people, on this day, will never happen again, and deserves to be treated accordingly. The idea scales down perfectly to a Tuesday kitchen.

**kabusecha (かぶせ茶)** — Leaf tea given a brief, lighter shading — days rather than the weeks of full cover. It sits between sencha and gyokuro in sweetness, and its name comes from the covering itself.

**koicha (濃茶)** — "Thick tea": roughly double the matcha and half the water of a thin bowl, kneaded slowly into something that flows like warm honey, with no foam at all. Intense, deeply umami, and unforgiving — tradition reserves the finest leaf for it, because koicha gives cheap matcha nowhere to hide.

**L-theanine** — An amino acid unusually abundant in shaded tea leaf, and a large part of matcha's savory sweetness. Many people describe the combination of theanine and caffeine as a steadier, calmer alertness than coffee gives them; the experience varies from person to person, and this book reports it as widely shared experience with a plausible basis, not as a guarantee.

**matcha (抹茶)** — Shade-grown tencha ground to a talc-fine powder and whisked directly into water. The defining fact: you consume the entire leaf, suspended, with nothing steeped out and nothing thrown away — the single fact from which the color, the taste, the price, and most of this book follow.

**mugicha (麦茶)** — Roasted barley steeped in water: Japan's great everyday caffeine-free "tea," toasty and faintly sweet. Not a true tea at all — barley is a grain, not a tea leaf — which is exactly why it anchors the evening chapter.

**nama chocolate (生チョコ)** — The soft, Japanese-style fresh ganache chocolate — *nama* means "fresh" — cut into squares and dusted with powder. The matcha version in Part Six trades the usual cocoa dusting for green.

**ooika (覆い香)** — The "covered aroma": the fresh, sweet, faintly marine fragrance that develops specifically in shaded leaf. It is the first thing to fade from a tired tin, which makes it one of the most honest quality signals a nose can catch.

**pheophytin** — What chlorophyll becomes when heat or acid knocks the magnesium atom out of its center: a dull olive-brown pigment, the color of overcooked vegetables. It is why ovens dull matcha's green, why bright bakes stay cool and brief, and why the khaki drift of an old tin cannot be reversed.

**sakura no shiozuke (桜の塩漬け)** — Cherry blossoms pickled in salt and plum vinegar, traditionally floated in hot water as a celebration tea. In this book they lend the spring latte its faint cherry-almond perfume and whisper of salt.

**sencha (煎茶)** — The everyday standard of Japanese green tea: unshaded, steamed, rolled into slender needles, and brewed as leaf. Most of Japan's tea is sencha. Ground into powder it does not become matcha — see *tencha*.

**suspension** — What a bowl of matcha actually is: solid particles held temporarily in water, as opposed to an infusion, where water borrows flavor from leaves that are then discarded. Suspensions are not selective — you get the leaf's whole argument — and they settle, which is why matcha is whisked, drunk promptly, and swirled at the last sip.

**tencha (碾茶)** — The leaf matcha is ground from, and its sole legitimate raw material: shaded for the final weeks, steamed within hours of picking, dried flat without rolling, then stripped of stems and veins until only the soft flesh of the blade remains. Only tencha earns the stone mill.

**Uji (宇治)** — The river-valley tea region near Kyoto where Japanese matcha's craft tradition runs deepest, roughly eight centuries deep. On a tin, "Uji" names a lineage of processing with a geographic heart rather than a fence around a field — real information, unlike a grade word, but worth understanding precisely.

**umami (旨味)** — The savory "fifth taste": the deep, brothy, faintly marine satisfaction that shading builds in the leaf. It is the counterweight to matcha's bitterness, and its presence in balance is much of what separates a bowl you finish gladly from one you endure.

**usucha (薄茶)** — "Thin tea," the everyday bowl and the foundation of every recipe in this book: about two grams of matcha to 70–80 ml of water below the boil, whisked briskly in a zigzag until a fine pale-green foam forms.

**wabi (侘び)** — The aesthetic at the heart of the tea ceremony: beauty found in plainness, imperfection, and quiet — the unglazed jar, the single flower. The style of tea built on it is *wabi-cha* (侘び茶).

**wagashi (和菓子)** — Japanese confections, served before the bowl so that sweetness prepares the palate for the tea's pleasant bitterness. The logic — sweet first, then bitter — travels happily to chocolate, mango, or a good kuih.

## The questions

**Is matcha actually healthy?** It is a good drink, not a medicine. The compounds in it — caffeine, L-theanine, catechins — are real and well documented, and drinking a whole leaf means you get all of them; what is not established is the parade of disease-prevention and detox promises attached to matcha by marketing. Drink it because it is delicious and the pause does you good, and let any benefit be a quiet maybe. Chapter 15 has the full argument.

**How much caffeine does it have compared with coffee?** A standard two-gram bowl is commonly cited in the broad region of forty to eighty milligrams; a mug of brewed coffee is commonly put at eighty to a hundred and fifty or more. The ranges are wide and they overlap, because the biggest variable is not the leaf — it is the scoop, the bean, and the size of the cup. Chapter 14 walks through it honestly; the summary is that a bowl of usucha usually lands between a cup of tea and a modest coffee.

**Why is matcha so expensive?** Because every stage of making it properly subtracts yield and adds labor: weeks of shading that reduce the harvest, hand-picking of only the youngest spring leaves, steaming within hours, stripping out stems and veins, and granite mills that grind roughly one small tin per hour. That stack of slow choices is a cost floor, not a markup — which is also why suspiciously cheap "ceremonial" matcha almost never is.

**Is culinary grade bad?** No — it is differently purposed, and often the smarter buy. Milk and ovens erase exactly the delicate qualities you pay a premium for, and culinary matcha's robust core is engineered to survive them. Keep two tins with two jobs; Chapter 10 makes the case in full.

**Does matcha expire?** It fades rather than spoils. Old matcha will not hurt you; it simply loses its aroma, its vivid green, and its sweetness, drifting toward hay and khaki within weeks of opening, not years. A faded tin still makes respectable cookies — waste is the only unforgivable ending.

**Should I keep it in the fridge?** Sealed, unopened tins: yes — cold storage is close to a free win, and the freezer is defensible for long-term reserves. The tin you use daily is better off airtight in a cool, dark cupboard, because a cold tin opened in warm humid air collects condensation on the powder itself. If you do refrigerate, let the tin come fully to room temperature before breaking the seal. Chapter 12 has the whole regime.

**Why is my matcha bitter?** First suspect the water: off the boil is too hot, and 70–80°C is the kind range — boil, then wait two minutes. If it is still harsh with cooler water, the powder itself is the likely culprit: old, or a low grade sold as a high one. Chapter 23's troubleshooting table and Chapter 31 rank the causes.

**Why does it clump?** Because matcha is a talc-fine powder with enormous surface area, and fine powders clump — especially in humid air. Sift every time, without exception, and store the tin sealed and cool. Sifting is the difference between silk and gravel.

**I don't have a bamboo whisk. Is a milk frother okay?** Yes. A small electric frother makes a respectable everyday bowl and an excellent latte — hold it low in the bowl first, then near the surface to build foam. The chasen is genuinely better at the job and not expensive, so it is worth growing into; a balloon whisk is the worst option, and a fork is not one.

**I'm pregnant — can I drink matcha?** The one thing to know is that a proper bowl carries meaningful caffeine — the neighborhood of a cup of coffee, not weak tea — and because you drink the whole leaf, you cannot brew your way around it. How much caffeine is appropriate in pregnancy or breastfeeding is a genuinely medical question, so this book will not hand you a number: mention your matcha habit to your doctor or midwife and let them give you guidance that is actually theirs to give. Most people come away with an adjustment rather than a farewell.

**Can children have matcha?** As an occasional flavor — ice cream, a mild matcha milk — rather than a daily bowl, because caffeine dose is a matter of body size and a moderate bowl for you is a large one for a seven-year-old. Keep servings small and early in the day, and save the true habit for a body that has finished growing. Chapter 17 borrows Japan's own sensible model.

**I read that matcha blocks iron. Should I worry?** Tea polyphenols can genuinely reduce how much plant-source iron your body absorbs from a meal eaten at the same time — old, uncontroversial nutrition knowledge that applies to black tea too. For most well-nourished people it is a shrug; if you are managing anemia or low iron, simply drink matcha between meals rather than with them, keep it away from any iron supplement, and ask your clinician how they want you to time things. Timing defuses almost all of it.

**Should I worry about lead or contamination?** Worry is the wrong size; diligence is the right one. Because you consume the entire leaf, you consume its history, so sourcing matters more than with steeped tea: buy from producers who name where the leaf grew and when it was milled, favor origins with strong testing cultures, and treat an impossibly low price as information. Traceable tea from a reputable Japanese producer is the boring, effective answer.

**Is matcha vegan?** The powder itself is nothing but ground tea leaf, so yes. Watch the things around it — milk in lattes, white chocolate, condensed milk — and swap as the recipes in Parts Five and Six show; oat milk in particular is a genuinely good partner, not a concession.

**Does putting matcha in a latte cancel the health benefits?** The question assumes the benefits were the point, and that framing is the trap. Milk does mute delicate flavors and binds some tea polyphenols, so a latte is a different, softer experience than a plain bowl — but you were never drinking a medicine that milk could ruin. Make the latte because you like it; Chapter 18 explains what milk actually does.

**What is the real difference between ceremonial and culinary?** In honest hands: harvest timing, shading, leaf selection, grind fineness, and whether the flavor is built to be drunk straight or to shout through milk and butter. In practice: neither term is regulated anywhere, so the words tell you what the seller wants you to assume. Chapter 20 gives you the six tests that replace them.

**Why is my powder khaki instead of green?** Because vivid green is chlorophyll, and chlorophyll is destroyed by light, heat, air, and time — or was never there in quantity, if the leaf was barely shaded or late-picked. Olive powder is always a fact about the tea, whatever the label says: whether it started dull or faded in storage, it cannot deliver what "ceremonial" promises. Use it in the oven, and buy smaller tins next time.

**Can I bake with my expensive matcha?** You can, but the oven cannot tell the difference and neither will your guests. Heat evaporates the delicate aromatics, converts the green chlorophyll toward olive pheophytin, and spares mostly the bitterness — so what survives baking is exactly what culinary grade already offers at a fraction of the price. Chapter 32 explains why saving the good tin for drinking is competence, not stinginess.

**Can I substitute ordinary green tea powder for matcha?** In baking, at a pinch, with expectations adjusted: powdered sencha is bitterer, duller, and coarser, because it was never shaded, de-veined, or stone-ground. In a bowl, no — grinding ordinary green tea does not make matcha, and whisking will not save it. The word matcha should mean powdered tencha; the process is the definition.

**What is the pale film or layer on my drink?** Settling, almost always. Matcha is a suspension, and suspensions settle — an overnight cold-brew separates into a pale top and a dense green bottom, and a rested bowl leaves a fine film behind. Nothing has gone wrong; stir or shake and carry on. On a hot latte, the skin is the milk's doing, not the tea's.

**Can I reheat matcha?** Better not to. A bowl is built to be drunk within minutes, while the suspension and foam are alive; reheating gives you settled powder, dulled aroma, and — since heat amplifies staleness — often a harsher taste than the first pour. A bowl takes ninety seconds to make; make a fresh one.

**How long does an open tin really last?** Expect roughly four to eight weeks of peak character once the seal is broken, stretched by careful storage and collapsed by careless storage, but never stopped. Buy amounts you will finish inside that window — small, often, finished. An opened tin is not a possession; it is an appointment.

**What water should I use?** Water at the right temperature — 70 to 80°C, reached by boiling and waiting two minutes — matters far more than water pedigree. Beyond that, one rule: if your tap water tastes strongly of chlorine, your tea will too, and a basic filter is the cheap fix. If the water tastes clean to you, it will make clean tea.

**Is Chinese matcha fake?** No — the process is the test, not the passport. Powdered tea was born in China, and a grower anywhere who shades, steams, de-veins, and grinds with care is making real matcha; equally, powder milled in Japan from unshaded leaf is not. Chapter 6 draws the line properly: judge the tencha method and the tea in the bowl, never the flag on the tin.

**How do I learn the real tea ceremony?** From a person, not a book — this one included. Chanoyu is a living discipline passed teacher to student through the established tea schools, many of which have branches and study groups far beyond Japan, and a beginner is welcome at more of them than you might guess. Go, bow badly, hold the bowl the wrong way, and be corrected kindly; Chapter 39's home ritual will keep you company in the meantime.

## Where to go deeper

When this book runs out, the trail does not. The deepest well is the tea ceremony tradition itself: the major Japanese tea schools publish their own curricula, journals, and beginner materials, and translations of the tradition's classic texts on tea and its aesthetics are widely available. Reading what the schools themselves teach — rather than summaries of summaries — is the difference between hearing about the symphony and reading the score. If a school teaches near you, one afternoon as a guest will teach your hands what no page can.

For the craft and the leaf, go to the source side of the industry. Japan's national and regional tea organizations, and the producer regions themselves — Uji, Nishio, Yame, Kagoshima — publish real material about cultivation, shading, processing, and grading standards, much of it translated. Producer websites from serious mills often contain more honest education than any reseller's blog, for the simple reason that people proud of their process like explaining it. Museums are the unexpected treasure here too: major collections in Japan and worldwide hold tea bowls, whisks, caddies, and tearoom artifacts spanning centuries, usually with catalogues that double as excellent histories of the objects your kitchen versions descend from.

For the science, skip the headlines and go to the literature they garble: peer-reviewed food-science and nutrition journals carry the actual research on tea chemistry — catechins, theanine, caffeine, chlorophyll degradation — complete with the sample sizes, caveats, and "further research is needed" that the headlines strip away. You do not need a degree to read an abstract and a conclusion, and the habit of doing so is the single best vaccine against matcha mythology in both its miraculous and its fearmongering strains.

And the most underrated syllabus of all costs nothing but attention: pick one good, transparent producer and taste your way up their ladder, from their culinary grade to their finest tin, one at a time, plain, in the same bowl. Every concept in this book — shading, harvest, grind, freshness — becomes a flavor you can point to when the only variable changing is the grade. Your tongue, patiently educated, outranks every label ever printed.

That is the whole drawer, and the whole book. Somewhere near you there is a tin; inside it, a leaf that was grown in the dark on purpose, ground by stone at the pace of stone, and asked to wait for you. It only ever needs the same three things it needed on page one — water not quite boiling, a whisk moved with intent, and ninety seconds of your undivided company. Give it those, as often as your weeks allow, and you will have understood everything this book knows.
