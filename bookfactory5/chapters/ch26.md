# Sparkling and Refreshers

Somewhere around the middle of every hot afternoon, the idea of a warm bowl of anything stops making sense — and this is the chapter that takes over. A sparkling matcha drink, made properly, is the best-looking thing you will produce from this entire book: a band of deep green resting on a column of pale, rising bubbles, the two layers holding their border for a slow minute before they blur into each other like weather. Cafes charge handsomely for that minute. You can make it at home with a spoon, a glass, and the ninety-second skill you learned in Chapter 23.

But carbonation has rules, and the first one matters enough to put in plain warning language: never, ever spoon dry matcha powder onto an open bottle or a full glass of anything fizzy. Carbonated water is crowded with dissolved gas looking for any excuse to leave, and every particle of a fine powder is an excuse — thousands of tiny surfaces for bubbles to form on at once. Sift matcha onto soda water and the glass erupts into a column of green foam that climbs over the rim, across the counter, and onto the floor, all in about two seconds, while you stand there holding the sifter. I am describing this vividly because I have mopped it up. The fix is simple and permanent: **always make a matcha paste first** — powder kneaded smooth with a small splash of still water, exactly the koicha motion from Chapter 23 — and introduce that liquid paste to the bubbles, never the powder itself.

The other two rules are gentler. First, layer by pouring slowly over the back of a spoon: hold a spoon upside down just above the surface of the drink, bowl-side up toward you, and let the matcha paste (thinned slightly) trickle onto it so it fans out and settles as a distinct green stratum instead of punching straight down. Second, drink promptly. Carbonation is the whole delivery system here — each bursting bubble throws a little of the matcha's aroma up toward your nose — and a sparkling matcha that has gone flat is a sadder drink than one that was never fizzy at all. These are not drinks that wait on the counter while you find your phone to photograph them. Photograph fast, then drink. Five recipes follow, from the austere to the crowd-pleasing, and every one of them uses the same paste-then-layer method, so once you have made the first, you own all five.

### Matcha Tonic

*Serves one. Takes about five minutes. This is the reference recipe for the whole chapter — make it first.*

On paper this drink should not work. Tonic water is bitter; matcha is bitter; stacking one bitterness on another sounds like a punishment. In the glass, something better happens: the quinine bitterness of the tonic is bright and citrusy, the matcha bitterness is deep and green, and they sit at opposite ends of the tongue like two notes an octave apart. Add tonic's sweetness — most tonic water carries more sugar than people realize — and the result is grown-up, balanced, and strangely refreshing. This is the sparkling matcha that cafes and bars discovered first, and it is still the best one to learn the pouring technique on, because the pale tonic shows off the green layer like a lit window.

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 30 ml still water, room temperature or cool (2 tablespoons)
- 200 ml tonic water, straight from the fridge (about ¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon)
- A tall glass full of ice cubes — genuinely full
- A small bowl and whisk or frother for the paste, and a spoon for layering
- Optional: a wedge of lime or a thin round of lemon

**Steps:**

1. Sift the matcha into a small bowl. Add the still water and knead with the whisk — slow, smooth, no zigzag — until you have a glossy, lump-free liquid paste, about the thickness of cream. This is the koicha motion from Chapter 23 doing weekday work.
2. Fill the glass to the top with ice. More ice than feels polite. A crowded glass keeps everything colder and gives the layers something to rest on.
3. Pour in the cold tonic water down the side of the glass, stopping about two fingers below the rim.
4. Hold a spoon upside down just above the tonic's surface and pour the matcha paste slowly onto the back of it, letting it spread and settle. Pour with patience — the slower you go, the sharper the green band.
5. Carry it to whoever is going to drink it, let them look at it for ten seconds, then stir once and drink while it is loudly fizzy. Squeeze in the lime at the table if using.

**The tricky part:** the pour, and specifically the speed of it. Everyone pours too fast the first time; the paste torpedoes through the tonic and you get a uniform pale-green drink — which still tastes fine, but you have lost the show. Slow down until it feels almost silly. If your paste is too thick to pour smoothly, loosen it with a teaspoon of water; if it is too thin, it will not layer at all, so start thick and thin it gradually. Tonic brands also vary a lot in sweetness and bitterness — taste yours on its own once so you know what the matcha is landing on, and adjust.

**One variation:** for guests who find tonic-plus-matcha one bitterness too many, make it half tonic and half plain soda water. You keep the fizz and the look, halve the quinine, and the drink turns noticeably gentler — a good gateway pour before graduating them to the full version.

### Sparkling Yuzu Matcha

*Serves one. Takes about five minutes, once you have yuzu syrup in the house.*

Yuzu — the knobbly Japanese citrus that tastes like a committee of lemon, mandarin, and grapefruit reaching an unusually good decision — is matcha's most natural citrus partner, and this is the prettiest drink in a chapter of pretty drinks: a floor of golden syrup, a column of bubbles, a ceiling of green. Fresh yuzu is hard to find in most places, but you do not need it. Jars of yuzu marmalade sold for making Korean *yuja-cheong* tea, or bottled yuzu syrup, both work beautifully — they are mostly yuzu peel and sugar, which is exactly what this drink wants.

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 30 ml still water (2 tablespoons)
- 25–30 ml yuzu syrup or yuzu marmalade (about 1½–2 tablespoons — start low; brands vary in sweetness)
- 180 ml cold soda water (¾ cup)
- A tall glass of ice, a small bowl and whisk for the paste, a spoon for layering

**Steps:**

1. Make the matcha paste: sift the matcha into the bowl, add the still water, and knead until glossy and smooth.
2. Spoon the yuzu syrup or marmalade into the bottom of the empty glass. If you are using marmalade with big pieces of peel, that is a feature, not a problem — they look like stained glass at the bottom.
3. Fill the glass with ice, then pour the soda water gently down the side. Do not stir yet — the syrup stays low, and that is the point.
4. Layer the matcha paste over the back of a spoon onto the surface, slowly, as in the matcha tonic.
5. Serve with a long spoon or straw. The drinker stirs from the bottom, dragging gold up through green, and drinks while it fizzes.

**The tricky part:** the sweetness balance, because yuzu products range from tart syrups to candy-sweet marmalades. The drink should finish refreshing, not sticky; the matcha needs enough bitterness left to answer the citrus. Start with less syrup than you think, taste after the first stir, and add more only if the yuzu is getting lost. If your marmalade is very thick, loosen it with a teaspoon of hot water before it goes in the glass, or it will sit at the bottom in a stubborn lump and the first half of the drink will be plain soda.

**One variation:** no yuzu anywhere nearby? Stir together 1 tablespoon of fresh lemon juice, 2 teaspoons of honey, and a little finely grated mandarin or orange zest, and use that as the floor of the glass. It is not yuzu, and I will not pretend otherwise — but it is aiming at the same target, and the drink still works.

### Cucumber-Mint Matcha Spritz

*Serves one. Takes about seven minutes. The quietest drink in the chapter, and the one for the hottest day.*

Where the matcha tonic is a statement, this is a sigh. Cucumber and mint are the two most cooling things in the produce drawer, and matcha — used gently here, at a lower dose than usual — slides in underneath them as a green baseline rather than a headline. This is the drink for the afternoon that is too hot for opinions, and it is also the one to hand to someone who insists they want "something refreshing, not something intense." Note the smaller amount of matcha: this recipe is deliberately soft-spoken, and doubling the powder does not improve it.

**You need:**

- 1.5 g matcha (about 1 level half-teaspoon)
- 30 ml still water (2 tablespoons)
- 5 thin slices of cucumber, plus 1 more for the glass
- 6–8 fresh mint leaves, plus a small sprig for the top
- 2 teaspoons simple syrup (or 1½ teaspoons sugar dissolved in a splash of warm water)
- 1 teaspoon fresh lime juice
- 180 ml cold soda water (¾ cup)
- A tall sturdy glass, ice, a muddler or the handle of a wooden spoon, a small bowl and whisk

**Steps:**

1. Put the cucumber slices, mint leaves, syrup, and lime juice in the bottom of the glass. Press down gently with the muddler a few times — you are bruising the mint and crushing the cucumber slightly, not making a paste. Six or eight soft presses is enough.
2. Make the matcha paste in the small bowl: sift, add the still water, knead until smooth.
3. Fill the glass with ice, dropping the extra cucumber slice in against the wall of the glass where it will show.
4. Pour in the soda water gently, then layer the matcha paste over the back of a spoon.
5. Top with the mint sprig — give it one sharp clap between your palms first, which releases its smell — and serve with a straw or long spoon. Stir once at the table.

**The tricky part:** the muddling, because mint punishes enthusiasm. Pressed gently, mint gives up its cool, sweet aroma; pounded hard, the torn leaves release harsh, bitter, swampy notes and shred into flecks that stick in teeth and straws. If your instinct says "more force," override it. You want the leaves bruised and fragrant but still recognizably leaf-shaped when you are done.

**One variation:** for a party pitcher, scale everything by six, but muddle the cucumber, mint, syrup, and lime in the pitcher first, add ice and soda just before guests arrive, and float the matcha in a wide layer poured over a large serving spoon. Stir it at the table in front of everyone — the moment the green folds into the pale drink is the closest this book comes to theater.

### Matcha Ginger Ale

*Serves one, with ginger syrup left over for about six more. Syrup takes twenty minutes; the drink itself takes five.*

Ginger and matcha are both, in their own ways, warming and sharp — and homemade ginger syrup runs rings around anything from a can, because you control the heat. This recipe therefore comes in two halves: a small batch of real ginger syrup you make once and keep in the fridge, and the two-minute assembly you repeat all week. The syrup is deliberately simple — ginger, sugar, water — and once it is in the fridge door you will find it wandering into tea, lemonade, and Chapter 27's smoothies without asking permission.

**You need:**

- For the syrup: 80 g fresh ginger, unpeeled and thinly sliced (about ¾ cup of slices); 100 g sugar (½ cup); 200 ml water (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon)
- For each drink: 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon); 30 ml still water (2 tablespoons); 25–30 ml of the ginger syrup (1½–2 tablespoons); 180 ml cold soda water (¾ cup)
- A small saucepan and a jar for the syrup; a tall glass, ice, small bowl, whisk, and spoon for the drink
- Optional: a wedge of lime

**Steps:**

1. Make the syrup: put the ginger slices, sugar, and water in the saucepan, bring to a simmer, and let it bubble gently for 15 minutes with the lid off. Turn off the heat and let it sit until cool — the resting time pulls out as much flavor as the simmering did. Strain into a clean jar and refrigerate; it keeps for about two weeks.
2. For each drink, make the matcha paste: sift the matcha into the bowl, add the still water, knead smooth.
3. Pour the ginger syrup into the glass, fill with ice, and add the soda water gently down the side.
4. Layer the matcha paste over the back of a spoon onto the surface.
5. Serve immediately, lime wedge on the rim if you like. Stir at the table and drink while the ginger still prickles.

**The tricky part:** ginger itself, which varies enormously — young, smooth-skinned ginger is mild and floral, older wrinkled roots can be fiercely hot, and fifteen minutes of simmering treats them very differently. So taste your finished syrup on a spoon before you build the first drink, and let that taste set your dose: a mild syrup might want a full 2 tablespoons, a fiery one might want half that. This is the "brands vary — taste and adjust" rule of the baking chapters, applied to a root.

**One variation:** add the juice of half a lime to the glass along with the ginger syrup and you have made, in effect, an alcohol-free matcha mule — sharper, brighter, and the version I would hand to a cocktail drinker who claims soft drinks bore them.

### Green Apple Matcha Fizz

*Serves one. Takes about five minutes. The chapter's diplomat.*

Every matcha drinker eventually meets someone who announces, with the confidence of settled law, that they hate matcha. Make them this. Apple juice and matcha share a family of green, orchard-ish flavors, so instead of fighting, they finish each other's sentences — the apple rounds off matcha's bitterness, the matcha stops the apple from being merely sweet, and the soda lifts the whole thing into something that tastes like a green apple sweet from a better universe. It is the least challenging recipe in this chapter and the most requested. There is no shame in that. Gateways matter.

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 30 ml still water (2 tablespoons)
- 100 ml good apple juice, cold — cloudy pressed juice if you can get it (a scant ½ cup)
- 100 ml cold soda water (a scant ½ cup)
- 1 teaspoon fresh lemon juice
- A tall glass, ice, small bowl, whisk, and spoon
- Optional: 2 thin slices of green apple for the glass

**Steps:**

1. Make the matcha paste: sift the matcha into the bowl, add the still water, and knead until completely smooth and glossy.
2. Pour the apple juice and lemon juice into the glass and give them one stir.
3. Fill the glass with ice, sliding the apple slices down against the inside wall if you are using them.
4. Add the soda water gently down the side of the glass — pouring it after the juice keeps the fizz lively instead of stirring it away.
5. Layer the matcha paste over the back of a spoon, admire the green-on-gold for the ten seconds it deserves, then serve. Stir once and drink promptly.

**The tricky part:** the apple juice, which does the sweetening for the entire drink and varies from bracing to syrupy depending on the carton. Clear, sweet juice from concentrate can tip the drink toward soft-drink territory — the lemon juice in the recipe is there precisely to pull it back, so do not skip it. If your juice is very sweet, nudge the ratio toward soda; if it is a tart cloudy pressing, nudge toward juice. The drink is right when the first sip reads as apple and the swallow reads as green.

**One variation:** freeze chunks of apple juice in an ice-cube tray and use them in place of half the ice. As they melt they strengthen the drink instead of watering it down — a trick worth stealing for every other recipe in this chapter, and one we will lean on hard in the frozen desserts of Part Six.

That trick — fruit doing the work of ice — is also the perfect handoff, because the next chapter abandons the spoon-layered elegance of sparkling drinks for the blender's cheerful violence. Smoothies forgive everything this chapter fussed over: no pastes, no pouring technique, no racing the bubbles. You will throw matcha, fruit, and ice into a jug, press a button, and get something thick, cold, and green enough to drink with a spoon. Different rules entirely. Turn the page.
