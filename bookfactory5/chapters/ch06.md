# One Plant, Many Teas

Here is a fact that surprises almost everyone the first time they hear it: green tea, black tea, oolong, white tea, pu-erh, and matcha all come from the same plant. Not the same family of plants — the same species. *Camellia sinensis*, an unassuming evergreen shrub with glossy leaves and small white flowers, cousin to the camellias people grow for their blooms. Every true tea on earth begins as its leaf.

If one plant makes every tea, then everything that distinguishes them — color, flavor, strength, price — must come from what people *do* to the leaf after it is picked. Tea is not many plants. Tea is one plant and a thousand years of human decisions.

Matcha is what happens when you make the most extreme set of decisions available.

## The family tree, in one walk

Imagine a freshly plucked tea leaf, and walk with it down different roads.

Take the leaf and simply let it wither and dry with minimal fuss, and you get **white tea** — the least processed, palest, quietest member of the family.

Let the leaf fully oxidize — bruise it, let its enzymes darken it the way a cut apple browns, then dry it — and you get **black tea**, which most of the world outside East Asia grew up calling simply "tea." The oxidation builds the malty, brisk, red-brown character.

Stop that same oxidation partway — anywhere from a little to a lot — and you get the vast middle kingdom of **oolong**, which can taste like flowers or like toast depending on where the maker chose to stop.

Now take the road matcha starts on: **kill the enzymes immediately** so that no oxidation happens at all, and you get green tea. In China this is usually done with dry heat — pan-firing in a wide wok, which gives Chinese greens their toasty, chestnut warmth. In Japan it is done with **steam**, seconds of it, which locks in a greener, more vegetal, more oceanic character. That single choice — steam, not pan — is much of why Japanese green tea tastes the way it does.

Every Japanese green tea you have heard of lives on this steamed road: sencha, the everyday standard; gyokuro, the shaded luxury; bancha, the humble late harvest; genmaicha, tea cut with toasted rice; hōjicha, tea roasted until it turns amber and cozy. And at the very end of the steamed road, past a gate most leaves never reach, sits matcha.

## The gate: what makes tencha

Matcha is not simply "green tea, ground up." Grind sencha in a mill and you get a bitter, dull powder that no amount of whisking will save. Real matcha is ground from a leaf called **tencha (碾茶)** — a leaf that exists for no other purpose — and tencha differs from ordinary green tea in three deliberate ways.

**It is shaded.** For the final weeks before harvest, the plants are covered and the sunlight is taken away from them. The next chapter is entirely about this strange, brilliant move; for now, know that shade is where the color, the sweetness, and the umami come from.

**It is steamed but never rolled.** Ordinary sencha is rolled and kneaded into thin needles as it dries — rolling ruptures cells and shapes the leaf for infusion. Tencha is steamed and then dried *flat*, unrolled, like tiny green leaves of paper. It is not meant to be infused; it is meant to be ground, and grinding wants a brittle, open leaf.

**It is stripped to almost nothing.** Stems and veins — bitter, woody, pale — are removed, by air and machine, until what remains is only the soft flesh of the leaf blade. It takes a lot of harvest to make a little tencha. This is one of the honest reasons matcha costs what it costs; you are buying only the best part of the leaf, with the rest already paid for and discarded.

Only this — shaded, steamed, unrolled, de-veined leaf flesh — earns the stone mill. Ground fine, tencha becomes matcha. Ground coarsely, or brewed whole, tencha is actually drinkable on its own, and tea people prize it as a curiosity; but its destiny was always powder.

## Cousins, imposters, and honest neighbors

Knowing the family tree protects you from the two most common confusions in the tea aisle.

The first is **powdered green tea that is not matcha**. Grinding ordinary sencha or bancha into powder is cheap and increasingly common, and sold honestly — as "green tea powder" for smoothies or baking — it is a perfectly reasonable product. Sold dishonestly, it borrows the word matcha and the price that comes with it. The tell is usually color (olive and dull rather than vivid), texture (slightly coarse rather than silken), and a bitterness no water temperature can tame. Part Two's later chapters will give you a full buyer's defense; the first line of it is simply knowing that the word matcha *should* mean shaded, de-veined, stone-ground tencha — and that no law in most countries forces it to.

The second is the **"matcha" of other traditions**. You may meet Chinese powdered teas, Korean garu-cha, or new-world powders grown outside Japan. Some are genuinely made the tencha way and can be lovely; some are ordinary leaf, powdered. The country is not the fraud test — the process is. A grower anywhere who shades, steams, de-veins, and grinds with care is making real matcha, wherever their farm sits. This book uses the Japanese vocabulary because Japan perfected and named the craft, not because a border makes the tea.

## Why this chapter matters for your cup

Everything you will read in the rest of Part Two — the shading, the harvest rush, the stone mills, the grades, the regions — happens *between* the family tree and your tin. When a matcha disappoints you, the cause is almost always a corner cut somewhere along that road: leaf that was never properly shaded, stems that were never removed, a mill that ran too hot, a tin that sat too long in a warehouse. And when a matcha astonishes you — when the powder smells like fresh peas and cut grass and something faintly sweet you cannot name — it is because at every fork on that road, someone chose the slow option.

One plant. A thousand decisions. Matcha is simply the leaf that had every decision made in its favor.
