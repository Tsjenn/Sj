# Cookies and Small Bakes

If you have never baked with matcha before, start here, with something small. Cookies are the friendliest possible entrance to green baking, and not by accident. A batch of shortbread asks for four ingredients and half an hour. A tray of drop cookies goes from bowl to cooling rack before a cake would have finished mixing. When something goes slightly wrong — and the first time, something usually goes slightly wrong — you have lost a handful of butter and flour, not a layer cake and an afternoon. Better still, cookies give you feedback fast: within fifteen minutes you will know whether your oven runs hot, whether your matcha survived the heat, whether five grams of powder is your amount or you are a seven-gram person. Every lesson learned on a cookie sheet transfers directly to the cakes in the next chapter, at a fraction of the cost.

One rule from the last chapter governs everything that follows, so let it be this chapter's single commandment: **sift the matcha into the dry ingredients.** Not into the butter, not into the wet mix, never straight from the tin in a heap. Matcha clumps the moment it meets moisture, and a clump that forms in dough does not whisk out the way it does in water — it bakes in, and someone bites into a bitter green pocket. Whisk the powder through the flour with the salt and any leavening until the whole bowl is one even pale green, and the problem never gets the chance to exist. Every recipe below says "sift the matcha with the flour." That is not filler. That is the chapter.

The other thing to hold onto is color. Matcha's green is fragile in the oven — heat dulls it toward khaki, and the longer and hotter the bake, the browner the result. This turns out to be a gift in disguise, because the color reads doneness for you. A matcha cookie that is still vividly green is probably perfect; one gone olive at the edges has baked a minute too long. Where a plain cookie asks to be judged by golden-brown edges, a matcha cookie asks the opposite: pull it while it still looks slightly underdone and let residual heat finish the job on the tray. And as always in this book — ovens and brands vary. The times below are honest estimates from sound ratios, not guarantees. Watch the bake, not the clock.

### Matcha Shortbread

*Makes about 20 cookies. Twenty minutes of work, an hour of chilling, fifteen minutes of baking.*

This is the reference bake of the whole chapter — the recipe to make first, and the one to return to when you change matcha brands and want to know what you bought. Shortbread is the oldest ratio in the biscuit world: two parts butter, one part sugar, three parts flour, by weight. Nothing else — no egg, no leavening, no vanilla to hide behind. That bareness is exactly why it suits matcha: with only butter and sugar in the room, the tea is the loudest voice, and you taste precisely what your powder has to offer. The dose that works is 5 g of matcha per 150 g of flour — unmistakably matcha, without letting bitterness take over. Culinary grade is the right choice here, as the last chapter argued; save the ceremonial tin for the bowl.

The dough is shaped into a log, chilled, and sliced — which means you can keep a log in the freezer and bake six cookies on a random Tuesday.

**You need:**

- 150 g plain (all-purpose) flour (1¼ cups)
- 5 g culinary-grade matcha (about 2½ teaspoons)
- 100 g unsalted butter, soft but not melting (7 tablespoons)
- 50 g caster or granulated sugar (¼ cup)
- A pinch of fine salt

**Steps:**

1. Sift the flour, matcha, and salt into a bowl and whisk until one even pale green with no darker streaks. The commandment, in action.
2. In a second bowl, beat the soft butter and sugar until creamy — a minute by hand is enough. You are combining, not whipping; shortbread wants density, not air.
3. Add the dry mix and stir, then press with a spatula, until it forms a dough that just holds together. It will look impossibly crumbly for a while. Keep pressing; the butter will get there.
4. Tip the dough onto a sheet of baking paper and roll it into a log about 4 cm (1½ inches) across. Twist the ends like a sweet wrapper. Chill at least 1 hour, until firm.
5. Heat the oven to 160°C (320°F). Slice the log into rounds about 8 mm (⅓ inch) thick and lay them on a lined tray with a little space between — they barely spread.
6. Bake 12–15 minutes. The cookies are done when they are set at the edges and still fully green on top; the undersides should be barely blond, not brown. Cool on the tray for 5 minutes — they are fragile hot — then finish on a rack.

**The tricky part:** stopping the bake in time. Shortbread's usual cue — golden edges — is exactly the wrong target here: by the time a matcha shortbread turns golden, the green has died and a toasty note argues with the tea. Pull the tray when the tops still look a shade underbaked. They firm up completely as they cool, and the color you take out of the oven is the color you keep.

**One variation:** dip half of each cooled cookie in melted white chocolate and let it set on baking paper. The sweetness of white chocolate against matcha's gentle bitterness is one of the great pairings in this book, and it turns a plain log of shortbread into something you could box up and give away.

### White-Chocolate Matcha Cookies

*Makes about 16 cookies. Thirty minutes of work plus 30 minutes of chilling; 10–12 minutes per tray.*

If the shortbread is the reference, this is the crowd-pleaser — a soft-centered, chewy-edged drop cookie in the American style. It is the recipe most likely to convert someone who claims not to like matcha, because milk-sweet white chocolate does for the cookie what steamed milk does for a latte: it rounds off the tea's edges without erasing its character.

One honest word before the ingredient list: use a bar of white chocolate, chopped, rather than a bag of chips. Chips are made with stabilizers so they hold their shape in an oven — convenient for the manufacturer, less lovely for you. A chopped bar gives you irregular chunks and shards: the big pieces melt into soft pools, the fine shavings vanish into the dough and sweeten it from inside. The cookie is simply better, and the bar is often cheaper per gram than the chips.

**You need:**

- 180 g plain (all-purpose) flour (1½ cups)
- 6 g culinary-grade matcha (about 3 teaspoons)
- ½ teaspoon baking soda
- ¼ teaspoon fine salt
- 115 g unsalted butter, soft (½ cup / 1 stick)
- 100 g light brown sugar, packed (½ cup)
- 50 g granulated sugar (¼ cup)
- 1 large egg, room temperature
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
- 120 g white chocolate, chopped from a bar into rough chunks (about ¾ cup)

**Steps:**

1. Sift the flour, matcha, baking soda, and salt together and whisk until evenly green.
2. Beat the butter with both sugars until creamy and lighter in color, 2–3 minutes. The brown sugar is doing the chew; the white sugar is doing the spread.
3. Beat in the egg and vanilla until smooth.
4. Stir in the dry mixture until just combined — stop when the last streak of flour disappears. Overmixing builds gluten, and gluten makes tough cookies.
5. Fold in the white chocolate chunks, saving a small handful of the prettiest pieces.
6. Chill the dough 30 minutes. Meanwhile, heat the oven to 175°C (350°F) and line two trays.
7. Scoop balls of about 40 g (a heaped tablespoon) onto the trays, six per tray — they spread. Press the reserved chunks onto the tops; a cookie that shows its chocolate eats better than one that hides it.
8. Bake 10–12 minutes, one tray at a time, until the edges are set and the centers still look slightly soft and puffed. The tops should stay green. Cool on the tray 10 minutes before moving; the centers finish setting there.

**The tricky part:** believing the centers. A drop cookie pulled at the right moment looks a minute underdone — matte at the edges, faintly shiny and soft in the middle. Wait until the center looks fully baked in the oven and the cooled cookie will be dry, the green dulled. Pull early, wait on the tray, and be rewarded.

**One variation:** add 40 g (⅓ cup) of chopped roasted macadamias with the chocolate. Macadamia, white chocolate, and matcha make a rich, faintly tropical cookie — and the crunch keeps all that softness from becoming one-note.

### Matcha Crinkles

*Makes about 18 cookies. Twenty minutes of work, at least 2 hours of chilling; 10–11 minutes per tray.*

Crinkles are the theatrical ones — fudgy cookies rolled in icing sugar before baking, so that as they rise and spread, the white coat cracks open and the color shows through the fissures. The chocolate version is a classic; the matcha version is, frankly, more beautiful — snow-white sugar over deep green crevices, like moss breaking through frost. These are the cookies people photograph.

The texture target is a fudgy, brownie-like center under a crisp sugared shell, which is why the dough is wetter and softer than any other in this chapter — and why it must be chilled. The rest is not optional patience; it is structural.

**You need:**

- 180 g plain (all-purpose) flour (1½ cups)
- 8 g culinary-grade matcha (about 4 teaspoons)
- 1 teaspoon baking powder
- ¼ teaspoon fine salt
- 60 g unsalted butter, melted and cooled slightly (4 tablespoons)
- 150 g granulated sugar (¾ cup)
- 1 large egg plus 1 yolk, room temperature
- 2 tablespoons milk
- For rolling: 50 g granulated sugar (¼ cup) and 60 g icing (powdered) sugar (½ cup), in two separate small bowls

**Steps:**

1. Sift the flour, matcha, baking powder, and salt together; whisk until uniform.
2. In a larger bowl, whisk the melted butter and sugar together, then whisk in the egg, the yolk, and the milk until glossy.
3. Stir in the dry mixture until just combined. The dough will be soft and sticky — closer to a thick batter than a dough. That is correct.
4. Cover and chill for at least 2 hours, or overnight. The dough must be firm enough to roll into balls.
5. Heat the oven to 175°C (350°F) and line two trays. Roll the dough into balls of about 30 g (a level tablespoon, generously).
6. Roll each ball first in granulated sugar, then heavily — really heavily — in icing sugar, so it looks like a snowball. Place on the trays with room to spread.
7. Bake 10–11 minutes, until the cookies have puffed, spread, and cracked, and the surface between the cracks looks just set. The centers should still feel soft.
8. Cool on the tray for 10 minutes. The fudgy middle sets as it cools; moving them hot will break them.

**The tricky part:** the double roll, and the resting rule behind it. Skip the granulated layer and the icing sugar dissolves into the damp dough in the oven, leaving grey-green smears instead of white crackle — the granulated coat is a moisture barrier that keeps the snow white. Skip the chilling and you cannot roll the balls at all; worse, warm dough spreads before it puffs, so the cracks never form. Two hours in the fridge is the minimum; overnight is better, and the matcha flavor deepens as it sits.

**One variation:** press a square of white chocolate into the center of each ball before rolling in sugar. The baked cookie hides a molten heart, and the first person to discover it will assume you are a professional.

### Matcha Madeleines

*Makes 12. Twenty minutes of work, at least 1 hour of chilling; 10–11 minutes of baking. Requires a madeleine pan.*

Madeleines are the one recipe in this chapter that asks for a special pan, and they are worth it — little shell-shaped French sponge cakes with crisp scalloped backs, tender crumbs, and, when everything goes right, a proud rounded bump on top. Bakers call it the hump, and a whole mythology surrounds it. The truth is simpler: the hump comes from thermal shock. Cold, rested batter goes into a hot oven; the outside sets quickly while the cold center is still heating, and when the center's leavening finally fires, the only direction left is up. Chill the batter and the hump appears. Bake the batter warm and it will not, no matter what else you do. That is the whole secret, and it costs nothing but an hour.

Matcha suits the madeleine because the cake itself is gentle — eggs, sugar, butter — and because the browned-butter option below adds a nutty depth that flatters the tea the way toasted rice flatters green tea in genmaicha.

**You need:**

- 100 g unsalted butter (7 tablespoons), plus extra for the pan
- 2 large eggs, room temperature
- 90 g granulated sugar (a scant ½ cup)
- 1 tablespoon honey
- 100 g plain (all-purpose) flour (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon)
- 5 g culinary-grade matcha (about 2½ teaspoons)
- 1 teaspoon baking powder
- A pinch of fine salt

**Steps:**

1. Melt the butter — or, better, brown it: keep it over medium heat after it melts, swirling, until it smells nutty and the milk solids at the bottom turn amber, then pour it into a bowl to stop the cooking. Let it cool until warm, not hot.
2. Whisk the eggs, sugar, and honey together for 2–3 minutes, until pale and slightly thickened.
3. Sift the flour, matcha, baking powder, and salt together, then fold into the egg mixture until just smooth.
4. Fold in the cooled butter in two additions. The batter will be glossy and loose.
5. Cover and chill for at least 1 hour, or up to a day. This is non-negotiable — see above.
6. Heat the oven to 200°C (400°F). Butter the madeleine pan thoroughly, into every ridge, and dust with flour, tapping out the excess. Do this even if the pan is nonstick — madeleines are escape artists.
7. Spoon a scant tablespoon of cold batter into the center of each shell. Do not spread it — the oven will.
8. Bake 10–11 minutes, until the humps have risen and the edges are just beginning to turn gold while the tops stay green. Rap the pan on the counter and turn the madeleines out onto a rack immediately.

**The tricky part:** the temptation to skip the chill. If your kitchen is hot, chill the filled pan itself for 10 minutes before it goes in the oven — cold batter, hot oven, in that order, always. And eat madeleines young: they are at their best within an hour of baking, still faintly warm, and merely good the next day. This is not a flaw. It is an excuse.

**One variation:** once cooled, dip the scalloped side of each madeleine in melted dark chocolate and let it set. Dark chocolate, rather than white, is the right partner here — the browned butter and honey have already added sweetness, and the bitter edge of good dark chocolate meets the matcha as an equal.

### Matcha Sablés

*Makes about 22 cookies. Twenty minutes of work, at least 1 hour of chilling; 14–16 minutes of baking.*

A sablé is shortbread's French cousin, and the family resemblance is close — butter, sugar, flour, a log, a knife. The differences are two. First, an egg yolk: it makes the dough richer and easier to handle, and gives the baked cookie a finer, more tender crumb — *sablé* means "sanded," for the delicate way it breaks. Second, the finish: the log is rolled in coarse sugar before slicing, so every cookie bakes with a glittering, crunchy crust around its rim. If the shortbread earlier is a weekday cookie, this is what it wears to a dinner party.

Because the yolk softens matcha's grip a little, the same 5 g dose tastes slightly rounder here than in the shortbread — a good option for guests new to the flavor.

**You need:**

- 150 g plain (all-purpose) flour (1¼ cups)
- 5 g culinary-grade matcha (about 2½ teaspoons)
- ¼ teaspoon fine salt
- 120 g unsalted butter, soft (8½ tablespoons)
- 60 g icing (powdered) sugar (½ cup)
- 1 large egg yolk (keep the white)
- For the crust: about 3 tablespoons demerara or granulated sugar

**Steps:**

1. Sift the flour, matcha, and salt together; whisk until evenly green.
2. Beat the butter and icing sugar until smooth and creamy, then beat in the yolk.
3. Add the dry mix and bring together into a soft dough — stir, then press. Handle it as little as possible.
4. Roll into a log about 4 cm (1½ inches) across, wrap, and chill at least 1 hour, until firm.
5. Heat the oven to 160°C (320°F). Lightly beat the reserved egg white and brush it over the outside of the log, then roll the log in the coarse sugar until the surface is fully coated.
6. Slice into rounds 1 cm (⅜ inch) thick and arrange on a lined tray with a little space between.
7. Bake 14–16 minutes, until set through and still green on top, with only the faintest gold at the very base. Cool on the tray 5 minutes, then on a rack.

**The tricky part:** slicing without shattering the sugar crust. A log that is rock-hard from a long chill cracks when the knife goes through, and the sugar coat flakes away. Let a very cold log sit at room temperature for 10 minutes, use your sharpest thin knife, and cut with a gentle sawing motion rather than pressing straight down. If a slice breaks anyway, press it back together on the tray — the oven forgives.

**One variation:** sandwich two cooled sablés around a little sweetened azuki red-bean paste. Matcha and azuki is the oldest flavor partnership in Japanese confectionery — you will meet it again in the layer cake next chapter — and the sandy cookie against the smooth paste is a very good preview.

### Matcha Biscotti

*Makes about 24. Fifteen minutes of work; about 50 minutes of baking in two stages.*

Biscotti are the marathon runners of this chapter: the only cookie here that improves your tea for weeks rather than days. The name means "twice-cooked," and the logic of the double bake explains the whole method. The first bake cooks a soft log of dough through, like a flat loaf of bread. Then the log is sliced, and the slices return to a gentler oven — not to cook further, but to surrender their moisture. What comes out is hard, light, and nearly imperishable: in a sealed jar, biscotti stay good for two or three weeks, which no butter-soft cookie in this chapter can claim.

That hardness is a design, not a flaw. Biscotti are built to be dunked, and matcha biscotti are unusually versatile dunkers — excellent in coffee, where the tea and the roast meet surprisingly well, and just as good in a plain bowl of usucha or a hot hōjicha, where green meets green. Whole almonds are the classic partner and the right one here: their toasted sweetness sits in the same register as matcha's vegetal side, and the cross-sections of almond in each slice are half the beauty of the finished cookie.

**You need:**

- 250 g plain (all-purpose) flour (2 cups plus 1 tablespoon)
- 8 g culinary-grade matcha (about 4 teaspoons)
- 1½ teaspoons baking powder
- ¼ teaspoon fine salt
- 150 g granulated sugar (¾ cup)
- 2 large eggs plus 1 yolk, room temperature
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
- 100 g whole raw almonds, skins on (¾ cup)

**Steps:**

1. Heat the oven to 165°C (330°F) and line a tray. Sift the flour, matcha, baking powder, and salt together; whisk in the sugar.
2. In a small bowl, beat the eggs, yolk, and vanilla. Pour into the dry mix and stir to a stiff, slightly sticky dough. It will seem too dry at first; keep folding.
3. Knead in the almonds until evenly distributed.
4. With damp hands, shape the dough into two logs on the tray, each about 22 cm long and 6 cm wide (9 by 2½ inches), and flatten slightly. Leave space between — they spread a little.
5. Bake 25–30 minutes, until the logs are set, risen, and dry to the touch, with hairline cracks on top. They should still be green; if they are browning, your oven runs hot — note it for next time.
6. Cool the logs 10 minutes — no longer, no less. Then slice on a slight diagonal into pieces 1 cm (⅜ inch) thick with a serrated knife, using a confident sawing stroke.
7. Lay the slices flat on the tray, cut side up. Lower the oven to 150°C (300°F) and bake 15–20 minutes, turning every slice over halfway, until the surfaces feel dry. They crisp fully as they cool, so judge by touch, not snap.
8. Cool completely before storing in an airtight jar.

**The tricky part:** the ten-minute rest between bakes. Slice too soon and the hot log crumbles around the almonds; wait too long and it hardens enough to shatter under the knife. Ten minutes is the window where the log is cool enough to hold and soft enough to cut. Set a timer, and use a genuinely serrated knife — a straight blade crushes where serrations saw.

**One variation:** for a softer, café-style biscotto, cut thicker slices — 2 cm (¾ inch) — and shorten the second bake to 10 minutes. The center stays slightly tender, which suits eating them plain rather than dunking. Purists will object. Purists are not eating your biscotti.

## Where the small bakes lead

Six recipes, one commandment, one running lesson: in matcha baking, green is information. Every recipe here asked you to pull the tray while the color still looked alive, and rewarded you for it — the same gentle heat that preserves the green preserves the tenderness, the fudge, the hump, the tea itself. Bake any two of these and you will have calibrated your oven, your matcha, and your eye.

Which is exactly the equipment you need next. Cakes are cookies with higher stakes — the same sift-into-dry rule, the same fragile green, the same watch-don't-trust-the-clock honesty, scaled up and given a crumb worth slicing. You have done the apprenticeship. Turn the page.
