# Who Should Go Gently

A friend of mine — I will call her Mei, because she would be mortified otherwise — fell for matcha the way people fall for it now: one good latte, then a tin of her own, then a bamboo whisk, then two bowls a day and a genuine little ritual she looked forward to. She did everything this book teaches. She bought well, stored well, whisked well.

And she started lying awake at two in the morning, staring at the ceiling, wondering what was wrong with her life.

Nothing was wrong with her life. What was wrong was that her second bowl happened at four in the afternoon, and Mei, as she eventually worked out, is one of those people whose body holds on to caffeine the way a cast-iron pan holds heat. Her afternoon bowl was still glowing at midnight. She did not need to quit matcha. She needed to move it to the morning and make it a little smaller — which she did, and the ceiling-staring stopped, and she still drinks matcha almost every day, happily, a decade's worth of bowls later.

I tell you about Mei because this chapter is the one where I stop being matcha's advocate for a few pages and become yours. Everything in the bowl that we have spent Part Three admiring — the caffeine, the tannic catechins, the sheer concentration of drinking a whole ground leaf — is exactly why a few groups of people should approach it thoughtfully rather than enthusiastically. None of what follows is alarming. Most of it amounts to *smaller, earlier, or ask someone qualified.* But an honest book owes you this chapter, and it owes it to you plainly, so here it is.

One promise before we start, and it is the same promise as always: I am a tea writer, not a clinician. Where a question crosses into medicine, I will not pretend to answer it. I will tell you who can.

## If caffeine hits you hard

Caffeine is not experienced equally. Some people can drink an espresso after dinner and sleep like a stone; others feel a midday cup at bedtime. This is not weakness or imagination — people genuinely differ, sometimes dramatically, in how quickly their bodies clear caffeine. Slow metabolizers get a longer, later tail from the same dose. If you have ever suspected you are one of them, you probably are.

Matcha complicates the picture in a specific way. As we saw in the caffeine chapter, many people find its alertness smoother than coffee's — the L-theanine riding alongside seems, for many drinkers, to soften the edges. Smoother going up, though, does not mean shorter overall. The caffeine in a bowl of matcha is real caffeine, in meaningful amounts, and it does not care how serene you felt while drinking it. A gentle arrival can even be a trap: because matcha rarely gives sensitive people the jittery, heart-fluttery warning that coffee does, it is easy to drink more of it, later in the day, than you ever would espresso.

So learn your own signs. For caffeine-sensitive people they are usually some mix of: trouble falling asleep or shallow, restless sleep; a racing or fluttering feeling in the chest; anxiety with no obvious cause; irritability; an urgent acquaintance with the bathroom. If any of that shows up on your matcha days, you have three easy levers to pull before you even think about giving it up.

- **Smaller.** Half a teaspoon instead of a full one makes a genuinely lighter bowl, and a well-made half-measure usucha is still a lovely drink. There is a reason Part Five includes an entire chapter of evening bowls built exactly this way.
- **Earlier.** Caffeine lingers for many hours in everyone, and longest in the sensitive. Making matcha a morning ritual — finished by noon, say — is the single most effective change, as Mei can testify.
- **Softer leaf.** Not every shaded tea is matcha-strength. Kabusecha (冠茶), the briefly-shaded sister leaf we met in Part Two, is brewed as an infusion rather than drunk whole, so you get some of that shaded sweetness with a gentler dose. Hōjicha (焙じ茶), the roasted tea, is gentler still, and its toasty warmth makes it the traditional Japanese answer to "something comforting, but it's evening." Blending matcha half-and-half with either, as some of the recipes ahead do, is a perfectly honorable dodge.

What I will not tell you is that anyone "shouldn't" drink matcha because they are sensitive. That is your call, made with your own ceiling at 2 a.m. as the witness. The tradition, notice, is on your side here: the classical bowl was never a bottomless mug. It was small, and it was usually finished by afternoon.

## Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Here is the shortest and most important section in this book, and I am going to resist every temptation to pad it.

If you are pregnant, trying to be, or breastfeeding, the thing you need to know about matcha is simply this: **it contains meaningful caffeine — a proper bowl sits in the same general neighborhood as a cup of coffee, not a cup of weak tea.** Because you drink the whole leaf, you cannot brew your way around this; the caffeine is in the powder, and the powder is in you.

How much caffeine is appropriate during pregnancy or breastfeeding is a genuinely medical question. It depends on guidance that gets reviewed and updated, on your own health, and on circumstances only your doctor or midwife knows. You will find numbers on the internet presented with great confidence, and you may notice this book declining to join them. That is deliberate. A tea book quoting caffeine limits at a pregnant reader is exactly the kind of confident-sounding, secondhand advice this book exists to refuse.

So the whole of my advice is: bring it up at an appointment. Tell your doctor or midwife that you drink matcha, mention roughly how much and how often, and let them give you a number that is actually theirs to give. Most people I know who have had this conversation came away with something workable — often an adjustment rather than a farewell — but the conversation is the point. It costs you one minute of an appointment you were having anyway.

And if the answer for a while is "not now," matcha is patient. It has waited eight hundred years; it will wait nine more months.

## Matcha and iron

This one surprises almost everyone, and it is worth knowing even if you are perfectly healthy.

Tea — all real tea, matcha very much included — is rich in polyphenols, the family of plant compounds that includes the catechins we met two chapters ago. Polyphenols are most of why tea tastes like tea. But they have a well-established quirk: when they are in your stomach at the same time as plant-source iron — the kind in beans, lentils, tofu, leafy greens, and fortified grains — they bind to some of it and can meaningfully reduce how much your body absorbs from that meal. This is old, boring, uncontroversial nutrition knowledge, not a matcha scare. It applies to a mug of ordinary black tea too. But since matcha is concentrated leaf, it earns the reminder.

For most well-nourished people this is a shrug. Bodies regulate iron uptake over weeks, not single lunches, and no one balanced falls into deficiency because of a beverage. But if iron is a live issue for you — you have been told you are anemic or borderline, you are vegetarian or vegan and watching your levels, you are pregnant (iron again — see above), or you are a heavy period-haver who runs low — then timing is a free and painless fix:

- **Drink matcha between meals rather than with them.** Give it some comfortable distance — an hour or so on either side of your iron-important meals is the usual sensible habit — and you have removed most of the interaction without giving up a single bowl.
- **Keep it away from the iron pill specifically.** If you take an iron supplement, do not wash it down with tea of any kind. Water is the escort it wants.
- **If you are actually managing anemia, ask your clinician** how they want you to time tea around your meals and supplements. This is precisely the kind of small, practical question they answer all day, and their answer for you beats any general rule I can print.

Notice what this section is not saying. It is not saying matcha "blocks iron" or "causes anemia" — sentences you will meet online in both fearmongering and dismissive forms. The honest version is smaller: a real, well-established interaction that timing almost entirely defuses, and that matters mainly to people already managing their iron.

## Children and the small bowl

Children ask for matcha now, because matcha is green and pretty and their parents drink it, and a matcha soft-serve is objectively one of the great arguments for being alive. So what is the honest position?

Simply this: children are small, and caffeine dose is a matter of body size. A bowl that is a moderate dose for you is, for a seven-year-old, a large one. Add that children's sleep is both precious and easily disrupted, and there is no world in which a full-strength daily bowl makes sense for a small child. This is not controversial anywhere on earth.

The culture that has lived with matcha longest handles this with characteristic good sense, and it is worth copying. Japanese children mostly meet matcha not as a drink but as a flavor — a matcha ice cream, a small wagashi-adjacent sweet, a mild matcha milk where a little powder is stretched across a lot of milk and the dose per serving is small. The full bowl comes later, often first encountered in a school tea-ceremony club as something you are taught to receive with a bow, in a small quantity, on an occasion. Matcha as a treat and a ceremony first; matcha as a habit only when the body drinking it has finished growing into itself.

That is the model I would hand any parent: let matcha be an occasional flavor in childhood, keep servings small and early in the day, and save the true daily bowl for later. If your child has any health condition that makes caffeine a live question, that is — you can finish the sentence by now — one for the pediatrician.

## A word about medicines

This section is one honest paragraph, because one is all I can responsibly write.

Tea compounds — caffeine first among them, but not only caffeine — can interact with some medications: sometimes changing how a drug is absorbed, sometimes amplifying a stimulant effect, sometimes mattering in ways that are specific to one drug and irrelevant to a hundred others. Which medications, and whether yours is among them, is not something a tea book can tell you, and you should be suspicious of any that tries. What I can tell you is who answers this question quickly and well: a pharmacist. Interactions are their home ground, the consultation is usually free, and "I drink a bowl or two of matcha most days — does that matter with this?" is a question they will take seriously and answer in under two minutes. If you take regular medication of any kind, ask it once. Then you will know, which beats wondering.

## The cheap-tin caveat, one more time

We spent part of the last two chapters on this, so here I will only close the loop: everything in this chapter assumes you are drinking decent matcha, because with matcha you consume the entire leaf, and so you also consume the entire leaf's history — its soil, its handling, its storage. That is why the diligence we discussed in the antioxidants chapter matters more here than with steeped tea, where the leaves stay in the pot. Very cheap powder of no stated origin, sold with no story and no accountability, is the version of matcha I would actually worry about — not because of any single scary headline, but because you cannot audit what you cannot trace. Buy from sellers who tell you where the leaf grew and when it was milled, favor origins with strong testing cultures, and treat a price that seems impossible as information. The groups in this chapter — the pregnant, the small, the medicated, the anemic — have the least slack for sloppy sourcing, and the good news is that the fix is the same buying checklist you already learned in Part Two.

## More bowls are not more benefit

And now the caution that is really a philosophy, which is why I saved it to close Part Three.

Somewhere in the modern matcha boom, an idea took hold that if one bowl is good, four must be better — that matcha is a wellness input to be maximized, scaled, optimized. You can see how it happened; that is how we treat everything now. But it runs exactly backwards to both the evidence and the tradition. Nothing we covered in these chapters — not the calm caffeine, not the catechins, not the quiet pleasure of the ritual — works on the logic of *more is more*. Past a moderate daily amount, additional bowls mostly add caffeine load, tannin load, and cost. The benefits, such as they honestly are, live in the modest range where matcha has actually been drunk for centuries; what lives in the immoderate range is Mei's ceiling at 2 a.m.

The tradition figured this out long before anyone could assay a catechin. Consider how matcha was actually consumed for most of its history: not by the vat, not on a drip through the day, but as a small bowl, taken occasionally, prepared with attention and finished in a few sips. Chanoyu built four hundred years of practice around the idea that the power of the bowl came precisely from its smallness and its rarity — that you honored the leaf by giving it your full attention for a few minutes, not by making it constant background fuel. A thing that is always in your hand is soon not really tasted at all.

So the closing advice of the science part of this book turns out to be the same as the opening wisdom of the tea masters, and I find that genuinely satisfying: drink matcha in small, attentive, unhurried doses. If you are sensitive, make them smaller and earlier. If you are pregnant or medicated or managing your iron or pouring for a child, spend one minute asking the professional whose job it is to know. And whoever you are — drink it because it is delicious and because the pause does you good, not because a headline promised transformation by the fourth bowl.

That is everything the quiet chemistry has to say. What happens next to this leaf has less to do with chemistry and everything to do with commerce — because while we have been peering into the bowl, the world has been building a very large business on top of it. Part Four follows the money.
