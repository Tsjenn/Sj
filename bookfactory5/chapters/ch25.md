# Iced and Cold-Whisked

Somewhere in the last chapter, in the small print of a variation, a secret slipped out: whisk matcha into cold water and the bitterness softens. This is not a trick of perception. The compounds that make matcha taste harsh — the catechins and the caffeine — dissolve eagerly in hot water and reluctantly in cold, while the sweeter, rounder side of the leaf comes through at any temperature. Chill the water and you quiet the sharpest voice in the room without asking anyone else to leave. The practical consequence is wonderful and slightly unfair: cold drinks are the most forgiving place in this entire book. An everyday, mid-priced matcha that tastes a little rough as a hot bowl can taste genuinely lovely over ice. If you are still building confidence, or still using up a tin you have doubts about, start here.

Cold asks one thing of you in return. Remember the clump warning from Chapter 23 — powder does not dissolve, it suspends, and it suspends only when convinced. Cold water is roughly twice as stubborn as hot. Sprinkle matcha straight onto a glass of cold liquid and it will sit on the surface like green pollen, then sink into little bitter dumplings that no amount of stirring will fix. So every recipe in this chapter begins the same way: either make a small paste first (a splash of water, a firm stir, exactly like starting koicha), or put powder and water in a sealed jar and shake it like you mean it. Paste or shake. Never sprinkle. If you hold onto one sentence from this page, hold onto that one.

Everything else is assembly. Six drinks follow, from a thirty-second jar of green lightning to a lemonade that puts on a show. None of them needs a single piece of equipment you do not already own.

### Iced shaken matcha

*Serves one. Thirty seconds of shaking, maybe two minutes all told. This is the reference recipe for the whole chapter.*

This is the fastest good matcha there is, and I mean that without qualification. No kettle, no waiting for water to cool, no whisk to rinse afterward. A jam jar with a tight lid does everything the bamboo whisk does — the ice cubes act as tiny whisks themselves, knocking the powder apart as they tumble. The result is cold, foamy, vividly green, and ready before your toast is. On a Malaysian afternoon, or any afternoon that behaves like one, this is the recipe I actually make.

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 150 ml cold water (2/3 cup)
- 4–5 ice cubes, plus more for the glass
- A jam jar or cocktail shaker with a lid that seals

**Steps:**

1. Sift the matcha straight into the dry jar. Sifting matters even more here than for a hot bowl — cold water will not rescue a lump for you.
2. Add the cold water, then the ice cubes. Screw the lid on properly. Check it. Green splatter is very hard to explain.
3. Shake hard for 20–30 seconds. Not a polite wobble — a real shake, the kind a bartender would recognize. You should hear the ice battering around.
4. Fill a glass with fresh ice and pour everything in, foam and all. The foam from a shaken matcha is looser and frothier than whisked foam, almost like the head on a soda.
5. Drink it while it is lively. Like every matcha, it settles; unlike a hot bowl, you can simply put the lid back on and shake again.

**The tricky part:** committing to the shake. Twenty seconds feels long when you are standing in a kitchen shaking a jar, and almost everyone stops at eight. Count, or play a song, or watch the clock — under-shaken matcha tastes dusty and looks flat, and the fix costs you twelve more seconds.

**One variation:** shake in a strip of lime peel or two mint leaves with the ice. They bruise as they tumble and leave a cool edge on the drink without turning it into a cocktail. (If you do want it to become a cocktail, Chapter 26 is patient and will wait for you.)

### Iced latte with cold foam

*Serves one. About five minutes. The cafe drink, at home, for a fraction of the price.*

The iced matcha latte is the drink that carried this leaf around the world, and the version with a cold foam cap is the one people photograph: a white cloud sitting on green sitting on white, three layers in one glass. Cafes make the foam with steam wands and specialized pitchers. You need neither — cold milk froths into a dense, pourable foam with the same cheap electric frother you may already be using as a whisk substitute, or with sixty seconds of determined shaking in another jar. The foam is not decoration, either: it softens each sip before the matcha reaches you, which is why this drink converts people who claim they do not like matcha.

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 30 ml cold water (2 tablespoons), for the paste
- 180 ml cold milk (3/4 cup) — dairy or oat froth best
- 50 ml additional cold milk (a scant 1/4 cup), for the foam
- 1–2 teaspoons sugar or honey, optional, to taste
- Ice to fill the glass

**Steps:**

1. Sift the matcha into a small bowl or cup. Add the cold water and stir firmly with a spoon or small whisk until you have a smooth, glossy, lump-free paste — the koicha kneading motion from Chapter 23, just colder. If you are sweetening, stir the sugar into the paste now; it dissolves here far better than it ever will in a cold glass.
2. Fill your serving glass with ice, then pour in the 180 ml of milk, leaving a couple of centimeters of headroom.
3. Froth the 50 ml of milk cold: frother for 30–40 seconds, or shake it in a sealed jar for a minute until it thickens and roughly doubles. Cold foam is denser and slower than hot foam — that is correct.
4. Pour the matcha paste gently over the milk. It will drift down in green ribbons; give it one lazy stir if you like, or leave the layers for the first sip to mix.
5. Spoon the cold foam on top and serve with a straw or a long spoon.

**The tricky part:** the paste. Every failed iced latte I have ever been served — cafes included — failed at this step: powder met cold liquid without an introduction, and the drink arrived with a gritty green sediment line. Take the full thirty seconds. The paste should shine and flow like thin honey before any milk gets involved. Milks froth differently brand to brand, too, especially plant milks — taste and adjust rather than trusting any label.

**One variation:** a salted cold foam. Add a single small pinch of fine salt to the foam milk before frothing. It sounds wrong and tastes right — the salt sharpens the milk's sweetness and makes the matcha underneath taste rounder. This is the same logic as salted caramel, borrowed for green.

### Coconut iced matcha

*Serves one. About five minutes. Matcha goes tropical, and it turns out to be a natural fit.*

Coconut and matcha sound like a fusion experiment and taste like old friends. Both are a little sweet, a little savory, and both carry a faint green-vegetal note — pandan drinkers will recognize the family resemblance immediately. The trick is using two coconuts at once: coconut water for lightness and a clean, faintly sweet base, and coconut milk for the rich, santan silkiness that plays the role dairy usually does. All coconut milk and the drink turns heavy; all coconut water and it turns thin. The blend is the recipe.

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 30 ml cold water (2 tablespoons), for the paste
- 100 ml chilled coconut water (a scant 1/2 cup)
- 100 ml chilled coconut milk (a scant 1/2 cup) — the drinking kind, or canned shaken well and thinned with a splash of water
- 1–2 teaspoons palm sugar syrup or plain sugar syrup, optional
- Ice to fill the glass

**Steps:**

1. Make the paste: sift the matcha into a cup, add the cold water, and stir until completely smooth and glossy.
2. Stir the coconut water into the paste — because it is thin, it loosens the paste evenly and without fuss.
3. Fill a glass with ice and pour in the coconut milk.
4. Pour the green coconut-water mixture over the top and watch it fold into the white in slow marbled swirls. Stir before drinking, or don't, depending on how long you want to look at it.
5. Taste, then add syrup only if the drink needs it — coconut water varies a lot in sweetness, and some days it carries the whole drink unassisted.

**The tricky part:** the coconut milk itself. Canned coconut milk separates into thick cream and thin liquid, and a spoon of unmixed cream turns into waxy white flecks in a cold drink. Shake or stir the can until uniform before measuring, and if it is still very thick, thin it with a little water. Brands vary enormously here — taste and adjust is not a disclaimer in this recipe, it is step zero.

**One variation:** make it a float. Use slightly less ice, and add a small scoop of coconut ice cream on top at the end. The drink slides from afternoon refreshment to dessert in one move, and the melting ice cream becomes its own slow-motion cold foam.

### Cold-brew style overnight matcha

*Serves one, tomorrow. Two minutes tonight, zero minutes in the morning.*

Coffee people discovered years ago that time can replace heat: steep grounds in cold water overnight and you get something smoother and rounder than any hot brew. The same principle applies to matcha, with one honest difference. Coffee cold brew is an infusion — you strain the grounds out. Matcha is a suspension, and no amount of patience changes that; the powder you shake in tonight will be sitting quietly at the bottom of the bottle by morning. That is not a failure. It is physics, and the fix is the same jar lid you started with. What the long, cold night actually does is give the water time to draw out the leaf's gentler flavors while the harsher ones stay half-asleep. The result is the mellowest matcha in this book — soft, sweet-edged, almost brothy — and it is waiting in the fridge before you have had a single thought about breakfast.

**You need:**

- 3 g matcha (about 1 heaped teaspoon)
- 300 ml cold water (1 1/4 cups)
- A bottle or jar with a tight lid, roughly 500 ml, so there is room to shake

**Steps:**

1. The night before: sift the matcha into the dry bottle, add the cold water, seal, and shake hard for 20 seconds, exactly as for the iced shaken matcha.
2. Refrigerate overnight, or at least six hours.
3. In the morning the liquid will look pale with a dense green layer settled on the bottom. This is normal and will happen every single time. Shake again for ten seconds to wake it up.
4. Pour over ice, or drink it cool straight from the fridge. Finish within a day — the flavor is at its best the first morning.

**The tricky part:** trusting it. The settled bottle looks like something went wrong, and more than one person has poured a perfectly good overnight matcha down the sink because of that pale top layer. Nothing went wrong. Suspensions settle; that is what the word means. Shake, pour, enjoy, and if a fine green film clings to the bottle afterward, that is your souvenir.

**One variation:** overnight matcha made with cold jasmine or barley tea instead of plain water. Brew the tea, chill it completely first — warm liquid defeats the whole point — then shake the matcha into it and refrigerate as usual. Barley tea in particular gives a toasty depth that tastes far more considered than two minutes of work deserves.

### Matcha lemonade

*Serves one. About five minutes. The color show.*

Made in the right order, this drink is briefly the most beautiful thing in your kitchen: a layer of cloudy lemon at the bottom, ice glittering through the middle, and a slow green storm front descending from above. The show lasts about a minute before you stir it into a uniform, luminous chartreuse — enjoy both acts. It tastes the way it looks, too. Lemon and matcha share a certain briskness, and the sugar is not optional here; it is the diplomat that makes the two of them get along. Serve this to somebody at the hottest hour of the day and watch their opinion of green tea reorganize itself.

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 30 ml cold water (2 tablespoons), for the paste
- 30 ml fresh lemon juice (2 tablespoons — about one juicy lemon)
- 20–30 ml simple syrup (4–6 teaspoons), to taste
- 180 ml cold soda water or still water (3/4 cup)
- Ice to fill the glass; a lemon slice if you are feeling generous

**Steps:**

1. If you need simple syrup: stir equal spoonfuls of sugar and hot water until dissolved, then cool. It keeps in the fridge for a couple of weeks, and half this book's cold drinks will find uses for it.
2. Make the matcha paste: sift, add the 30 ml cold water, stir until glossy and smooth.
3. In your serving glass, stir together the lemon juice and syrup. Fill the glass with ice.
4. Pour in the soda water slowly, down the side of the glass, leaving room at the top.
5. Now the show: pour the matcha paste gently over the back of a spoon held just above the surface. It will hover, then descend in green curtains. Present the glass, allow the audience its moment, then stir well and add the lemon slice.

**The tricky part:** the order of operations. Matcha added before the lemon, or stirred in too early, gives you a uniformly green drink that tastes identical but skips the theater. Worse, matcha pasted directly into neat lemon juice can turn drab and khaki — acid is hard on that beautiful chlorophyll. Keep the green apart until the last moment. Lemons vary wildly in sharpness, so taste the lemon-syrup base before the matcha goes anywhere near it: it should taste like slightly too-strong lemonade on its own.

**One variation:** frozen matcha lemonade. Put the lemon juice, syrup, still water (skip the soda), and a cup of ice in a blender with the matcha paste and blitz to a slush. The layers are lost, the refreshment is doubled, and children who would never touch a bowl of usucha will ask for this by name.

### Yuzu-matcha cooler

*Serves one. About five minutes. The elegant one.*

Yuzu is the citrus Japan reaches for when lemon would be too blunt — floral, fragrant, somewhere in the aromatic triangle between mandarin, grapefruit, and lime blossom. With matcha it forms one of the great pairings of the modern Japanese cafe: the same green-meets-citrus logic as the lemonade, but rounder, perfumed, and a little more grown-up. Fresh yuzu is rare and expensive outside Japan and Korea, but you do not need it. A jar of yuzu marmalade — yuja-cha in Korean shops, sold as a tea base, peel and all — sits happily in the fridge for months and gives you the aroma, the sweetness, and little amber ribbons of peel in one spoonful.

**You need:**

- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 30 ml cold water (2 tablespoons), for the paste
- 1 generous tablespoon yuzu marmalade (or 15 ml bottled yuzu juice plus 2 teaspoons honey)
- 180 ml cold water or soda water (3/4 cup)
- Ice to fill the glass

**Steps:**

1. Make the matcha paste: sift, add the 30 ml cold water, stir smooth.
2. In the serving glass, stir the yuzu marmalade with a small splash of the measured water until it loosens from a jammy clump into a syrup with peel drifting through it.
3. Fill the glass with ice and top with the rest of the cold water or soda, stirring once so the yuzu is evenly distributed.
4. Pour the matcha paste over the top, admire the green-over-gold for a moment, then stir well.
5. Sip, and fish out a ribbon of candied peel with the last mouthful — cook's privilege.

**The tricky part:** dosage. Yuzu marmalades range from delicately fragrant to aggressively sweet, and bottled yuzu juice is pure aromatic acid with no sweetness at all. Build the yuzu base first and taste it before the matcha arrives: it should taste bright and a touch too intense on its own, because the matcha and the melting ice will both soften it. Adjust with honey or a squeeze of lime until the base earns your approval — the green half of the drink is the consistent one; the citrus half is where brands vary.

**One variation:** and here the book gets to come home — calamansi works beautifully. If you live where limau kasturi falls off backyard trees, squeeze four or five of the little fruits (about 20 ml), sweeten with a teaspoon or two of honey or gula melaka syrup, and proceed exactly as above. Calamansi has the same floral-citrus quality that makes yuzu special, at pocket change per glass, and a matcha-calamansi cooler is — as far as I am concerned — a Malaysian cafe drink waiting for its moment.

## Where the bubbles come in

Six drinks, one lesson, endlessly repeated: respect the paste, or shake like you mean it, and cold matcha will forgive nearly everything else. You now hold the two motions — the sixty-second citrus base, the glossy green paste poured over ice — that the entire chilled half of this book is built from.

Which leaves one ingredient this chapter kept politely at arm's length, allowing it in only as an understudy: carbonation. Fizz changes the rules. Bubbles carry aroma to your nose before the glass reaches your mouth, sharpen every flavor they touch, and collapse in protest if you stir them the way these recipes stirred. Matcha with tonic, matcha with ginger, matcha over cucumber and mint and rising beads of silver — that is its own small discipline, and it is where we go next.
