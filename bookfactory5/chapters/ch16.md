# Matcha or Coffee? An Honest Comparison

Somewhere on the internet right now, two articles are being published. One is titled something like "Why I Quit Coffee for Matcha and Never Looked Back." The other is a coffee lover explaining, with equal conviction, that matcha is overpriced grass water for people who photograph their breakfast. Both authors are enjoying themselves. Neither is being especially useful to you.

Here is what this chapter is instead: the comparison I wish someone had written for me — one that assumes you might genuinely like both drinks, treats coffee with respect (it has earned it; humanity is not wrong about coffee), and tells you honestly where matcha wins, where coffee wins, and where the difference is simply a matter of who you are and what your morning is for.

A declaration of interest first, since this is a book about matcha written by someone who drinks it daily: of course I lean green. But I have no wish to convert you by exaggeration. Matcha's honest advantages are enough; it does not need coffee to lose.

## The energy question

This is the comparison everyone actually wants, so let us take it first and take it slowly.

Both drinks work for the same fundamental reason: caffeine. Nobody should pretend otherwise. A bowl of matcha is not caffeine-free, and its alertness is not mystical — it comes substantially from the same molecule that powers your espresso. The differences are in dose, company, and pacing.

**Dose.** A standard bowl of usucha, made with about two grams of powder, generally delivers a caffeine dose in the broad neighborhood of a cup of tea to a modest cup of coffee — commonly cited somewhere in the tens of milligrams. A typical mug of brewed coffee usually lands higher, often meaningfully higher. Both vary enormously with preparation: a double-shot flat white and a thimble of weak filter are different animals, and a thick koicha made with double the powder doubles matcha's dose too. As a rough everyday rule: bowl for cup, coffee usually hits harder.

**Company.** Here is matcha's genuinely distinctive feature. Because you consume the whole shaded leaf, the caffeine arrives alongside L-theanine, the amino acid that shading preserved so carefully back in the farm chapters. L-theanine is widely reported to promote a relaxed kind of alertness, and the caffeine-plus-theanine combination is the leading explanation for the experience matcha drinkers describe over and over: awake, but not wired. Focused, but not jangly. I want to be honest about the state of knowledge here — this combination effect has real research interest and a plausible mechanism, but the marketing has sprinted far ahead of the certainty, and individual experience varies more than any headline admits. Some people feel an unmistakable difference. Some feel none. The only experiment that matters is the one you run on yourself.

**Pacing.** Many drinkers describe coffee as a spike — a sharp lift half an hour in, a peak, and for some people a mid-afternoon trench where the energy used to be. Matcha drinkers more often describe a plateau: a gentler climb, a longer level stretch, a softer landing. Some of this may be dose (less caffeine, smaller spike). Some may be the theanine. Some may be the whole-leaf matrix slowing absorption — a tidy theory that remains more plausible than proven. And some of it, honestly, may be ritual and expectation, which are real forces and nothing to be ashamed of. Whatever the mechanism, the pattern is reported too widely and too consistently to dismiss: if coffee's arc gives you trouble — the racing heart, the 3 p.m. crash, the second cup that fixes nothing — matcha's flatter curve is the single best reason to try it.

And if coffee's arc gives you no trouble at all? Then you have no problem here to solve, and nobody should talk you into one.

## The stomach question

Coffee is acidic, and it stimulates the stomach in ways some digestive systems greet with enthusiasm and others protest for hours. If you love coffee but your stomach files complaints — the burn, the churn, the empty-stomach regret — you already know this, and no brewing trick has fully fixed it.

Matcha is gentler on most stomachs. It is less acidic, and a bowl of usucha is mostly water with two grams of leaf in suspension. This is not a universal law — caffeine itself affects the gut, and a strong koicha on an empty stomach can still make itself known — but as a broad pattern, people who abandoned coffee for digestive reasons often find matcha entirely comfortable. If that is you, this section alone may settle the question.

Point to matcha — for the people who need the point. For everyone else, a tie you never think about.

## The speed question

Now a point in coffee's favor, and it is a big one: coffee has spent a century optimizing for convenience, and it shows.

Coffee meets you wherever you are. A capsule machine delivers it in forty seconds. Every street on earth sells it. Instant coffee — no insult intended; modern instant is respectable — dissolves in anything hot. The entire infrastructure of the working world is built around getting coffee into your hand with zero skill and near-zero time.

Matcha asks more. Made properly, even the quick everyday way, it wants a sift, water at the right temperature, and fifteen seconds of committed whisking — call it two or three minutes of attention, plus tools you had to buy on purpose, plus powder that (as the storage chapter explained) is quietly dying in the tin and cannot be stockpiled like beans. There are shortcuts — shaker bottles, electric frothers, cafe lattes — but the honest statement stands: coffee is easier, faster, and more available, everywhere, every time.

Whether that settles anything depends on what you think the two or three minutes are. If they are a cost, coffee wins them. But this book has been arguing since chapter one that matcha's small ceremony is not overhead — it is the product. The ninety seconds of full attention are the part of the drink you cannot buy pre-made. Coffee at its best offers this too — anyone who hand-grinds beans for a pour-over knows — but coffee lets you skip it, and mostly you will. Matcha barely gives you the option. Some mornings that is matcha's greatest flaw. Other mornings it is the entire point.

## The money question

Sticker prices lie in both directions here, so let us reason it through honestly.

Good matcha looks alarming per tin. But a tin is fifteen to twenty bowls, and even genuinely fine drinking matcha usually works out, per bowl made at home, to less than a cafe coffee — often much less. Everyday-grade matcha for lattes costs less still. Coffee at home spans everything from very cheap (supermarket ground) to matcha-level and beyond (specialty beans, proper equipment). Cafe prices for the two drinks are, these days, nearly identical — green lattes and brown ones sit a ringgit apart on most menus.

The honest summary: at the cheap end, coffee wins comfortably — nothing in matcha competes with budget instant. In the middle, where most readers of this book live, the per-cup difference is small enough that it should not decide anything. At the top end, both hobbies will happily absorb any amount of money you offer them.

## The taste question

Taste gets left out of these comparisons, which is absurd, because you will drink this beverage thousands of times and every single time will be about taste.

Coffee's flavor needs no defense: roasted, deep, bitter-sweet, endlessly variable, and beloved by most of the adult world. It is also, crucially, an easy taste to begin — sweetened, milked coffee is instantly likeable, and most people's palates walk the road from mocha to black filter over years without effort.

Matcha's taste asks for a slightly longer courtship. The first plain bowl surprises people: green, vegetal, oceanic, with a bitterness that has nowhere to hide and an umami that some tongues initially read as "seaweed soup" rather than "tea." The latte is the easy on-ramp — that is precisely why it conquered the world — but the plain bowl usually takes a few honest attempts before it clicks. When it clicks, though, something interesting happens: the flavor turns out to have the same depth coffee people spend years exploring — sweetness, brothiness, bitterness, finish, all shifting with grade, region, water, and your own improving technique. Neither drink is objectively better-tasting. But coffee's learning curve is a ramp and matcha's is a step, and you deserve to know that before your first disappointing bowl convinces you matcha "isn't for you." It might just not be for you *yet* — or it might truly not be, which is also allowed.

## The ritual question

Strip away the chemistry and the economics and one difference remains, and I think it is the real one.

Coffee's culture, at its everyday center, is fuel culture. Its language gives it away: grab a coffee, need my coffee, coffee run, don't talk to me before my coffee. It is the drink of getting going — brilliant, beloved, and fundamentally in service of the day's momentum.

Matcha's inheritance runs the other way. Four centuries of tea ceremony built the drink around stopping — around the radical idea that preparing and drinking something can be the day's still point rather than its accelerant. You do not need the ceremony (this book has said so repeatedly), but even the minimal kitchen version — sift, pour, whisk, lift — refuses to be done absentmindedly. You cannot whisk matcha while scrolling. The drink physically requisitions ninety seconds of your presence.

Neither orientation is superior. There are mornings built for momentum and mornings that desperately need a still point, and most lives contain both. But if you recognize yourself in a particular modern condition — caffeinated, productive, vaguely frantic, unable to remember the last time you did one thing at a time — then matcha is not just a different molecule delivery system. It is a different posture toward the morning, and the posture may help you more than the theanine does.

## So who should drink what?

The honest scorecard, with no winner declared, because there isn't one:

**Coffee is probably your drink if:** you love its taste (sufficient reason all by itself); you want maximum alertness per cup; convenience and speed matter most; your budget lives at the cheap end; or coffee simply gives you no trouble worth fixing. Do not let anyone wellness you out of a drink that serves you well.

**Matcha deserves a serious trial if:** coffee makes you jittery, crashes you in the afternoon, or upsets your stomach; you want a gentler, longer arc of alertness; you are drawn to the flavor or curious about where it leads; or your days need a built-in pause more than they need another accelerator. Give it two weeks and a decent tin, not one bowl of stale powder, before you judge.

**And both is not a compromise — it may be the actual optimum.** Plenty of thoughtful drinkers run coffee on the mornings that need horsepower and matcha on the afternoons and slow days, or coffee at the desk and matcha as the weekend's quiet bowl. The two drinks are not competing religions. They are two different tools for the ancient project of being awake on purpose, and a well-stocked kitchen may simply contain a grinder and a whisk, at peace, one shelf apart.

That is the fairest verdict I can give you, and notice that nothing in it required matcha to be a superfood or coffee to be a villain. The leaf survives comparison on its own merits: a distinctive taste, a gentler arc, a built-in pause, and eight hundred years of knowing exactly what it is.

What it also became, in the last few decades, is something else entirely: a business — one of the most photogenic, fastest-moving, and strangest markets in the world of food. How a monk's medicine turned into the green gold rush is the story of the next part of the book.
