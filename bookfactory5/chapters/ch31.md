# When It Tastes Wrong

Somewhere right now, someone is standing in a kitchen holding a bowl of matcha that cost real money, tasting it with a slight frown, and quietly concluding that they are not a matcha person. Maybe it is you. You followed the steps. You bought a decent tin. And the thing in your hands is bitter, or lumpy, or flat, or the color of an old army jacket, and the gap between what you made and what the cafe sells feels like proof of some personal deficiency.

It is not. Here is the most reassuring sentence in this book: **every failed bowl of matcha has a boring cause.** Not a mystical one, not a talent one — a boring, physical, findable cause. Water that was twenty degrees too hot. Powder that sat in a warm cupboard since March. A whisk moved at half the speed it needed. In eight hundred years, nobody has ever ruined a bowl of matcha through lack of soul. They ruined it through temperature, dose, freshness, or speed, which is wonderful news, because all four of those can be fixed by Thursday.

This chapter is the clinic. Six complaints walk through the door over and over — bitterness, clumps, no foam, off flavors, split milk drinks, and wrong color — and each one gets the same treatment: a short diagnosis, then a table of causes ranked from most likely to least, each with a test you can run in your own kitchen and the fix that follows. Chapter 23 gave you the emergency card; this is the full consultation. Read it once now, then come back whenever a bowl disappoints you, because one will. Mine still do, some mornings, and the cause is always on one of these pages.

One habit before we start: **change one thing at a time.** If you swap the water temperature, the dose, and the tin all at once and the bowl improves, you have learned nothing. Run one test, taste, then run the next. It takes two extra minutes and it turns guessing into knowing.

## Complaint one: "It's too bitter"

This is the complaint that walks in most often, and it carries the most self-blame — people assume bitterness means they bought badly or that matcha is simply not for them. Usually it means neither. Bitterness has four common causes, and they are not equally likely, so test them in order and stop when the bowl improves.

The overwhelming favorite is water temperature. Catechins — the bitter compounds Chapter 13 introduced — extract enthusiastically from hot water and much more politely from cooler water, and the difference between 95°C and 75°C is the difference between a scalding interrogation and a civil conversation. Boiling water does not merely warm matcha; it strip-mines it. The second suspect is dose: two grams in 80 ml is a drink, four grams in 80 ml is an ordeal, and an uncalibrated "heaped teaspoon" can easily be double what you think it is. Third is the job-grade mismatch — culinary matcha is deliberately bolder and more bitter because it must survive milk and sugar, and drunk plain it tastes exactly like what it is. Fourth, and often overlooked, is age: as matcha oxidizes, its sweetness and umami fade first, which leaves the bitterness standing alone in the ruins, more exposed than it ever was when the tin was fresh.

| Cause (in order of likelihood) | The test | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Water too hot | Boil, then wait a full two minutes (or thermometer to 70–75°C); make the same bowl again | Never pour straight off the boil; cooler water is the single biggest bitterness lever you own |
| Too much powder | Weigh your usual scoop on a kitchen scale | Hold the line at 2 g per 70–80 ml; if no scale, level the spoon instead of heaping it |
| Culinary grade drunk plain | Check the tin; try the same powder in a milk latte instead | Keep bolder powder for lattes and bakes; buy a smaller, better tin for plain bowls (Chapter 10's checklist) |
| Stale powder | Open a fresh tin of the same matcha side by side | If the fresh one is sweeter, the tin was the problem; store as Chapter 12 orders and buy smaller amounts |

If you run all four tests and the bowl is still harsher than you like, the honest answer may be that this particular matcha is simply a bitter one — grades vary, producers vary, and your palate gets a vote. That is a buying problem, not a technique problem, and Chapter 20 is its clinic.

## Complaint two: "It's clumpy and gritty"

Little green lumps floating on the surface, or a last sip like licking a chalkboard. This one has the most satisfying diagnosis in the clinic because the cause is almost always the same and the fix costs nothing: the powder was not sifted, and matcha that is not sifted does not disperse — it huddles.

Matcha particles are so fine that they cling to each other through static and humidity, forming tiny clumps with dry powder trapped inside. Water wets the outside of a clump and seals it, like flour in a badly made gravy, and no amount of furious whisking reliably breaks it open afterward. Sifting takes ten seconds and destroys the clumps before the water ever arrives. If you sift and still get lumps, the powder itself has absorbed moisture from the air — the tin has been opened over a steaming kettle once too often, or lives somewhere warm — and its texture has coarsened, which brings us to grit: a slightly coarse, sandy last sip can also simply be the grind. Machine-ground budget matcha has larger, sharper-edged particles than stone-ground powder, and no technique of yours will polish them.

There is also one clumping problem that has nothing to do with sifting: adding powder directly to a large volume of cold liquid. Cold water wets matcha even more grudgingly than hot, which is why iced-drink recipes in Chapter 25 always whisk the powder into a small amount of liquid first.

| Cause | The test | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Didn't sift | Sift once and remake the bowl | Sift every single time; it is the highest-return ten seconds in this book |
| Powder absorbed humidity | Pinch the powder — does it feel damp, or press into pellets? | Reseal fast, store cool and dry, never spoon over a steaming kettle; accept the tin's remaining life is short |
| Powder added straight to cold liquid | Compare: whisked-into-a-splash-first vs dumped into the glass | Always make a small slurry or warm concentrate first, then build the drink on top |
| Coarse machine grind | Rub a pinch between your fingers — silk, or fine sand? | No fix at the bowl; use it for baking, where texture disappears, and grade up for drinking |

A gritty *final* sip alone, after a smooth bowl, is not a defect at all — suspended powder settles. Swirl gently as you finish. That is matcha being matcha.

## Complaint three: "There's no foam"

A proper bowl of usucha wears a fine, pale-green crema, and a bowl without it tastes thinner and flatter even though the chemistry is identical — foam carries aroma and softens texture, and its absence is the most visible failure in the clinic. It is also the one most reliably caused by technique rather than ingredients, which means it is the most fixable.

The chief cause, by a wide margin, is whisking too slowly. Foam is made by beating air into liquid faster than the bubbles can escape, and almost everyone underestimates the speed required — Chapter 23 said it and the clinic repeats it: the motion is a fast, light zigzag from the wrist, like flicking water off your fingertips, not a stately stir from the elbow. The second cause is too much water: 150 ml in a mug gives the whisk twice the liquid to aerate with the same effort, and the foam never gets ahead of the drainage. Third is the tool — a fork or a spoon cannot do this job, and even a chasen with tines splayed from age moves less water per stroke. Fourth, foam is one of the first abilities matcha loses as it ages; the surface-active compounds that stabilize the bubbles fade with oxidation, so a tired tin gives tired foam no matter how heroic your wrist.

| Cause | The test | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Whisking too slow or too briefly | Count honestly: did you whisk fast for a full 15–20 seconds? | Speed from the wrist, tines just off the bottom, M/W zigzag — faster than feels dignified |
| Too much water | Measure your water for once instead of eyeballing | 70–80 ml for usucha; foam a small bowl first, then dilute if you want a longer drink |
| Wrong tool | Borrow or buy a chasen or an electric frother and repeat | A chasen (茶筅) or a cheap electric frother both work; cutlery does not |
| Old, oxidized matcha | Fresh tin, same technique, side by side | If the fresh tin foams and the old one won't, the verdict is in; Chapter 12 again |
| Big soapy bubbles instead of fine foam | Watch your own motion — are you circling? | Circles make bubbles, zigzags make foam; finish with a slow lift from the center |

One consolation while you practice: koicha never foams, on purpose. If tonight's foam refuses to appear, add the second half of the water gently, call it a rustic bowl, and try again tomorrow with a faster wrist.

## Complaint four: "It tastes fishy, stale, like hay"

This complaint is different from the first three, and the clinic owes you directness about it: bitterness, clumps, and flat foam are usually *your technique*, but hay, cardboard, dust, and that faintly fishy, dried-seaweed-gone-wrong note are almost always *the powder itself*. No water temperature fixes them. They are the taste of oxidation — the same slow chemical surrender that turns cooking oil rancid and old nuts sad — and matcha, being a powder with enormous surface area and no protective leaf structure left, oxidizes faster than nearly anything else in your kitchen. Chapter 12 called it the tea that dies young; this is what the dying tastes like.

The fishy note deserves its own word, because it alarms people. Matcha is seaweedy-savory even when fresh — that is umami, and it is supposed to be there. The off version is different: sharper, older-smelling, closer to the fish counter at closing time than to a clean coastline. It appears when a marine-leaning matcha has oxidized, and once you have smelled fresh and stale side by side you will never confuse them again. Note also that heat amplifies the problem — a powder that tastes merely dull in a cold latte can turn convincingly stale in a hot one.

| Cause | The test | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tin open too long | Check when you opened it — more than a month or two ago? | Finish opened tins within weeks; buy 30 g tins, not 100 g bags, whatever the per-gram price says |
| Bad storage (heat, light, air, damp) | Where does the tin live? Next to the stove? On a sunny shelf? | Airtight, dark, cool; a fridge works if the tin is truly sealed and comes to room temperature before opening |
| Old stock at purchase | Harvest or packing date on the label — or no date at all | Buy from sellers who print dates and turn stock over; a missing date is itself information (Chapter 20) |
| Smells fine, tastes dusty only when hot | Taste the same powder cold-whisked | Heat is exposing marginal powder; demote the tin to smoothies and bakes and open a fresh one for hot bowls |

The fix table is honest but limited, because oxidation runs one way. Cooking can shelter a fading tin — sugar, dairy, and flour are forgiving — but nothing resurrects it. The real fix is the next purchase: smaller, fresher, dated.

## Complaint five: "My iced latte split"

You built a beautiful layered iced matcha, carried it to the table, and it curdled into snowflakes of protein drifting in green whey. Or the oat milk went grainy. This complaint belongs to Part Five's cold drinks, and it has three culprits that often work as a gang: acid, temperature shock, and the particular personalities of plant milks.

Acid is the ringleader. Milk proteins — dairy casein and many plant proteins alike — hold themselves apart in liquid until the surroundings turn acidic, at which point they give up and cling together into curds. That is deliberate in cheesemaking and heartbreaking in a glass. Matcha itself is only mildly acidic and rarely splits anything alone, but the moment a recipe adds lemon, yuzu, passion fruit, or berry syrup to a milk drink, you are running a small cheesemaking experiment with a countdown timer. Temperature shock is the accomplice: proteins tolerate insults better when changes come gently, and pouring hot matcha concentrate straight onto ice-cold milk (or icy liquid into warm milk) pushes fragile milks over the edge. And plant milks vary enormously in how much of this they endure — soy is famously acid-sensitive, homemade and single-ingredient nut milks split at a harsh word, while "barista" oat and soy editions carry stabilizers precisely so cafes can abuse them.

| Cause | The test | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Citrus or fruit acid meeting milk | Did the recipe contain lemon, yuzu, or fruit syrup plus milk? | Keep acid drinks dairy-free (Chapter 26 does); if you must combine, add acid last, over ice, and drink promptly |
| Temperature shock | Did hot concentrate hit cold milk, or ice hit warm milk? | Cool the matcha concentrate to room temperature before it meets cold milk; build iced drinks cold from the start |
| Fragile plant milk | Same recipe with a barista-edition oat or soy | Barista formulations exist for exactly this; or switch to coconut milk, which shrugs off matcha's acidity |
| Milk near its date | Smell and taste the milk alone | Older milk curdles at the slightest provocation; this one is not matcha's fault at all |

A split drink is safe to consume — it is texture, not spoilage, assuming the milk itself was fresh — but nobody enjoys it. Prevention is the whole game.

## Complaint six: "The color is wrong"

Matcha promised you that lit-from-within green of Chapter 1, and your bowl is khaki. Before fixing anything, run the clinic's most important fork in the road: **is the powder khaki, or only the drink?** Open the tin and look at the dry powder against something white, in daylight.

If the powder itself is dull — olive, yellowish, army-surplus — no technique will help, because color was decided months ago in the field and the months since. Dull powder means less shading, older leaves, coarser grade, or age and oxidation, and everything downstream of a khaki powder is khaki. That is a buying-and-storage verdict: Chapters 10 and 12 are the referral. But if the powder is vividly green and the *drink* goes dull, you are doing something to it in the kitchen, and there are exactly three usual suspects. Boiling water is the first — chlorophyll degrades with aggressive heat, and the same too-hot pour that made complaint one bitter also browns the green. Acid is the second, and the most dramatic: chlorophyll's bright color depends on a magnesium atom seated at the center of the molecule, and acid knocks it loose, shifting the color toward olive — squeeze lemon into a bright green matcha drink and you can watch it happen within minutes. Time is the third: any whisked matcha left standing for hours will slowly dim as it oxidizes in the glass.

| Cause | The test | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Powder was never green | Dry powder on white paper, daylight | No kitchen fix; buy better and store colder — and use this tin where color is hidden (bakes, smoothies) |
| Boiling water | Remake with properly rested 70–75°C water | The same two-minute wait that fixes bitterness protects the green |
| Acid in the drink | Add the citrus to half the batch only and compare after ten minutes | Serve acidic matcha drinks immediately; add citrus at the last moment, not in advance |
| Drink left standing | Note the color at minute one vs hour three | Matcha is a drink-it-now suspension, not a keep-it-around beverage; make less, more often |

One reassurance for the bakers reading ahead: matcha *always* dulls in the oven — heat and time guarantee it — and Part Six has its own tricks for protecting the green. A khaki cookie is normal. A khaki bowl is a message.

## The five-minute reset

When a bowl goes wrong and you cannot face running diagnostics, there is a blunter instrument, and I offer it with the confidence of someone who has prescribed it many times: reset everything to known-good and remake the bowl once, properly. In my kitchen and in every kitchen I have talked through this, the reset cures the overwhelming majority of complaints in this chapter — because most failed bowls have not one cause but two or three small ones stacked, and the reset clears them all at once.

- **Fresh, well-kept powder** — from a tin opened within the last month, stored sealed, cool, and dark. If you do not have one, this is the moment to accept that no technique outruns a tired tin.
- **Sift it.** Every time. Ten seconds.
- **Water at 70–75°C** — boiled, then rested two full minutes. Time it; do not vibe it.
- **2 g in 70–80 ml.** Weigh the powder once so your eye learns what 2 g looks like; measure the water in anything with markings.
- **Whisk fast** — the M/W zigzag from the wrist, tines just off the bottom, a genuine 15–20 seconds, finishing with a slow lift.

Five lines, five minutes. If the reset bowl is good, work backward to find which shortcut had crept in — it is usually the water temperature or the unsifted tin. If the reset bowl is *still* wrong, you have learned something equally valuable: the problem is the powder, not you, and Chapters 10, 12, and 20 will not let you buy that problem twice.

And that closes Part Five. You arrived at these chapters knowing how to order a matcha drink; you leave knowing how to build nearly forty of them, from the austere perfection of a plain bowl to a sparkling yuzu spritz, and — as of this chapter — how to rescue any of them when they misbehave. The whisk has done all it can for you. What comes next needs an oven.
