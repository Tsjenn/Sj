# No-Bake and Chilled

Take the dish out of the refrigerator and hold it a moment before you dust it. Under the cling film the surface is a pale, even cream, cold and faintly sweet-smelling, and the kitchen has done nothing violent to get here — no preheating, no timers, no anxious peering through an oven door. Now lift the film, load the small sieve, and sift a thin green snowfall across the whole surface, corner to corner. Look at that color. It is not the sage-green of a baked sponge or the olive compromise of an overdone cookie edge. It is the color from inside the tin — the actual, unreasonable, lit-from-within green you paid for — sitting on a dessert, unharmed. Nothing in this chapter will ever ask that color to survive an oven, because nothing in this chapter goes near one.

For the last three chapters we have been fighting a defensive war. Chapter thirty-two laid out what heat does to a ground leaf — the aromatics out the oven vent, the chlorophyll sliding toward khaki — and the cookie and cake chapters were built around limiting the damage. This chapter is the armistice. The hottest thing any recipe here touches is cream warmed to steaming, and most of the work is done by the refrigerator, which is not merely harmless to matcha but actively kind to it: cold slows the same chemistry that storage chapter twelve taught you to fear. That is why these five are the greenest, most fragrant recipes in Part Six, and why this chapter contains the tiramisu I told you about in chapter one — the one I am unreasonably proud of.

It also changes the buying advice, and this is worth saying plainly. Twice now this book has told you that the workhorse culinary tin is the *correct* tool — for lattes, because milk muffles the fine top notes, and for baking, because the oven destroys them. Here, for the first time in Part Six, the better tin earns its place in a bake. Nothing below is heated enough to steal the aroma, and two jobs in this chapter — the matcha you dip the ladyfingers in, and the matcha you dust over finished surfaces — are eaten essentially raw, exactly as they left the tin. My honest split: keep the workhorse tin for anything folded deep into cream cheese or condensed milk, where fat and sugar will muffle subtlety anyway, and let the good tin do the dips and the dusting, where every ringgit of quality lands on the tongue. The dusting rule that goes with it: **always dust at the last minute.** Matcha powder on a moist surface drinks the moisture within an hour or two, darkens to a wet forest green, and turns bitter and streaky. Sift it on just before serving — at the table, if you like the small theatre of it — and it stays dry, vivid, and fragrant.

Two more standing orders carry over from chapter thirty-two: sift, always, and add matcha to liquids as a slurry or into warm — never simmering — cream. And one honesty note before the recipes, because this book does not invent ancestors for its food: nothing in this chapter is ancient. Tiramisu and panna cotta are Italian; nama chocolate is modern Japanese confectionery, a few decades old; the matcha versions of all of them belong to the cafe era, not the tearoom. That takes nothing away from them. A dessert does not need a fake pedigree to be worth making — it needs to be good, and these are.

### Matcha Tiramisu

Here it is — the flagship. Classic tiramisu is a swap machine: sponge soaked in something intense and bitter, layered with a rich, barely-sweet cream, dusted with something dark. Replace the espresso with strong whisked matcha and the cocoa with sifted green, and every joint of the original design still works, because matcha and espresso do the same structural job — a bitter, aromatic soak that keeps all that cream from being cloying. This version builds the cream from mascarpone and whipped cream, with no raw egg, so you can serve it to anyone; the traditional egg route is waiting in the variation. Make it a day ahead. Tiramisu is one of the rare desserts that is genuinely better on the second day, and the fridge does that work for free.

*Serves 6–8 from a 20 cm (8 in) square dish. About 30 minutes of work, then at least 6 hours — ideally overnight — of chilling.*

**You need:**

- 8 g matcha (about 4 teaspoons) for the soak — use a tin you would happily drink
- 250 ml (1 cup) hot water, off the boil (70–80°C)
- 2 tablespoons sugar, for the soak
- 250 g (a generous 1 cup) mascarpone, at cool room temperature
- 250 ml (1 cup) cold whipping cream
- 60 g (5 tablespoons) sugar, for the cream
- ½ teaspoon vanilla extract (optional)
- About 200 g savoiardi ladyfingers (roughly 24 — the dry, crisp kind)
- 1–2 teaspoons matcha for dusting, the best you own

**Steps:**

1. Make the soak first so it can cool: sift the 8 g of matcha into a wide bowl, add a splash of the hot water, and knead to a smooth paste, then whisk in the rest of the water and the 2 tablespoons of sugar. This is essentially a double-strength sweetened usucha. Pour it into a shallow dish and let it cool to room temperature — a hot soak would melt the cream layers later.
2. Whip the cold cream with the 60 g of sugar and the vanilla to soft peaks — the stage where the cream holds a shape but the peak's tip folds over. Stop there.
3. In a large bowl, stir the mascarpone briefly with a spatula just until smooth and spreadable. Fold in the whipped cream in two additions, gently, until no streaks remain. Do not beat it: mascarpone is high-fat and overworking it turns the cream grainy, then buttery.
4. Dip the ladyfingers, one at a time, in the cooled matcha: **about one second per side.** Roll it through the liquid and out. It will look barely wet. Trust it — a dry-style savoiardi keeps drinking after it is in the dish.
5. Lay half the dipped fingers in the dish in a snug single layer, trimming a few to fit. Spread on half the cream. Repeat: fingers, then the rest of the cream, smoothed level.
6. Cover and refrigerate at least 6 hours, ideally overnight. The soak migrates, the layers fuse, and the whole thing becomes sliceable and tender at once.
7. Just before serving — not sooner — sift the dusting matcha evenly over the top, edge to edge. Cut with a knife wiped between cuts, and watch the cross-section do its green-and-cream stripes.

**The tricky part:** the one-second dip. Every first tiramisu fails in one of two directions, and both happen at step 4. Linger, and the fingers arrive at the dish already sodden; by tomorrow the base is a green swamp. Rush so much the liquid never penetrates, and tomorrow you cut through dry biscuit in cream. One second per side looks wrong and is right — the biscuit finishes the job overnight with the moisture it took in. If your ladyfingers are the soft, cake-like kind, halve even that: a brush of soak on each side is plenty.

**One variation:** the traditional egg cream, for when you want the old-fashioned silkiness. Whisk 3 egg yolks with 50 g (¼ cup) of the sugar until pale and thick, fold into the mascarpone, then fold in the cream whipped without sugar. This is the classic route and it is wonderful — and it contains raw egg, so treat it plainly as such: use pasteurized eggs if you can get them, keep the dish cold and eat it within two days, and let anyone pregnant, very young, elderly, or with a weakened immune system have the main version instead. That is the whole safety note, and it is not optional.

### No-Bake Matcha Cheesecake

A baked matcha cheesecake fights for its color in the oven; this one never has to. A pressed biscuit base, a whipped cream-cheese filling set with gelatin, and the refrigerator do everything a springform in a water bath would do, minus the heat and most of the anxiety. The result is lighter than a baked cake — closer to a mousse standing at attention — and green all the way through in a way its baked cousins only manage on their best day.

*Serves 8 from a 20 cm (8 in) springform tin. About 40 minutes of work, then at least 6 hours of chilling.*

**You need:**

- 150 g plain sweet biscuits, digestive-style (about 1½ cups fine crumbs)
- 70 g (5 tablespoons) butter, melted
- 400 g (1¾ cups) full-fat cream cheese, at room temperature
- 100 g (½ cup) sugar
- 8 g matcha (about 4 teaspoons)
- 200 ml (a scant 1 cup) whipping cream, plus 60 ml (¼ cup) extra for the matcha slurry
- 6 g (2 teaspoons) powdered gelatin, bloomed in 45 ml (3 tablespoons) cold water
- Finely grated zest of half a lemon (optional — zest, not juice; chapter thirty-two explained why acid and chlorophyll are enemies)

**Steps:**

1. Crush the biscuits to fine crumbs, stir in the melted butter until the texture is wet sand, and press firmly into the base of the tin — the bottom of a glass makes a good tamper. Refrigerate while you make the filling.
2. Sprinkle the gelatin over the 45 ml of cold water and leave it to bloom into a solid sponge, about 5 minutes.
3. Warm the extra 60 ml of cream until comfortably hot to a fingertip — not simmering. Sift the matcha into a small bowl, add the warm cream, and whisk to a smooth, glossy slurry.
4. Beat the cream cheese with the sugar until completely smooth, then beat in the matcha slurry and the zest, if using.
5. Melt the bloomed gelatin gently — a few seconds over a pan of warm water, or 10-second bursts in a microwave — until just liquid, never boiling. Working quickly, beat it into the cheese mixture until fully combined.
6. Whip the 200 ml of cream to soft peaks and fold it in, in two additions, until even. Pour over the base, smooth the top, cover, and chill at least 6 hours.
7. To unmold, run a thin knife dipped in hot water around the inside of the tin before releasing the ring. Dust with a little extra matcha at the table if you like — last minute, as always.

**The tricky part:** the gelatin choreography in steps 5 and 6. Melted gelatin poured into a cold mixture sets on contact into little rubbery threads you cannot beat out; gelatin that boils loses setting power. The fix is temperature management: room-temperature cheese, gelatin just barely melted, and no pauses — the moment the gelatin is liquid, it goes in and gets beaten through. If your kitchen is cold, stir a spoonful of the cheese mixture into the gelatin first to temper it, then add that back to the bowl.

**One variation:** the condensed-milk set, which needs no gelatin at all. Skip the gelatin and the sugar; beat the cream cheese smooth, beat in 320 g (1 cup) sweetened condensed milk, the matcha slurry, and 60 ml (¼ cup) lemon juice — the acid thickens the dairy into a soft set. Two honest caveats: it is sweeter, and it sets softer, so serve it spooned from a dish or in glasses rather than unmolded, and accept that the lemon juice will pull the green a shade toward olive by day two. (For a vegetarian version of the main recipe, agar works in place of gelatin — the method and the honest texture difference are in the panna cotta below.)

### Matcha Panna Cotta

Panna cotta means "cooked cream," which flatters it — the cream is only warmed, the gelatin does the rest, and the whole recipe is fifteen minutes of work followed by a wait. It is the purest expression in this chapter of what cold does for matcha: nothing between you and the leaf but sweetened dairy, and because the mixture never simmers, the aroma you smell when you open the tin is recognizably the aroma in the finished glass. It is also the recipe where you learn the single most transferable technique of the chilled kitchen: how to treat gelatin so it treats you well.

*Makes 4 servings in glasses. About 15 minutes of work, then at least 4 hours of chilling.*

**You need:**

- 300 ml (1¼ cups) whipping cream
- 200 ml (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon) whole milk
- 50 g (¼ cup) sugar
- 4 g matcha (about 2 teaspoons)
- 5 g (1¾ teaspoons) powdered gelatin, bloomed in 45 ml (3 tablespoons) cold water

**Steps:**

1. Bloom the gelatin: sprinkle it evenly over the cold water in a small bowl and leave it 5 minutes, until it has swollen into a translucent sponge with no dry patches.
2. Warm the cream, milk, and sugar in a saucepan until steam rises and the sugar has dissolved — well short of a simmer. Take it off the heat.
3. Ladle out a small cupful of the warm liquid. Sift the matcha into a bowl, add the ladleful, and whisk into a smooth slurry, then whisk the slurry back into the pan.
4. Add the bloomed gelatin to the warm pan and stir until it has melted completely — a minute, no more heat needed.
5. Strain the mixture through your matcha sieve into a jug, then pour into four glasses. Cool to room temperature, then refrigerate at least 4 hours.
6. Check the set with the wobble test: tilt a glass gently. The surface should lean as one piece and shiver like something alive — a tremble, not a slosh, and not a rigid, unmoving disc. That narrow band of wobble is the whole point of panna cotta.

**The tricky part:** the bloom, because it is the step that looks skippable and is not. Gelatin granules dropped straight into hot liquid seal themselves into clots — swollen outside, dry inside — that never fully dissolve, and you find them later as chewy flecks in a silky dessert. Blooming in cold water first lets every granule hydrate evenly, so it melts completely and invisibly into the warm cream. Cold first, always; heat second. That one habit fixes gelatin everywhere in this chapter.

**One variation:** the agar version — and here the tradition actually runs the other way, because agar (kanten, 寒天), extracted from seaweed, has centuries of history in Japanese confectionery, far longer than gelatin has. Replace the gelatin with 2 g (1 teaspoon) agar powder: whisk it into the cold milk, bring to a boil, and hold at a gentle simmer for 2 full minutes — unlike gelatin, agar *must* boil to activate — then add the cream, sugar, and matcha slurry off the heat and pour. It is vegetarian and it sets even at room temperature, but be honest with your expectations: agar sets firmer and cleaner, with a delicate snap instead of a wobble, and it does not melt on the tongue the way gelatin does. It is a different dessert — a good one — not an invisible substitute.

### Matcha Nama Chocolate

Nama chocolate (生チョコ — *nama* meaning "raw" or "fresh") is a modern Japanese confection: ganache with the cream turned up so high it can barely hold a shape, chilled in a slab, cut into small squares, and rolled in powder. The famous versions come from Hokkaido, dairy country, which tells you what the style celebrates — cream, freshness, and a texture that dissolves rather than melts. The matcha version pairs a white-chocolate ganache with green both inside and out, and it is chapter thirty-two's pairing logic in its purest form: matcha's bitterness pushing against cocoa butter's sweetness, with nothing else in the room. Sixteen small squares, and they vanish at a pace that will alarm you.

*Makes 16 squares from a 15 cm (6 in) square container. About 20 minutes of work, then at least 4 hours of chilling.*

**You need:**

- 200 g (7 oz) white chocolate, chopped small and evenly — use a bar you would eat, not baking chips, which contain stabilizers that fight the texture
- 90 ml (6 tablespoons) whipping cream
- 10 g (2 teaspoons) unsalted butter, soft
- 6 g matcha (about 1 tablespoon) for the ganache
- 2 teaspoons matcha for dusting, your good tin
- A 15 cm (6 in) square container lined with parchment, with overhang for lifting

**Steps:**

1. Put the chopped chocolate in a heatproof bowl. Warm the cream to steaming — small bubbles at the pan's edge, no further — and pour it over the chocolate. Leave it alone for 2 full minutes.
2. Stir gently from the center outward with a spatula until the ganache comes together smooth and glossy. If a few stubborn pieces remain, set the bowl briefly over warm (not simmering) water.
3. While the ganache is warm — comfortably warm, not hot — sift in the 6 g of matcha and whisk until the color is deep and even, then stir in the butter until it disappears.
4. Pour into the lined container, level the surface by tapping it on the counter, cover, and refrigerate at least 4 hours, until fully set but still yielding.
5. Lift the slab out by the parchment. Cut into 16 squares with a long knife warmed under hot water and wiped dry — warm, wipe, cut, repeat, and the faces come out mirror-clean.
6. Sift the dusting matcha generously over the squares just before serving, turning them to coat the sides if you like. Keep them refrigerated and eat within 4 days — this is fresh confectionery, as the name says, not shelf candy.

**The tricky part:** heat control at the start. White chocolate is the most temperature-fragile chocolate there is — cream that is actually boiling, or impatient stirring over direct heat, will split the ganache into a grainy sludge sitting in a slick of cocoa butter. Chop fine, heat the cream only to steaming, and give the two minutes of patience in step 1; the resting bowl does the melting better than your spatula can. If it splits anyway, all is not lost: whisk in warm cream a teaspoon at a time until it swings back to glossy.

**One variation:** perfume the cream. Warm it with a few strips of yuzu or orange zest, let it steep off the heat for 10 minutes, then strain, reheat to steaming, and proceed. Chapter thirty-two's pairing list promised that citrus zest lends matcha the fragrance without the color-attacking acid, and inside this much cocoa butter the combination tastes like a very good truffle from a very good shop — one you made in a lined takeaway container.

### Matcha Pudding Cups

Every chapter of recipes should end with the one you can make on a weeknight with a child on a stool beside you, and this is it. No gelatin, no whipping, no technique that punishes wandering attention for more than a minute: milk, sugar, cornstarch, matcha, one pot, constant stirring, four cups. It is the humblest thing in Part Six and the one you may make most often.

*Makes 4 small cups. About 10 minutes at the stove, then 2 hours of chilling.*

**You need:**

- 500 ml (2 cups) whole milk
- 45 g (3½ tablespoons) sugar
- 30 g (¼ cup) cornstarch
- 3 g matcha (about 1½ teaspoons) — for small children, halve this: matcha is still tea, caffeine included, and a gentler cup is the honest one to hand them
- A pinch of salt

**Steps:**

1. In a cold saucepan, whisk the cornstarch, sugar, salt, and sifted matcha together thoroughly. Dry-whisking is this recipe's clump insurance: every particle of matcha ends up separated and chaperoned by starch before liquid arrives.
2. Add the cold milk gradually, whisking as you pour, until smooth and green.
3. Set over medium heat and stir constantly with the whisk, sweeping the bottom and into the corners. For a few minutes nothing will happen. Then, quite suddenly, it will thicken.
4. When the first slow bubbles break the surface, keep stirring and cook 1 minute more.
5. Pour into four cups. For skinless pudding, press cling film onto each surface; some households consider the skin the best part, in which case leave them bare and let the argument settle itself. Chill 2 hours.

**The tricky part:** stopping too early. Cornstarch does not finish its work until the mixture genuinely bubbles — pull the pot at "thick but never boiled" and you get pudding that is loose by serving time and carries a faint raw-flour chalkiness. The full minute at a bubble, stirring all the while so the bottom never catches, cooks the starch completely: clean flavor, proper set. When your whisk leaves brief trails that hold their shape, you are there.

**One variation:** the parfait treatment. Layer the warm pudding into glasses alternately with crumbled biscuits, chill, and crown with softly whipped cream and — last minute, final time I will say it — a sift of good matcha. It is three pantry steps past plain pudding and looks like something from a cafe case, which is roughly what it is.

Five recipes, and not one of them asked the green to survive anything worse than warm cream. That is the lesson of this chapter, and the next one pushes it to its logical end: if the refrigerator is matcha's friend, the freezer is its bodyguard. Cold is matcha's ally — it slows every enemy the storage chapter named — and below zero it preserves color and aroma almost perfectly while opening a new set of doors: an ice cream that needs no machine, just as chapter one promised, and the syrup for a proper kakigōri. Clear a shelf in the freezer. We are going colder.
