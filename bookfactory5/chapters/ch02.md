# Powdered Tea Before It Had a Name

Picture a spring morning in the hills of Fujian, in the south of China, somewhere around nine hundred years ago. Two men kneel on a wooden veranda with a small crowd pressing in behind them. Each has a dark stoneware bowl, a kettle of water just off the boil, and a little heap of tea — not leaves, but a powder, pale and fine, ground that morning from a roasted cake of pressed tea. A referee of sorts stands between them. Money has probably changed hands over the outcome. It usually did.

Each man spoons powder into his bowl, adds a thread of hot water, and works it into a paste. Then more water, poured in careful stages, and now the real performance begins: a fast, practiced beating of the tea until it rises into a froth — pale, thick, standing on the surface of the bowl like snow settling on dark ground. The crowd leans in. They are not waiting to taste anything. They are watching the foam.

Because here is how you win: your froth must be whiter than your rival's, and it must last. As the minutes pass, the foam clings to the wall of the black bowl, and the first bowl to show a water mark — a thin line where the froth has pulled away from the glaze — loses. The Song Chinese had a lovely phrase for froth that held on well: they said it *bit the bowl*. Whole afternoons were lost to this. Fortunes, too, and famous bowls, and the attention of at least one emperor who should probably have been doing something else.

If you have ever whisked a bowl of matcha and watched the foam settle, you have done — almost gesture for gesture — what those two men were doing on that veranda. The tea was Chinese, the bowl was Chinese, the whisking was Chinese, and the word *matcha* did not exist yet. This chapter is about that world: how China invented powdered, whisked tea, raised it to a competitive art, and then — in one of history's stranger turns — walked away from it so completely that its own invention had to be reintroduced to it centuries later, wearing a Japanese name.

## An Empire That Drank Its Tea in Bricks

To understand why anyone was grinding tea into powder in the first place, you have to forget the loose-leaf tea in your cupboard and start with a practical problem: how do you store and transport a leaf across a very large empire without it crumbling to dust or rotting in the humidity?

The answer, for many centuries of Chinese history, was compression. Freshly picked tea leaves were steamed to soften them, pounded, pressed into molds, and dried into solid cakes or bricks. A tea cake traveled well, stacked well, kept for a long time, and could even function as a kind of currency along the trade routes — which tells you how standardized and valued it had become. When you wanted a drink, you did not steep this thing. You broke a piece off, roasted it over a fire to dry and awaken it, ground it into granules or powder, and cooked it in water.

This is the world of the Tang dynasty — roughly the seventh through ninth centuries, one of the high-water marks of Chinese civilization — and it is the world that produced the first great book about tea and the man usually called the father of tea culture: Lu Yu (陸羽).

The outline of Lu Yu's life reads like a folk tale, and parts of it may be exactly that; even the account he left of himself blurs at the edges. The mainstream version goes like this: an orphan, taken in and raised in a Buddhist monastery in the early 700s, where he would have learned the preparation of tea as part of monastic life; a young man who ran away rather than take vows, spent time with a traveling theater troupe, and eventually found patrons who let him do the thing he actually cared about, which was to think about tea more carefully than anyone had ever thought about it before. Sometime in the later 700s he finished the *Classic of Tea* (茶經, *Chajing*) — the first known book in the world devoted entirely to tea.

What makes the *Classic of Tea* remarkable is not just that it was first. It is the attitude. Lu Yu treated tea as something worthy of complete attention: where the plant grows best, how the leaves should be picked and pressed, the two dozen or so implements a properly equipped tea preparer should own, which water to use — he ranked mountain streams above rivers, and rivers above wells — how to build the fire, when the water is ready, how to pour. He was also, delightfully, opinionated. Tea in his era was often boiled up with onion, ginger, jujube dates, orange peel, or mint, in the manner of a soup, and Lu Yu had no patience for it; he dismissed such concoctions as fit for the gutter. Tea, he argued, deserved to taste like tea.

Notice, though, what Tang tea still was: powdered, yes — ground from a roasted cake — but *boiled*. In Lu Yu's method the powder went into a cauldron of properly judged simmering water (with a pinch of salt), was stirred, and was then ladled out into bowls, froth and all. He cared about that froth already — the *Classic of Tea* lingers on the patterns the foam makes — but nobody was whisking anything in a bowl yet. The whisk needed another dynasty.

## The Song Turn: Tea at the Point of a Kettle

The Tang dynasty fell, China fractured and reassembled, and by 960 a new dynasty, the Song, had begun its three centuries. If the Tang was tea's childhood, the Song — from 960 to 1279 — was its extravagant, obsessive adolescence, and it is the era every matcha drinker is unknowingly reenacting.

Somewhere between late Tang and early Song — historians cannot pin a date on it, and it is honest to say so — the method changed. Instead of boiling the powder in a cauldron, people began preparing tea directly in the individual bowl. The powder went in first. Then hot water was poured over it from a kettle with a long, precise spout, and the mixture was beaten into that standing froth. The Chinese called the method *dian cha* (點茶) — often translated as "point tea" or "whipped tea," from the pouring of water in measured points or stages. The tea itself, powdered tea, appears in Song writing as *mo cha* (末茶) — literally "powder tea." Say it aloud and you can hear the future in it.

The refinement lavished on this simple act is hard to overstate. Manuals of the period — and the Song wrote manuals about tea the way other cultures wrote them about war — describe the sequence in loving detail. The cake of tea is roasted, then ground as finely as possible and sifted, because coarse powder will not froth. A little hot water first, worked into a smooth paste, so the powder cannot clump. Then water added in successive pours, whisking between each, the wrist doing the work, until the surface rises into a dense white foam. Early on the beating might be done with a spoon; in time the tool of choice became a whisk of finely split bamboo — the direct ancestor of the *chasen* that may be sitting in your kitchen drawer right now.

If that whole paragraph sounded familiar, it should. Paste first to prevent clumps; water in stages; fine sifted powder; bamboo whisk; judge the result by its foam. Eight centuries later, this is still, step for step, how a bowl of matcha is made. The Japanese tea ceremony did not invent its technique. It inherited it, and — as we will see — preserved it after the inventors moved on.

No figure captures Song tea mania better than the emperor Huizong (宋徽宗), who reigned from 1100 to 1126. Huizong was a gifted painter and calligrapher and, by the general verdict of history, a fairly disastrous head of state. Among his passions was tea, and he did something no reigning emperor is really supposed to have time for: he personally wrote a treatise on it. In it he holds forth on growing, grinding, water, bowls, and the correct multi-stage whisking of the froth, with the confidence of a man who had clearly stood there doing it himself. That an emperor of one of the largest states on earth wrote a connoisseur's manual on whipping tea tells you everything about the status the drink had reached. The end of his story is grimmer: the dynasty's northern collapse came in his lifetime, and he died a captive of the invaders. The tea culture he adored moved south with the court and carried on for another century and a half.

And at the very top of that culture sat the tribute teas. In the Northern Park gardens of Fujian — the same tea country where our chapter opened — imperial workshops pressed the finest early-spring leaf into small ornate cakes stamped with dragons and phoenixes, made exclusively for the court. Song writers marveled at what these dragon-phoenix cakes cost; the finest grades were said to be worth more than their weight in gold, and a cake was the kind of gift that could mark the high point of an official's career. It is worth pausing on that. A thousand years before anyone paid a premium for single-origin, stone-ground, first-harvest matcha, China had already established the essential economics of powdered tea: the best of it is scarce, labor-soaked, and shockingly expensive, and people will pay anyway.

## The Sport of Foam

Wherever humans refine a skill, competition follows, and Song tea culture produced one of history's most charming competitive formats: *doucha* (鬥茶), the tea contest — literally something like "tea fighting."

By most accounts the game grew up first among the tea growers and merchants of Fujian, as a working competition: whose tea, whisked side by side, was actually best? From there it spread upward and outward until everyone was playing — farmers, monks, poets, officials, and the court itself. Contests could be head-to-head or small tournaments; the stakes ranged from bragging rights to serious money to prized possessions.

The judging, as we saw on that veranda, centered on the froth. A whiter foam meant finer powder, better leaf, better grinding, better technique. And a durable foam — foam that *bit the bowl* and held its seamless surface — proved the whisking had been true. The first crescent of dark water to open up against the glaze was defeat, as objective and visible as a fallen wicket. Song paintings and poems return to these scenes again and again: the little braziers, the kettles, the concentration, the theater of it. Competitors developed reputations. Certain teas and certain springs of water were sought out specifically for contests. There was even a virtuoso offshoot in which practitioners coaxed patterns and pictures into the foam's surface — birds, branches, calligraphy — a Song ancestor, if you like, of the swan on your flat white, and one that contemporaries regarded with the same mix of admiration and eye-rolling.

It is easy to smile at all this, but stay with it a moment, because *doucha* explains something real about why matcha is the way it is. Competition is a ratchet. Once thousands of people are being publicly judged on the whiteness and stability of tea foam, everything upstream gets better: the picking gets more selective, the grinding gets finer, the sifting gets stricter, the whisking technique gets codified. Much of the precision that Japan later formalized — precision this book will spend chapters on — was first forged in the heat of a Chinese wager.

## Black Bowls, Built for White Foam

The contests also produced one of the great objects in the history of ceramics, and it exists for a beautifully practical reason.

If the game is judged on white foam and the first visible water mark, then the ideal bowl is dark — the darker the better. Against a black glaze, white froth glows, and the losing line of water shows the instant it appears. So the potters of the Jian kilns in Fujian, conveniently close to the great tea gardens, rose to the occasion with heavy stoneware bowls in deep, lustrous black-brown glazes: the famous *jianzhan* (建盞), Jian ware.

What makes these bowls astonishing is what happened in the kiln. The iron-rich glazes, fired at high temperatures, streaked and crystallized in ways the potters could guide but never fully control, producing effects that Song connoisseurs named like natural wonders: *hare's fur* (兔毫), fine radiating streaks like an animal's coat; *oil spot*, a scatter of silvery mirror-drops; and, in a vanishingly small number of surviving bowls, halos of iridescent blue that still defy easy explanation. Every bowl came out different. Emperor Huizong himself, in his treatise, pronounced the blue-black bowls with hare's-fur markings the most desirable — an imperial endorsement that did for Jian ware roughly what you would expect.

Hold that image: a dark, iron-glazed bowl, wide enough to whisk in, its glaze breaking into streaks where it thins at the rim. If you have ever seen a *tenmoku* tea bowl in a Japanese tea room or a museum case, you have seen this bowl's direct descendant — the Japanese name comes from the Tianmu mountains, where visiting monks encountered such bowls and carried them home. Centuries after the kilns of Jianyang fell quiet, their bowls were being treasured in Japan as objects beyond price. Several are today formally designated national treasures there. The country that made them had, by then, largely stopped making anything like them. Which brings us to the strange ending of this story.

## How a Whole Country Changed Its Mind

Dynasties end, and with them, sometimes, whole ways of doing things. The Song fell in 1279 to the Mongols, whose Yuan dynasty ruled China for nearly a century. Under the Yuan, the elaborate apparatus of Song tea culture — the tribute workshops, the contests, the connoisseurship of froth — lost its royal patron and much of its social base. Whisked tea did not vanish overnight; habits this deep never do. Simpler methods, including steeping whole leaves in hot water, had always existed alongside the aristocratic powder, especially outside the elite, and they gained ground.

The decisive stroke came early in the next dynasty, and unusually for cultural history, we can date it and name the man responsible. In 1391 the founding Ming emperor, Zhu Yuanzhang (朱元璋), issued an edict abolishing the tribute of caked tea to the court and requiring loose leaf instead. His stated logic was burden: he had risen from brutal peasant poverty, and the dragon-phoenix cake system — with its imperial workshops and its punishing demands on the growing regions — struck him as exactly the kind of extravagance his dynasty existed to correct. Whatever mixture of frugality and politics truly drove the decision, its effect was enormous. When the court stopped demanding pressed tea, the prestige economy built around it collapsed. Fashion followed the throne, as it always had.

Within a few generations, Chinese tea culture had reorganized itself around the loose leaf: whole dried leaves, steeped in hot water, then poured off. This was the era that gave the world the teapot as we know it — including the small unglazed clay pots of Yixing that steepers still treasure — and, in time, the great flowering of oolongs and other leaf teas that Chinese tea is famous for today. None of this was a decline; in most ways it was another golden age. But it was a golden age of *infusion*. Remember chapter one: an infusion borrows from the leaf and discards it. The powdered leaf, drunk whole — the very thing this book is about — simply fell out of Chinese life. By the middle of the Ming dynasty, writers were already describing the whisked tea of the Song as an antique curiosity. The grinding stopped, the whisks disappeared, and the black bowls became heirlooms of a game nobody played anymore.

A few cousins of the old way survived in the folds of the country — most famously *lei cha* (擂茶), "pounded tea," kept alive in Hakka and some other communities, in which tea leaves are ground in a bowl with herbs, seeds, and nuts into a savory drink. It is a wonderful thing, and it preserves the ancient instinct that a ground leaf can be food. But it is its own tradition, not a surviving thread of Song *dian cha*. Of the whisked bowl itself — the sifted powder, the staged pours, the standing froth — China kept, in the end, almost nothing but the literature and the bowls.

Here is the honest summary, and it is one of the odder facts in the history of food: the country that invented powdered whisked tea, perfected it, wrote its classics, built an imperial industry around it, and turned it into a national sport, gave it up — not because the tea failed, but because an emperor changed the paperwork and the culture moved on.

## The Practice That Left Home

So why are you able to buy a tin of stone-ground green tea powder and a bamboo whisk in the twenty-first century?

Because ideas migrate, and this one had already left home before the door closed. All through the Song centuries, Japanese Buddhist monks were making the dangerous sea crossing to China to study in its great monasteries — and in those monasteries, tea was not a pastime but part of the discipline: a medicine, an aid to long hours of meditation, a small ceremony of respect. The monks learned the monastic way of tea, which was the Song way of tea: powdered, whisked, taken from a bowl. Some of them, returning home, carried the habit with them — along with tea seeds, tea texts, and, in time, those dark bowls from the Tianmu mountains.

Japan, crucially, never got the memo about the Ming reforms — or rather, got it and felt no obligation to obey. Japanese tea culture had taken root in temples and among warriors and aristocrats as a *whisked* tradition, and when China pivoted to loose leaf, Japan simply carried on, deepening and refining the powdered way for centuries until it became something China had never quite made of it: not a competition, but a ceremony. The whisk that fell from Chinese hands was already resting in Japanese ones.

That transmission is not an abstraction. Tradition attaches it to real ships, real storms, and real people — above all to one monk, twice a passenger on the crossing to Song China, who came home in 1191 with tea seeds in his luggage and an unshakeable conviction that this bitter green powder was nothing less than medicine for a civilization. His name was Eisai, and he is where our story goes next.
