# Frozen

Open the freezer and take out the tub you made last week. Peel back the lid. There it is — the same green you sifted into the bowl seven days ago, bright and unbothered, as if no time had passed at all. Now think back to the two trays of cookies that opened chapter thirty-two: the proud green one, the khaki one, and the whole doctrine we built about defending color from heat. Here is the happy ending of that story. The freezer is the one place in your kitchen where matcha needs no defending. Cold does not knock the magnesium out of chlorophyll; it slows that chemistry almost to a stop. Cold does not evaporate the aromatics; it locks the door on them. Chapter twelve told you to keep the tin away from heat because heat is the thief — and a freezer is the opposite of an oven. Everything the storage chapter tried to protect, and everything the baking chapter mourned, survives below zero. These are, gram for gram of tea, some of the greenest and truest-tasting recipes in this book.

They are also — not coincidentally — the recipes that carried matcha to the world. Chapter five told the story: long before the latte conquered the cafes, it was matcha ice cream that walked the flavor out of the tearoom and into ordinary Japanese life. When you make the first recipe in this chapter, you are joining that lineage, in your own kitchen, with no machine.

But this book does not do happy endings without the honest footnote, so here it is, and it shapes every recipe that follows: **cold mutes taste.** This is ordinary sensory reality, not matcha chemistry — your tongue perceives sweetness and flavor less vividly in a very cold food than in the same food at room temperature, which is why melted ice cream tastes almost embarrassingly sweet and why a frozen dessert that tasted perfectly balanced as a warm base can come out of the freezer polite to the point of silence. The practical rule: **frozen recipes need a matcha dose that would taste too strong at room temperature.** When a base in this chapter tastes slightly louder and more bitter than you would want to eat with a spoon right now, it is correctly seasoned for the temperature at which you will actually eat it. Taste your base, aim for "a little too much," and trust the freezer to bring it back to just right.

One more honest note before the flagship, because you deserve to know what your equipment can and cannot do. Commercial ice cream is churned: frozen fast in a machine that scrapes and stirs continuously, which keeps ice crystals tiny and whips in a controlled amount of air. That equipment advantage is real, and no home method fully matches it — not the recipes here, and not the internet's either, whatever their titles promise. What the no-churn method below produces is genuinely excellent: smooth, scoopable, deeply green. It is a little denser and a little richer than churned ice cream, and after a week or two in a home freezer it will be a little icier than it was on day one. That is the honest trade, and once you know you are making it, you can stop comparing your tub to the cafe's and enjoy it for what it is — which is considerable.

### Matcha ice cream (no-churn)

*Makes about 1 liter (a standard loaf pan). Fifteen minutes of work, then at least six hours of freezer patience.*

This is the flagship of the chapter and one of the best effort-to-reward ratios in the entire book. Two ingredients do all the engineering, and it helps to know what each one is doing, because then the method stops being a trick and becomes obvious. An ice cream machine has two jobs: it beats air into the base so the result is light instead of a solid block, and it keeps the freezing mixture moving so large ice crystals cannot form. The no-churn method splits those jobs between the whisk and the can. Whipping the cream folds in the air *before* freezing instead of during it. And sweetened condensed milk is so concentrated in sugar that it lowers the freezing point of the whole mixture — sugar-dense water resists freezing solid, which is why the finished tub stays scoopable straight from the freezer instead of setting like a brick. Air from the whisk, softness from the sugar, fat from the cream keeping the texture plush: that is the entire machine, replaced.

**You need:**

- 10–14 g matcha (about 5–7 teaspoons) — 10 g is gentle, 12 g is the sweet spot for most tins, 14 g is for people who ordered koicha in chapter twenty-three and meant it
- 45 ml hot water, about 80°C (3 tablespoons)
- 1 can sweetened condensed milk, 397 g (about 1¼ cups)
- 480 ml cold whipping cream, at least 30% fat (2 cups)
- A pinch of fine salt

**Steps:**

1. Sift the matcha into a medium bowl — the chapter thirty-two rule applies below zero too; a clump that survives folding becomes a frozen capsule of bitterness. Add the hot water and work it into a completely smooth, glossy paste, exactly like the start of koicha: knead first, no speed, no dry pockets.
2. Whisk the condensed milk and the salt into the paste until the mixture is one even, deep green with no streaks. Taste it. It should read distinctly strong — sweeter and greener than you would want by the spoonful. Good; the cream will soften it and the cold will mute it.
3. In a large bowl, whip the cold cream to soft-to-medium peaks — the stage where the whisk leaves trails and a lifted peak folds gently over at the tip. Stop there.
4. Stir about a third of the whipped cream into the matcha mixture to loosen it, then add the rest and fold gently with a spatula — scrape down, turn over, rotate the bowl — until no white streaks remain.
5. Scrape into a loaf pan or a lidded container, press a piece of baking paper or plastic wrap directly onto the surface (air is still the enemy from chapter twelve, even here), lid or wrap the top, and freeze at least 6 hours, ideally overnight.
6. Before serving, give it 5–10 minutes on the counter. Scoop, and admire a green the oven could never have given you.

**The tricky part:** there are two, and they bracket the recipe. First, the paste. Matcha stirred dry into condensed milk does not disperse — the mixture is too thick to break clumps, and you will find them later, frozen and intact. The hot-water paste in step one is not optional; it is where the texture of the entire liter is decided. Second, the whipping. Cream taken past medium peaks turns grainy, and folding grainy cream into the base gives ice cream with a faintly buttery, broken texture that no amount of freezing repairs. If the cream starts looking stiff and matte rather than soft and glossy, stop immediately — slightly under-whipped folds in fine; over-whipped is on its way to butter.

**One variation:** fold less thoroughly on purpose. Add the final third of the cream in two or three lazy folds so ribbons of white marble through the green, then freeze as usual. Or reach back to the pairing list of chapter thirty-two and fold in a handful of chopped white chocolate, or spoonfuls of sweetened azuki, in the last few folds — both freeze beautifully and land exactly where matcha's bitterness wants company.

### Matcha kakigōri syrup

*Makes about 180 ml of syrup — enough for 4 to 6 servings. Ten minutes, plus cooling.*

Kakigōri (かき氷) is Japanese shaved ice: snow-fine ribbons of ice shaved from a solid block, mounded high, and doused in syrup. Crowned with matcha syrup, condensed milk, and often azuki, it becomes the classic called Uji kintoki (宇治金時) — named for the tea town from chapter eleven, and one of the great hot-weather desserts of the world. Here is the honest part before you get excited: the defining texture comes from a hand-cranked or electric ice shaver, and most home kitchens — probably including yours — do not own one. This book will not pretend a blender produces shaved ice; it produces crushed ice, which is coarser, crunchier, and still entirely worth pouring syrup over on a hot afternoon. So the recipe here is the part that actually carries the flavor and keeps for a week: the syrup. Make it once and you have a bottle of intense, sweet, bitter green that also earns its keep over vanilla ice cream, stirred into cold milk, or drizzled on the pancakes coming in chapter thirty-seven.

**You need:**

- 100 g sugar (½ cup)
- 100 ml water (scant ½ cup), plus 2 tablespoons (30 ml) cool water for the paste
- 8 g matcha (about 4 teaspoons) — syrup gets diluted by melting ice, so it must start loud
- To serve: plenty of ice, and sweetened condensed milk for drizzling

**Steps:**

1. Heat the sugar and the 100 ml of water in a small pan, stirring, just until the sugar fully dissolves. Take it off the heat and let it cool for a few minutes — warm is fine, steaming hot is not, for the chapter thirty-two reason: matcha whisked into near-boiling liquid gives up color and aroma for nothing.
2. Sift the matcha into a bowl, add the 2 tablespoons of cool water, and work it into a smooth, glossy paste.
3. Whisk the warm syrup into the paste in two or three additions until completely uniform. Cool fully, then bottle or jar it. It keeps about a week in the fridge; stir or shake before each use, because honest matcha settles — suspension, not solution, as ever.
4. To serve, crush ice as fine as your equipment allows — a blender's ice-crush setting, or cubes in a sturdy zip bag under a rolling pin — and pack it into a chilled bowl in a proud mound.
5. Spoon syrup generously over the mound — 2 to 3 tablespoons per serving — letting it streak down the sides, then finish with a slow drizzle of condensed milk. Eat immediately, with a small spoon and no dignity.

**The tricky part:** dilution. This dessert is mostly water, and every spoonful of ice that melts under the syrup waters the flavor down further — which is why the syrup is deliberately stronger and sweeter than seems reasonable when you taste it from the spoon. Resist the urge to thin it or cut the sugar; a "balanced" syrup produces kakigōri that tastes like faintly green water by the third bite. The mound of ice is the dilution. The syrup is the argument. Both have to be loud for the dessert to speak at a normal volume.

**One variation:** borrow the machine. Snow-cone stands, bingsu cafes, and supermarket freezer aisles in much of Asia offer finely shaved ice or shaved-ice makers cheaply — and a bag of store shaved ice with your own matcha syrup and condensed milk from home is a genuinely great use of both. Add a spoonful of sweetened azuki between layers of ice and you have built an honest Uji kintoki, whoever shaved the snow.

### Matcha frozen yogurt

*Makes about 750 ml. Fifteen minutes of work, then an afternoon of freezing with occasional stirring.*

After two recipes built on cream and condensed milk, this is the tangy counterpoint: the lactic sharpness of yogurt does for frozen matcha what lemon zest did for the bakes — cuts through the richness and makes the green taste brighter. Now the honesty that this recipe demands more than any other in the chapter: **homemade frozen yogurt is icier than commercial frozen yogurt, full stop.** Commercial fro-yo leans on churning equipment, stabilizers, and carefully engineered sugar blends; yogurt is mostly water, and water freezing slowly in a home freezer grows crunchy crystals. You cannot delete that gap, but every choice below narrows it, and it helps to see them as a system: **fat** (strained yogurt and a little cream) interrupts crystal growth; **sugar** depresses the freezing point; and a spoonful of **corn syrup or honey** adds sugars that resist crystallizing even better than plain sucrose, gram for gram. Skim yogurt, less sugar, and no syrup will give you a green ice block. The recipe as written gives you something scoopable after ten minutes on the counter — softer than the cafe's, sharper, and greener.

**You need:**

- 500 g plain strained (Greek-style) yogurt, whole-milk, cold (2 cups)
- 120 ml whipping cream (½ cup)
- 100 g sugar (½ cup)
- 2 tablespoons corn syrup or mild honey (about 40 g)
- 8–10 g matcha (about 4–5 teaspoons)
- 30 ml hot water (2 tablespoons)
- A pinch of fine salt

**Steps:**

1. Sift the matcha into a large bowl, add the hot water, and work it into a smooth paste.
2. Add the sugar, corn syrup or honey, and salt to the paste and stir; the sugar will loosen it into a thick green slurry.
3. Whisk in the cream, then the yogurt, until completely uniform. Taste — it should be noticeably tangy, sweet, and a touch too strong on the matcha, which means it is right.
4. Chill the base for 30 minutes, then pour it into a wide, shallow container — more surface area freezes faster, and faster freezing means finer crystals.
5. Freeze, and for the first 3 hours or so, take the container out every 45 minutes and beat the mixture vigorously with a fork or sturdy whisk, scraping the frozen edges into the middle. This is you, doing the churning machine's job by hand — three or four rounds of it makes a real difference.
6. After the final stir, smooth the top, press wrap onto the surface, and freeze until firm. Rest it 10 minutes at room temperature before scooping.

**The tricky part:** honoring step five. It is dull, it interrupts your afternoon, and it is the single biggest lever you have on the final texture — every stir breaks growing ice crystals into smaller ones. Set a timer, because a base left unstirred for the whole freeze is exactly the ice block this recipe was engineered to avoid. If you do own an ice cream machine, this base churns beautifully and you may skip the ritual entirely; the book just refuses to assume you have one.

**One variation:** frozen yogurt bark. Spread the just-mixed base about 1 cm deep on a paper-lined tray, scatter with granola-to-be from chapter thirty-seven (or chopped white chocolate and freeze-dried strawberries), freeze solid, and snap into shards. Bark forgives iciness completely — thin pieces eaten by hand are supposed to shatter — which makes it the most beginner-proof recipe in this chapter.

### Matcha ice pops

*Makes about 6 pops, depending on your molds (roughly 550 ml of base). Ten minutes, then frozen overnight.*

The easiest recipe in the chapter, and the one to make with the least ceremony: no whipping, no stirring schedule, no shaver envy. Ice pops are also where iciness stops being a flaw — a pop is *meant* to be an ice, eaten slowly off a stick in the sun — so the engineering anxiety of the last recipe simply does not apply. This version descends directly from the coconut iced matcha of chapter twenty-five, because coconut milk's fat and gentle sweetness do in a mold exactly what they did in the glass: cushion the tea and carry the green. The one rule that still matters is sugar — not only for taste but because a completely unsweetened pop freezes tooth-crackingly hard. Keep the sugar, and remember the chapter's law one final time: the base should taste a little too strong and a little too sweet at room temperature, because it will be eaten at minus eighteen.

**You need:**

- 7 g matcha (about 3½ teaspoons)
- 60 ml hot water (¼ cup)
- 350 ml coconut milk — the drinking kind, or canned shaken until uniform and thinned with a splash of water (1½ cups)
- 100 ml cold water (scant ½ cup)
- 50 g sugar (¼ cup), or 3 tablespoons condensed milk
- A pinch of fine salt
- Ice pop molds and sticks

**Steps:**

1. Sift the matcha into a jug, add the hot water, and stir to a smooth paste; stir in the sugar and salt while the mixture is still warm so they dissolve completely.
2. Whisk in the coconut milk and the cold water until even. Taste and adjust toward slightly-too-strong.
3. Pour into molds, leaving about half a centimeter of headroom — the base expands as it freezes and an overfilled mold pushes its own stick out.
4. Insert sticks and freeze at least 6 hours, preferably overnight.
5. To release, run the outside of the mold under warm tap water for ten to twenty seconds and pull gently. Never lever the stick sideways; that is how sticks come out alone.

**The tricky part:** settling. Matcha is a suspension, and in the hour before the base freezes solid, gravity is still working — pour and walk away, and the powder drifts downward, giving pops with a pale tip and an intense, slightly gritty base. Two defenses: make the paste properly (well-dispersed matcha settles far more slowly than half-dispersed matcha), and give each mold a gentle stir with the stick after the first 45 minutes in the freezer, when the base has thickened enough to hold the powder where it is.

**One variation:** layer them. Split the recipe into a green base (as written, with the full matcha) and a white one (plain sweetened coconut milk). Pour a white layer, freeze 45 minutes until slushy-firm, add a green layer, freeze again, and repeat — two or three bands each. It doubles the freezer time and the visual reward: striped green-and-white pops that look like far more work than they are. Insert the sticks after the second layer, when there is enough slush to hold them straight.

Look at what this part of the book has quietly done. In chapter thirty-two, matcha needed defending — from heat, from time, from acid. By chapter thirty-five it needed only a refrigerator. In this chapter, the coldest room in your kitchen turned out to be the leaf's best friend, and the only honest concessions left were about your tongue and your equipment, not about the tea. There is one chapter of recipes to go, and it makes the biggest move of all: out of dessert entirely. Ice cream is an occasion; the next chapter is about mornings — pancakes, oats, granola, the recipes you make half-awake on a Tuesday — because the real sign that matcha has moved in is not the tub in the freezer. It is the green streak in your breakfast, so ordinary you stop noticing it. Let us make it a habit.
