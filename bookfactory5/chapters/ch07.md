# Three Weeks in the Dark

There is a morning, somewhere in the first stretch of spring, when a tea field disappears.

It happens early, before the day warms. The farmers arrive with bundles of black netting, or — on a shrinking number of old farms — with reed screens and loose rice straw, and they begin to cover the rows. On a modern farm the work goes quickly: cloth is drawn over frames that stand above the bushes like the skeleton of a greenhouse, edges are pinned and tied, gaps are closed. By mid-morning, a field that yesterday sat green and open under the sky has become a long, low, dark tent. From the road it looks like the tea has been put to bed. In a sense, it has.

Step under the canopy — farmers do, constantly, to check on their crop — and the world changes. The light is dim and green-tinged, the air a little stiller and more humid than outside. Sounds soften. Above your head, sun filters through mesh or straw in small moving flecks, the way it does through a forest canopy. The bushes stand in rows in this half-light, and for the next several weeks, this is the only sky they will know.

Everything that makes matcha *matcha* — the improbable color, the sweetness, the savory depth, and a good part of the price — is decided in here, in the dark. Chapter Six ended with the family tree of tea and a gate called tencha; this chapter is about the strangest thing on the far side of that gate. Farmers spend all year coaxing these plants toward the sun, feeding them, pruning them, protecting them from frost. And then, in the final weeks before the most important harvest of the year, they deliberately take the sun away.

To understand why, you have to know what a tea plant does when you do that to it. But first, look up at the roof.

## The architecture of shade

There are two basic ways to put a tea field in the dark, and the difference between them is the difference between a blanket and a room.

The simpler way is direct covering — *jikagise* (直掛け) — in which the shading material is laid straight onto the bushes themselves, resting on the leaves like a blanket on a sleeper. It is fast, it is cheap, it needs no construction, and for lighter, shorter shading it works well. Its drawback is contact: the cloth touches the growing leaves, presses on them, rubs them in the wind, and traps heat against them on warm days. For the deepest, longest shading, most growers want something better.

The better thing is the shelf — *tana* (棚). Poles are set through the field and a horizontal frame is built above the bushes, high enough to walk beneath. The shading material goes on top of this frame, and sometimes down the sides as well, so the tea ends up inside a large, dim room with a mesh ceiling. Nothing touches the leaves. Air moves. Workers can go in and out to inspect the crop, adjust the covers, and eventually to pick. When you see photographs of famous shaded fields in places like Uji — long black pavilions rippling over the hillsides — you are looking at tana shading. It costs far more, in materials and in labor, and that cost is one of the honest reasons the teas that come out of those pavilions cost what they do.

What actually goes on top of the frame has changed more in the last century than in the several before it.

The traditional roof is the *honzu* (本簀) canopy: screens woven from reeds — the same *yoshizu* (葦簀) screens that once shaded Japanese verandas — laid across the frame, with rice straw spread loosely over them. The straw is the adjustable part. In the early days of shading, a thin layer lets a good deal of dappled light through; as harvest approaches, workers scatter more straw across the reeds, and the field sinks deeper into darkness by degrees. It is a beautiful system, and by most accounts a gentle one: the natural materials breathe, shed heat, and scatter light softly. It is also enormously laborious. The screens must be woven, maintained, hauled out, and laid by hand; the straw must be grown, stored, and spread; and at the end of the season the whole thing comes down again. Honzu shading survives today on a small number of farms, mostly in old growing districts, mostly for the very top of the crop. Some regard the tea it produces as subtly finer. Others will tell you, honestly, that the method survives partly because heritage itself has value, and partly because the best fields and the most careful farmers tend to be the ones still doing it — which makes it hard to say how much is the straw and how much is everything else. Nobody has fully untangled that, and this book will not pretend to.

The modern roof is *kanreisha* (寒冷紗) — synthetic shade cloth, usually black, woven to block a stated fraction of light. It rolls out, ties down, lasts for years, and can be layered: one sheet for moderate shade, a second sheet on top when it is time to go darker. The overwhelming majority of shaded tea in Japan now grows under synthetic cloth, on shelf frames or laid directly, and it would be romantic nonsense to sneer at it. Good cloth, used well, grows superb tea. What was lost in poetry was gained in control: a farmer can now choose shade the way a photographer chooses an exposure.

However the roof is built, the schedule underneath it follows the same broad logic. For the teas that define shading — gyokuro (玉露) among leaf teas, and tencha destined for matcha — the covers go on while the spring's new shoots are still young, and stay on for somewhere in the region of three to four weeks before harvest, sometimes a little less, sometimes longer for particular styles. The shade often deepens in stages: moderately dark to begin, then darker — under honzu, more straw; under cloth, another layer — until, in the final stretch, only a small fraction of daylight reaches the leaves. I am keeping these figures deliberately loose, and later in this chapter I will tell you exactly why.

## What the plant does in the dark

A tea bush is not a passive victim of all this. It responds — and the response is the entire point.

Start with what a leaf is for. A leaf is a solar panel: its job is to catch light and turn it into the sugars the plant lives on, using the green pigment chlorophyll as the light-catching machinery. Put a plant in deep shade and you have created a problem: the same work must now be done with a fraction of the light. The plant's answer is the same one you would reach for if your radio signal grew faint — turn up the antenna. It packs its leaves with extra chlorophyll, straining to harvest every photon that gets through the cloth. New leaves grown in shade also tend to stretch: broader, thinner, more delicate than sun-grown leaves, spreading themselves wide to catch what little light there is.

Hold that image, because you have seen its result. The almost unreasonable green of good matcha — the color from this book's first page — is not a trick of processing and certainly not a dye. It is the concentrated chlorophyll of leaves that spent their last weeks desperately tuned for darkness. The shade is the color.

The second change is the one you can taste, and it begins in the roots. All through the cold months, a tea plant stockpiles an amino acid called L-theanine, made in the roots and sent up into the young shoots in spring. Theanine is, for our purposes, the taste of shaded tea: it reads to the tongue as sweetness and as *umami* — that savory, brothy, faintly marine depth that Japanese cuisine prizes and that good matcha has in abundance. A young spring shoot arrives loaded with it.

Then sunlight interferes. In a leaf growing under full sun, theanine does not simply sit there; the plant breaks it down and uses it as raw material for building another family of compounds — the catechins. Catechins are the famous polyphenols of green tea, the ones the health headlines love, and we will give them a fair hearing in Part Three. But to your tongue, catechins are mainly one thing: bitterness and astringency, the puckering grip of a strong, sun-grown green tea. This conversion is light-driven. More sun, more theanine converted, more catechins: a brisker, more bracing, more bitter leaf.

Now take the sun away, and the conversion slows. The theanine that the roots sent up has nowhere to go; it stays theanine. The catechins that would have been built never fully arrive. Weigh the shaded leaf against its sun-grown neighbor and the ledger is simple: more chlorophyll, more retained theanine, fewer catechins.

Taste the ledger and it becomes the entire flavor argument of matcha: **less bitter, more savory, more sweet, and greener** — all four from one intervention. Shading does not add anything to the leaf. No flavoring, no treatment, no trick. It is closer to sculpture than to seasoning: the farmer removes light, and the removal shapes what the plant builds. Every expensive-sounding word on a matcha tin — the umami, the sweetness, the "oceanic" depth, the color that photographs like paint — traces back to a tarp and three or four weeks of patience.

One caution before we move on, because this is the kind of place where tidy stories outrun the truth. The picture above — chlorophyll up, theanine retained, catechins down — is well-established, repeatable, and you can verify it with your own tongue by tasting a sencha and a gyokuro side by side. But the leaf is not a machine with three dials. Shade shifts many things at once — aroma compounds, texture, the balance among dozens of substances — and how those shifts play out depends on the plant variety, the field, the season, and the farmer's choices. The headline chemistry is honest. Just do not mistake it for the whole book of what happens under the cloth.

## Degrees of darkness

Shade is not a switch; it is a dial, and where a farmer sets the dial defines whole categories of Japanese tea.

Set it modestly — cover the field for roughly a week to two weeks, blocking perhaps half the light or somewhat more — and you get *kabusecha* (かぶせ茶), "covered tea." The name comes from *kabuseru*, to drape over, and the tea sits exactly where its process sits: between worlds. Kabusecha is made like sencha but shaded briefly, and it tastes like the compromise it is — in the best sense. Some of sencha's brightness and briskness, some of gyokuro's sweetness and depth, at a price closer to the former than the latter. If you ever want to taste what shading *does*, in isolation, a decent kabusecha next to a decent sencha is the clearest lesson money can buy. This lighter regime is often done with cloth laid directly on the bushes — the blanket, not the room — because a short, moderate shading does not repay the cost of building shelves.

Turn the dial all the way — three to four weeks or so, with the shade deepening in stages until, by the end, the great majority of daylight is shut out — and you are in the territory of gyokuro and tencha. This is full shading, the regime the shelf structures and the honzu canopies were built for. Under deep shade the plant's responses run to their extremes: maximum chlorophyll, maximum retained theanine, leaves broad and thin and almost luminously green. Gyokuro takes such leaves and rolls them into needles for infusion; tencha — as we saw at the gate in the last chapter — steams them, dries them flat, strips them to leaf flesh, and sends them toward the stone mill. Same darkness, two destinies.

Between and around these two poles lies every intermediate a farmer might choose, because shading is ultimately a recipe with several ingredients: how long, how dark, in how many stages, laid directly or built overhead, straw or cloth. Different regions lean on different customs; different target grades call for different settings; different springs — warm ones, cold ones, wet ones — force adjustments on the fly. A farmer watching a slow, chilly spring may shorten or delay shading rather than stress young shoots that are already struggling; a warm early spring may allow a longer, deeper run. The dial is set by judgment, every year, per field.

Which is why you should treat one common style of matcha marketing with polite suspicion. A tin or a website that proclaims "shaded for exactly 21 days under 90 percent shade" is offering you the *feel* of precision. Perhaps that number is real for that farm in that year. But there is no universal formula — no council that decrees the correct days and percentages for matcha — and the honest general statement is a range: light shading around one to two weeks for kabusecha; roughly three to four weeks, deepening in stages toward heavy darkness, for gyokuro and tencha; details varying by farm, cultivar, region, season, and the grade the farmer is aiming for. Anyone who tells you the One True Number is selling certainty they do not have. The farmers themselves, notably, tend to talk in ranges.

## What the darkness costs

If shading were free, every tea field in the world would be covered and this chapter would be short. It is not free. It is one of the most expensive things you can do to a tea field, and the bill is paid three times over.

The plant pays first. Deep shade is, from the bush's point of view, a slow crisis. It is running its solar economy on a fraction of its normal income, spending stored reserves to push out those broad, tender, chlorophyll-crammed leaves. A heavily shaded plant emerges from harvest depleted in a way a sun-grown plant does not, and farmers must manage the aftermath: careful feeding, careful pruning, recovery time. Many fields that produce heavily shaded spring tea are asked for little or nothing else of consequence for the rest of the year — where a sencha field might yield multiple flushes, the great shaded fields largely rest after their one exhausting performance. Practices differ farm to farm, but the direction is constant: shading trades the plant's yearly vigor for a few weeks of extraordinary leaf.

The field pays second, in yield. Shaded shoots are grown for tenderness, picked young and picked selectively, from plants whose output the shading itself has suppressed. Less light in, less leaf out — there is no way around the arithmetic. A kilogram of finished tencha represents a disproportionate bite of a field's annual capacity even before the de-stemming and de-veining of the last chapter throw part of the harvest away.

And the farmer pays third, in labor and in nerve. The structures must be built, maintained, covered, adjusted, and struck. Under honzu, add the weaving and the straw-spreading — shading a single field the old way consumes an amount of skilled handwork that modern cloth was invented specifically to escape. Under cloth, the burden is lighter but real: layers on, layers off, edges secured against spring winds, temperatures watched, because a covered field on an unseasonably hot day can cook its own crop, and a late frost can threaten shoots the farmer has already spent a season's money to grow. For those final weeks the farmer is running a long, dark greenhouse with the year's income inside it.

Add the three bills together and you have most of the honest answer to a question every matcha drinker eventually asks: *why does this small tin cost so much?* Not mystique. Not ceremony. A field deliberately made less productive, a plant deliberately stressed, and weeks of skilled labor spent building and tending darkness — all before a single leaf is picked, and long before the slow mills of Chapter Nine do their part. When you pay for good matcha, the largest thing you are paying for is a farmer's willingness to make less tea, more carefully, on purpose.

That is also why true shading is where cut corners hide. Powder from unshaded or barely shaded leaf can be ground fine and sold in a green tin, and the label will rarely confess. But the leaf cannot fake what it never built: the color runs olive instead of vivid, the umami is missing, and the bitterness that shade would have prevented comes through in the cup. You now know enough chemistry to understand exactly what such a powder is: a leaf that was never asked to turn up its antenna, never allowed to keep its theanine. The dark weeks are not a flourish on top of matcha making. They are the substance of it.

## The opened sky

The three or four weeks pass. Under the canopy the shoots have grown long and languid, their leaves wide, thin, and a green so deep it looks almost blue in the gloom. The farmer has been in and out for days, lifting leaves, judging tenderness, watching the weather with the particular tension of someone whose whole year is about to fit into a very short window.

Then one morning the covers come off — or the pickers come in under them — and everything changes tempo. Shading is patient work: weeks of waiting, small adjustments, slow chemistry. What follows is its opposite. The tender, shade-built leaf that took a season of feeding and a month of darkness to create begins to change from the moment it is plucked, and the clock that starts then is measured not in weeks but in hours. The leaves must come off the bush at exactly the right youth, reach the steamer before the day is out, and pass through their brief storm of heat and drying into the flat, brittle stillness of tencha.

The farmers spent three weeks slowing the plant down. Now comes the day when nobody is allowed to be slow.
