# Seasonal Specials

In Chapter 4 you knelt in a room where the calendar was part of the furniture. In the cold months, the tea was heated over a sunken hearth — the *ro* (炉) — cut into the floor close to the guests so its warmth could reach them. In the warm months that hearth was closed and a portable brazier, the *furo* (風炉), stood farther away, keeping its heat politely to itself. The flowers were that morning's flowers. The sweets changed with the month — a soft pink one shaped like a cherry blossom in April, something translucent and cool-looking in July, a chestnut sweet in October. A tea gathering in early spring and one in late autumn were not the same event performed twice. They were different events, on purpose, because the day itself was considered an ingredient.

You do not need a sunken hearth to steal that idea. All you need is the willingness to let the time of year change what goes in the bowl — and this chapter is five ways to do exactly that. Let me be clear about what these recipes are, because honesty matters more in a "seasonal" chapter than almost anywhere else: these are modern, cafe-style drinks, not ancient traditions. Nobody in a sixteenth-century tearoom blended watermelon. What is traditional is the instinct behind them — the idea that a drink can answer the specific day on which it is made — and I think the old tea masters would recognize that instinct even if the drinks themselves would startle them.

One more thing before the recipes, for readers who, like me, live where the weather refuses to cooperate. I write this from Malaysia, where the year has two seasons — hot, and hot with rain — and "autumn" is something that happens on other people's Instagram. It took me an embarrassingly long time to realize this does not disqualify tropical readers from seasonal drinking. Our seasons are simply festivals instead of weather: Chinese New Year, Raya, Deepavali, Christmas, the durian months, the school holidays. A calendar of celebrations marks time just as honestly as a calendar of falling leaves. So take the four weather-seasons below as a template, not a rule — and note that the fifth recipe runs on the festival calendar, because that is the one my own kitchen actually follows.

### Sakura-Matcha Latte

*Spring. Serves one. Takes about ten minutes, most of it soaking.*

Every spring, Japanese convenience stores, cafes, and half the world's coffee chains turn pink, and the sakura latte appears — a thoroughly modern invention riding on a genuinely old ingredient. Salted cherry blossoms, *sakura no shiozuke* (桜の塩漬け), are real: blossoms pickled in salt and plum vinegar, traditionally floated in hot water as a celebration tea at weddings. The blossoms are old; putting them in a matcha latte is new, and I will not pretend otherwise. What the blossom brings is strange and lovely — a faint cherry-almond perfume and a whisper of salt that makes the matcha taste sweeter by contrast, the same trick as salted caramel.

**You need:**

- 3–4 salted cherry blossoms (from a Japanese grocer or online — a small jar lasts for years)
- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 50 ml hot water at 70–80°C (about 3½ tablespoons / a scant ¼ cup)
- 180 ml milk of your choice (¾ cup)
- ½–1 teaspoon honey or sugar, to taste

**Steps:**

1. Put the blossoms in a small bowl of cool water and soak for 10 minutes to draw out the excess salt. Change the water once. Fish them out and set the prettiest one aside for the top.
2. While they soak, warm the milk to steaming — not boiling — and sweeten it lightly. Sakura and scalded milk do not get along; keep it gentle.
3. Chop the remaining soaked blossoms very finely, almost to a paste, and stir them into the warm milk. Let it sit for 2–3 minutes so the perfume moves into the milk.
4. Sift the matcha into a bowl, add the hot water, and whisk to a fine foam exactly as in Chapter 23.
5. Pour the sakura milk into a cup, pour the matcha over it, and float your reserved blossom on the foam. It will open slowly on the surface like it is remembering April.

**The tricky part:** the salt. Under-soaked blossoms make the latte taste like a mistake; fully de-salted ones taste of nothing. Aim to keep a ghost of saltiness — taste a soaked petal, and if you flinch, soak five more minutes. If you cannot find salted blossoms at all, here is the honest substitute: ⅛ teaspoon of rose water plus one small pinch of salt in the milk. It is not sakura — rose is louder and rounder — but it recreates the *idea*, floral-plus-salt-plus-green, and it costs almost nothing to try. I would rather you drink an honest imitation than pay a scalper for blossoms in May.

**One variation:** a white sakura latte for people who find blossom-plus-matcha too busy — leave the matcha out entirely, use the sakura milk over ice with a spoon of condensed milk, and serve the matcha as a plain small usucha alongside. Two quiet drinks instead of one crowded one.

### Watermelon Matcha Cooler

*Summer. Serves one tall glass. Ten minutes, plus chilling the fruit.*

This is the drink I make when the afternoon heat gets serious, and it is built on a fact cafes discovered in recent years: watermelon and matcha are unreasonably good together. The fruit is all sweetness and water with almost no acidity, so it cushions matcha's bitterness without fighting it, and the colors — deep pink below, green above — do half the work of making you feel cooler before you take a sip. This is pure modern cafe engineering. It is also, in thirty-something-degree weather, close to perfect.

**You need:**

- 300 g cold seedless watermelon flesh, cubed (about 2 cups)
- A squeeze of lime — about 1 teaspoon
- A very small pinch of salt
- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 60 ml cold water (¼ cup)
- Ice cubes to fill the glass

**Steps:**

1. Chill the watermelon thoroughly first — an hour in the fridge, or 20 minutes in the freezer. A lukewarm cooler is a contradiction.
2. Blend the watermelon with the lime and salt until completely smooth, about 30 seconds. No sugar; ripe watermelon needs none, and the salt makes it taste more like itself.
3. Pour the watermelon into a tall glass, leaving about a quarter of the glass free. Add ice until the pink rises near the top.
4. Cold-whisk the matcha: sift it into a bowl, add the cold water, and whisk about twice as long as usual — 30–40 seconds — until smooth and lightly foamy (this is the cold technique from Chapter 23's variation).
5. Pour the matcha slowly over the back of a spoon held just above the ice, so it settles as a green layer on the pink. Serve with a straw and instructions: admire first, then stir.

**The tricky part:** the layer. Watermelon juice is only slightly denser than matcha-water, so the separation is delicate — pour fast and you get an even murk (still delicious, no longer beautiful). The spoon trick works, but the real secret is the ice: the cubes break the fall of the matcha and let it rest on top. No ice, no layers. And drink it within ten minutes; blended watermelon separates into juice and foam as it stands, politely but firmly.

**One variation:** freeze the blended watermelon in an ice-cube tray, then blend the frozen cubes with a splash of water into a slush and pour the cold matcha over that — a watermelon-matcha granita in a glass, for days when heat is not negotiable.

### Roasted Sweet Potato Matcha

*Autumn. Serves one, warmly. Fifteen minutes if the potato is already roasted.*

This one sounds strange until the first sip, and then it sounds inevitable. Across Japan and Korea, autumn officially begins when the roasted sweet potato appears — sold from carts and truck speakers in Japan as *yaki-imo* (焼き芋), piled in paper bags in Korea as *gun-goguma*, and in both places the dense, honeyed, chestnut-like varieties are the point: Japanese satsumaimo or Korean sweet potatoes, with pale flesh that roasts to something close to caramel. Korean cafes worked out years ago that this flesh, blended into warm milk, makes a drink like liquid custard — the goguma latte — and adding matcha to it is the small, modern, slightly rebellious step this recipe takes. Earthy-sweet below, grassy-bitter above. It should not work. It works.

**You need:**

- 100 g roasted Japanese or Korean sweet potato flesh, skin removed (about ⅓ cup, packed — half a small potato)
- 200 ml milk of your choice (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon); oat milk is particularly good here
- 1–2 teaspoons honey or brown sugar, to taste (the potato may need none)
- A tiny pinch of cinnamon — optional, and keep it tiny
- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 50 ml hot water at 70–80°C (3½ tablespoons)

**Steps:**

1. Roast the potato ahead: whole and unwrapped at 200°C (400°F) for 60–75 minutes, until it wrinkles, weeps a little syrup at the ends, and gives no resistance to a skewer. Slow roasting is what converts its starch to sugar; a microwave cooks it but never sweetens it properly. Roast two or three on a lazy Sunday — they keep for days and reheat beautifully.
2. Blend the warm flesh with the milk until completely smooth, about 45 seconds. Taste before sweetening; a well-roasted potato often needs nothing.
3. Warm the blend gently in a small pot, stirring, until steaming. It will be thick — closer to drinkable custard than to milk. Thin with a splash more milk if you prefer. Add the cinnamon here, if using.
4. Pour into a wide cup or small bowl. Sift the matcha, whisk it with the hot water into a foamy usucha, and pour it gently over the top.
5. Serve with a spoon, not a straw. The first sips are green and bracing, the last are pure roasted sweetness — drink it in that order, like a meal that saves dessert for the end.

**The tricky part:** the potato itself. An ordinary orange supermarket sweet potato, boiled, makes a thin, vegetal drink — this recipe genuinely depends on the dense honeyed varieties and the long roast. If you cannot find satsumaimo or Korean sweet potatoes, look for any variety sold as "chestnut" or "honey" sweet potato; failing that, roast what you have an extra fifteen minutes and add a half-teaspoon more honey, and accept a rougher draft of the same idea.

**One variation:** iced, which the Koreans also worked out first — blend the roasted flesh with cold milk and a few ice cubes into a thick shake, and pour cold-whisked matcha over the top. Autumn afternoons in the tropics run to thirty-three degrees; this version exists for them.

### Peppermint Matcha

*Winter. Serves one. Ten minutes with fresh mint, two with extract.*

I resisted this drink for a long time, because mint-plus-green-tea sounded like toothpaste-plus-lawn. What changed my mind was making it with actual mint leaves instead of a syrup: steeped gently in milk, fresh mint is not a candy-cane flavor at all but a cool, leafy one — much closer to the mint in a Moroccan tea than the mint in a mocha — and it sits beside matcha like a slightly chilly cousin. This is a winter drink by the logic of the peppermint season the coffee chains invented, and I am comfortable borrowing their calendar as long as we improve on their execution.

**You need:**

- 10–12 fresh mint leaves, or 1 drop — one, seriously — of food-grade peppermint extract
- 200 ml milk of your choice (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon)
- 1 teaspoon honey or sugar, to taste
- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 50 ml hot water at 70–80°C (3½ tablespoons)

**Steps:**

1. If using fresh mint: rinse the leaves, then slap them once between your palms — it sounds theatrical, but bruising releases the oils. Drop them into the milk in a small pot.
2. Warm the milk to steaming, then take it off the heat, cover, and let the mint steep for 5–7 minutes. Taste at 5. When it is clearly minty but still tastes like milk, strain the leaves out and stir in the honey.
3. If using extract instead: warm and sweeten the milk, take it off the heat, and add one drop. Stir and taste before even thinking about a second.
4. Sift the matcha, whisk with the hot water into a foamy usucha, and pour it over the warm mint milk.
5. If it is genuinely cold where you are, drink it wrapped in both hands. If it is not, see the variation.

**The tricky part:** the dose, and this warning is the whole recipe. Peppermint extract is shockingly concentrated — the step from "refreshing" to "drinking mouthwash" is a single extra drop, and there is no undo; a second drop can only be fixed with a second cup of milk. Add it off the heat, from the tip of a skewer if your bottle pours generously, and never straight from the bottle over the pot. Use only food-grade extract from the baking aisle — peppermint essential oil is not the same thing, is far stronger, and is not sold for drinking. Fresh mint, by contrast, is nearly impossible to overdose; it is the beginner's route for a reason.

**One variation:** iced, for tropical Decembers — steep the mint in the milk as above, chill it completely, pour over ice, and top with cold-whisked matcha. Mint's coolness reads twice as loud in a cold drink, so eight leaves are plenty.

### Chinese New Year Red Date Matcha

*Festival season. Serves two — this one is for sharing. Thirty minutes, mostly simmering.*

This recipe runs on my calendar, not Japan's. In the weeks around Chinese New Year, every Malaysian-Chinese household I know accumulates red dates — *hongzao* (红枣), also called jujubes — because red date tea is what you simmer for visiting relatives: sweet, warming, auspiciously red, and gentle enough for grandmothers and children at the same table. One January it occurred to me that the deep caramel-honey flavor of long-simmered dates might hold up a layer of matcha the way brown sugar holds up milk in a dalgona drink. It does, and better than that: the pairing tastes like the reunion-dinner table and the tea counter agreeing on something. To be plain, in the spirit of this book: red date tea is genuinely traditional; putting matcha over it is my kitchen's invention, and yours now too.

**You need:**

- 8 dried red dates (jujubes), about 40 g — the wrinkly kind from any Chinese grocer or supermarket
- 400 ml water (1⅔ cups)
- 1 thin slice of ginger — optional but traditional in the tea
- 1 teaspoon honey or rock sugar, only if needed
- 150 ml milk of your choice (⅔ cup), warm or cold
- 2 g matcha (about 1 heaped half-teaspoon)
- 50 ml water for whisking, hot or cold (3½ tablespoons)

**Steps:**

1. Rinse the dates, slit each one open with a knife, and flick out the pits — an opened date gives the water everything it has; a whole one keeps secrets.
2. Simmer the dates (and ginger, if using) in the 400 ml of water, lid ajar, for 20–25 minutes, until the liquid has reduced by about half and turned a deep amber-red. Press the softened dates against the pot with a spoon as it reduces; you are making something between a tea and a thin syrup.
3. Strain, pressing well. Taste — good dates often need no added sugar at all. You want it noticeably sweet, because it is the sweet floor of a layered drink. You should have roughly 150–200 ml.
4. Divide the warm date tea between two glasses, then pour the milk slowly over a spoon to form a paler middle layer.
5. Whisk the matcha with its 50 ml of water — hot for a warming version, cold over ice for the version that suits a Malaysian January — and float it on top, again over the spoon. Three layers: auspicious red-amber, cream, green. Stir before drinking, ideally in front of the relative you made it for.

**The tricky part:** the reduction. Stop the simmer too early and the date layer is watery — too weak in flavor and too thin in body to hold the milk up, so the layers collapse into a beige puddle. The tea is ready when it coats a spoon very lightly and tastes almost too sweet on its own. If you overshoot into thick syrup, no harm done: dilute with hot water back to where you want it. The dates forgive; the clock is flexible; this is a pot you can walk away from.

**One variation:** the longan version, for the other jar every CNY household owns. Swap half the dates for a small handful of dried longan (龙眼 — "dragon eye" fruit, about 15 g), and simmer the same way. Longan brings a muskier, honeyed, almost floral sweetness that some families prefer; the older generation drank the two simmered together as a matter of course, and the combination under matcha tastes like being sent home with a red packet.

Five drinks, five points on a circle. That is really what this chapter was for — not these particular recipes, which you should feel free to bend, but the habit of letting the calendar into the kitchen: blossoms or roses in the bright months, fruit in the hot ones, roots when the light goes flat, mint when the malls start playing carols, and red dates when the lanterns go up. The tea room changed its hearth twice a year to keep the guests comfortable; you can change your bowl as often as the world outside your window changes, whichever calendar your window follows.

There is one season we have not covered, and it happens every single day: evening. Matcha is a caffeinated leaf, and the bowl that serves you brilliantly at nine in the morning can sabotage you at nine at night. The next chapter is about winding the day down without giving the green up — smaller doses, softer blends, and the gentle art of the last bowl.
