# Cakes

There is a moment that belongs to every matcha cake, and it happens at the table, not in the oven. The cake arrives looking respectable but ordinary — a browned loaf, a pale gold dome, a cheesecake the color of strong tea. Then the knife goes in, the first slice leans away, and the room changes: inside, the crumb is green. Not a polite hint of green — a deep, sliced-open, where-did-that-come-from green that was hiding all along under an ordinary crust. Somebody always makes a small sound. I have cut that first slice more times than I can count and I still slow down for it, because it is the whole doctrine of the last two chapters made visible. The crust is what the oven took. The crumb is what you defended.

This chapter scales the cookie lessons up to the bakes where defense genuinely matters: longer times, deeper batters, more heat for the color to survive. The six cakes run in order of forgiveness. The pound cake is the entry-level bake — sturdy, hard to ruin, happy in any kitchen. The chiffon and the roll cake ask for technique with egg foam and repay it with lightness. The azuki layer cake is our payment of respect to the oldest pairing matcha has. Then comes the one recipe in Part Six that breaks every rule on purpose, gloriously, and finally a cake that never sees an oven at all and points straight at the chilled chapter ahead. Throughout: culinary grade, sifted always, moderate ovens wherever the cake allows, and doses you can reason about with chapter thirty-two's three-to-eight grams per hundred grams of flour. Ovens and brands vary, palates vary more — taste, adjust, and write your numbers down.

The pound cake first, because if you have never baked with matcha, this is where to start. It is the least glamorous cake here and the most reliable: one bowl, one loaf tin, no foam to protect, and a tight, buttery crumb that carries tea flavor beautifully for days. It is also, honestly, the hardest of the six to keep green — a deep loaf needs a long bake, which is exactly what chapter thirty-two warned about — so we fight back the only ways a loaf allows: a moderate oven, a dose that starts loud enough to lose a little, and pulling it at "just done," never "definitely done."

### Matcha Pound Cake

*Makes one loaf; serves eight to ten. About fifteen minutes of work, 50–60 minutes of baking.*

**You need:**

- 200 g unsalted butter, softened but still cool (¾ cup plus 2 tablespoons)
- 180 g sugar (scant 1 cup)
- 4 eggs, room temperature
- 200 g all-purpose flour (1⅔ cups)
- 10 g culinary matcha (about 5 teaspoons) — the 5 g per 100 g flour "conversation" register
- 1 teaspoon baking powder
- A pinch of salt
- 2 tablespoons milk
- A 21 × 11 cm loaf tin (a standard 8½ × 4½ inch loaf pan), lined with parchment

**Steps:**

1. Heat the oven to 160–170°C (320–340°F) — the lower end if your oven runs hot. Sift the flour, matcha, baking powder, and salt together twice, until the mixture is one even pale green with no dark freckles.
2. Beat the butter until creamy, add the sugar, and beat until pale and fluffy, three to four minutes. The butter must be soft but cool — remember the second tray from chapter thirty-two; warm butter is where khaki begins.
3. Add the eggs one at a time, beating well after each. If the mixture looks curdled, add a spoonful of the flour mixture and carry on.
4. Fold in the dry ingredients in two additions, then the milk, stopping the moment no dry streaks remain.
5. Scrape into the tin, level the top, and bake 50–60 minutes, until a skewer comes out with a few moist crumbs. Check at 50; if the top is browning fast, tent it loosely with foil.
6. Cool ten minutes in the tin, then finish cooling on a rack. Store covered and dark — the crumb stays vivid for days if the light never finds it.

**The tricky part:** the last ten minutes. A pound cake looks underdone from the outside long after the center is finished, and every extra "just to be safe" minute trades green for olive. Trust the skewer, not the color of the top — the top was always going to brown; that is the crust's job, and the reveal at the table is better for it.

**One variation:** beat the zest of a lemon into the butter at the start — zest, not juice, exactly as chapter thirty-two argued: the perfume without the acid that attacks the color.

Now the chiffon, the cake matcha seems to have been waiting for. Chiffon is a twentieth-century American invention that Japanese bakers took up so thoroughly it can now feel Japanese, and the fit with matcha is real: a foam cake, barely sweet, baked in a moderate oven, with so much air in the crumb that the tea reads soft and fragrant instead of heavy. It is also the chapter's classroom for the single most important cake skill: folding without deflating.

### Matcha Chiffon

*Makes one 17 cm (7 inch) tube cake; serves eight. About twenty-five minutes of work, 30–35 minutes of baking, and an upside-down hour of cooling.*

**You need:**

- 4 eggs, separated, whites cold
- 70 g sugar (⅓ cup)
- 40 ml neutral oil (3 tablespoons)
- 60 ml water (¼ cup)
- 70 g cake flour (⅔ cup)
- 6 g matcha (about 1 tablespoon) — the top of the range, near 8 g per 100 g flour, a "declaration," because all that air dilutes the tea; a chiffon dosed timidly tastes of nothing
- A pinch of salt
- A 17 cm (7 inch) aluminum tube pan, **ungreased** — this is not optional

**Steps:**

1. Heat the oven to 170°C (340°F). Sift the flour and matcha together twice.
2. Whisk the yolks with a third of the sugar, then the oil, then the water. Sift the flour mixture over and whisk just until smooth.
3. Beat the whites with the salt, adding the remaining sugar gradually, to glossy, softly drooping peaks — firm, not dry and blocky.
4. Whisk a third of the whites into the yolk base to loosen it. Then fold in the rest in two additions with a flexible spatula: cut down through the center, sweep along the bottom, turn the bowl, lift and fold over. Stop at "no white streaks," not at "perfectly uniform."
5. Pour into the ungreased pan, run a skewer once through the batter to pop large pockets, and bake 30–35 minutes, until the top springs back and a skewer comes out clean.
6. Immediately invert the pan onto its feet or over a bottle and let the cake hang upside down until completely cool, at least an hour. Then loosen the sides with a thin knife and unmold.

**The tricky part:** the fold. Every stroke past "combined" costs air, and the air is the cake. Fold like you are turning pages in a borrowed book. And do not grease the pan, however wrong it feels — chiffon rises by climbing the bare walls, and it cools upside down gripping them so the delicate crumb cannot slump under its own weight. A greased pan gives it nothing to hold, in either direction.

**One variation:** fold a teaspoon of finely grated yuzu or orange zest into the yolk base — borrowed perfume for the aroma the oven takes.

The roll cake uses the same foam logic in a different geometry, and the geometry is entirely on our side: a sponge barely a centimeter thick, in and out of the oven in about eleven minutes. Chapter thirty-two's second lever — minimize time at heat, not just temperature — is nowhere more visible; the oven runs hotter here, but so briefly that this thin sheet keeps more green than almost anything else in the chapter. Which is fortunate, because a roll cake is *displayed* green: the spiral is the whole show.

### Matcha Roll Cake (Swiss Roll)

*Makes one roll; serves eight. About twenty minutes of work, 10–12 minutes of baking, and patience while it cools rolled.*

**You need:**

- 4 eggs, room temperature
- 80 g sugar (⅓ cup plus 1 tablespoon)
- 60 g cake flour (½ cup plus 1 tablespoon)
- 5 g matcha (2½ teaspoons) — on paper a declaration, but the plain cream inside tempers it to a conversation
- 20 ml milk (4 teaspoons)
- For the filling: 200 ml cold heavy cream (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon) and 15 g sugar (1 tablespoon)
- A 30 × 25 cm (12 × 10 inch) rimmed baking sheet lined with parchment, plus a clean tea towel

**Steps:**

1. Heat the oven to 190°C (375°F). Sift the flour and matcha together twice.
2. Beat the whole eggs and sugar with an electric mixer on high for five to six minutes, until tripled in volume and thick enough that the batter falling from the beaters sits on the surface in a ribbon before sinking.
3. Sift the flour mixture over the foam in two additions, folding gently each time. Fold in the milk last.
4. Spread evenly into the lined sheet, right into the corners, and bake 10–12 minutes, until the top springs back lightly. Do not overbake — a dry sponge is a cracked sponge.
5. While it is still hot, turn the sponge out onto the tea towel, peel off the parchment, and — this is the crucial move — roll it up in the towel, from a short end, while warm. Let it cool completely rolled.
6. Whip the cream with the sugar to soft-medium peaks. Unroll the cooled sponge, spread the cream, leaving a bare strip at the far end, and re-roll firmly using the towel to guide. Chill seam-down for thirty minutes, then trim the ends for the clean spiral reveal.

**The tricky part:** cracking, and the towel move is the entire cure. A sponge that cools flat sets flat, and bending it afterward breaks it; a sponge that cools *rolled* learns the curve while its crumb is still soft and steamy, and unrolls and re-rolls without protest. Roll it warm — uncomfortably soon after the oven — every single time.

**One variation:** lay a line of sliced strawberries along the cream before rolling; the red against the green spiral needs no further argument.

The layer cake is where this book pays a debt. Matcha and azuki (小豆) — the red bean of Japanese confectionery — are not a pairing anyone alive invented: for centuries, tea-ceremony sweets built on gentle, earthy bean paste have been served before the bitter bowl, each making the other taste more like itself. Chapter thirty-two called that pairing an inheritance, and this cake is our payment of respect: moderate matcha genoise, softly whipped cream, and tsubuan (粒あん) — bean paste with the beans left partly whole — doing what it has always done.

### Matcha-Azuki Layer Cake

*Makes one 15 cm (6 inch) cake; serves eight. About forty minutes of work, 30–35 minutes of baking, best assembled once fully cool.*

**You need:**

- 3 eggs, room temperature
- 90 g sugar (scant ½ cup)
- 90 g cake flour (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon)
- 5 g matcha (2½ teaspoons) — about 5.5 g per 100 g flour, a clear conversation, so the beans are heard too
- 20 g butter, melted and cooled to warm (1½ tablespoons), plus 20 ml milk (4 teaspoons)
- 250 g sweetened azuki paste, tsubuan (about 1 cup)
- 250 ml cold heavy cream (1 cup) and 15 g sugar (1 tablespoon)
- A 15 cm (6 inch) round tin, lined

**Steps:**

1. Heat the oven to 170°C (340°F). Sift the flour and matcha together twice. Stir the warm butter and milk together — warm, not hot, exactly as chapter thirty-two insisted.
2. Beat the eggs and sugar on high for six to eight minutes to the full ribbon stage: thick, pale, and mousse-like.
3. Sift the flour mixture over in two additions, folding gently. Pour the butter-milk down the side of the bowl and fold it in with a few decisive strokes.
4. Bake 30–35 minutes, until the top springs back and a skewer comes out clean. Cool completely on a rack.
5. Slice the cake horizontally into three thin layers with a long serrated knife. Whip the cream with the sugar to medium peaks — firm enough to hold layers apart, still soft enough to spread.
6. Layer: sponge, a thin coat of cream, half the tsubuan spread to just shy of the edge, more cream; repeat; top with the last layer. Frost simply, or leave the sides naked so the green and red strata show. Chill an hour before slicing.

**The tricky part:** the ribbon. A whole-egg foam is subtler than beaten whites — underbeat it and the genoise bakes dense and low, and no assembly rescues a heavy layer. Six full minutes with an electric mixer, and test honestly: the falling batter should rest on the surface like a folded ribbon for a few seconds. If it vanishes instantly, keep beating.

**One variation:** whisk 2 g of matcha into the cream (sifted, always) for a double-green cake that leans further toward the tea and lets the beans be the sweetness.

And now the heretic. Everything since chapter thirty-two has said: moderate ovens, brief bakes, defend the green. The Basque burnt cheesecake — a style made famous by a Basque restaurant tradition in Spain, and adopted everywhere since — does the opposite on purpose: a ferociously hot oven, a deliberately scorched top, a surface taken far past golden into burnished, bitter brown. Baked with matcha, it comes out of the oven looking like the second tray of cookies — khaki, even charred. That is not failure; that is the recipe. The scorch is sacrificed on purpose, like the crust of a good loaf, and it insulates what matters: underneath, the interior barely sets, stays custard-soft and far cooler than the surface ever was, and keeps its green. Cut it cold and the contrast is the most dramatic reveal in this book — burnt umber above, deep green silk below, the caramel bitterness of the top meeting the tea bitterness of the middle like two people who have clearly met before. It breaks the rules and knows exactly why. That is the only way rules should ever be broken.

### Matcha Basque Burnt Cheesecake

*Makes one 15 cm (6 inch) cake; serves eight. Ten minutes of work, 25–35 minutes of baking, and an overnight chill it genuinely deserves.*

**You need:**

- 400 g cream cheese, fully softened (1¾ cups)
- 120 g sugar (½ cup plus 2 tablespoons)
- 3 eggs, room temperature
- 200 ml heavy cream (¾ cup plus 1 tablespoon)
- 10 g flour (1 heaped tablespoon)
- 8 g matcha (4 teaspoons) — no flour to dose against here, so reason against the whole base as chapter thirty-two said: about one percent of it, matcha as the point of the cake
- A pinch of salt
- A 15 cm (6 inch) springform tin lined with two overlapping sheets of parchment, crumpled and pressed in with a tall, rustic overhang

**Steps:**

1. Heat the oven to 220°C (about 430°F), rack in the upper half. Yes, really.
2. Beat the cream cheese and sugar until completely smooth. Beat in the eggs one at a time, then the cream and salt.
3. Sift the flour and matcha together, then sift them *again* directly over the batter and whisk in until no streaks remain. For total insurance, pour the finished batter through a sieve into the tin — a smooth interior is half this cake's luxury.
4. Bake 25–35 minutes. The top should go deeply, unevenly browned — real scorch, not a tan — while the center still sloshes with a confident wobble when you nudge the tin. It will look alarmingly underbaked. It is exactly right.
5. Cool completely in the tin — it will sink into its parchment ruffles, as it should — then chill overnight. Peel, slice with a hot knife, and serve cold.

**The tricky part:** trusting the wobble. Every instinct trained by the other five cakes says "five more minutes," and five more minutes turns silk into something merely fine. The center sets in the fridge, not the oven. Pull it while it trembles, and let the scorched top — not a skewer — be your only doneness signal a cake in this book will ever take from color.

**One variation:** for a softer contradiction, bake at 200°C (400°F) toward the longer time — less scorch, more green at the surface, a gentler cake. Both are honest; they are just different treaties with the oven.

The last cake makes peace by refusing to negotiate at all: no oven. A mille-crêpe — "a thousand crêpes," in cheerful French exaggeration; in practice ten or twelve — is a cake French in name and technique that has become a fixture of Japanese-style cafes, and matcha may be the best reason it did. Because every crêpe cooks in ninety seconds on a stovetop and the whole cake is then simply chilled, the color survives nearly untouched and even some of the aroma the oven always steals is still there at the table. It is a stack of tender green pancakes and lightly sweet cream, and it is the bridge between this chapter and the next: one foot in cake, one foot already in the chilled, no-bake country where matcha is happiest.

### Matcha Mille-Crêpe (No-Oven)

*Makes one 20 cm (8 inch) cake; serves eight. About an hour at the stove and assembly bench, then at least four hours of chilling.*

**You need:**

- 2 eggs
- 250 ml milk (1 cup)
- 100 g all-purpose flour (¾ cup plus 2 tablespoons)
- 5 g matcha (2½ teaspoons) — the 5 g per 100 g register, and with no oven losses it drinks like it reads: unmistakably matcha
- 20 g sugar (1½ tablespoons)
- 20 g butter, melted and cooled (1½ tablespoons)
- A pinch of salt
- For the cream: 300 ml cold heavy cream (1¼ cups), 30 g sugar (2½ tablespoons)
- Extra matcha for dusting, and a 20 cm (8 inch) nonstick pan

**Steps:**

1. Sift the flour, matcha, sugar, and salt together. Whisk the eggs with the milk, then whisk the liquid into the dry ingredients until smooth. Stir in the melted butter, pour the batter through a sieve, and rest it thirty minutes — rested batter makes supple, unbreakable crêpes.
2. Heat the pan over medium, wipe it with the thinnest film of butter, and pour in just enough batter to coat the base — about 50 ml — swirling fast. Cook until the edge lifts and the underside is set, about a minute, flip for fifteen seconds, and slide out. Repeat for ten to twelve crêpes, stacking them as they come.
3. Cool the crêpes completely — warm crêpes melt cream, and the cake collapses before it exists.
4. Whip the cream with the sugar to medium-firm peaks.
5. Lay the best-looking crêpe aside for the top. Build on a serving plate: crêpe, a thin, even layer of cream to the edge, crêpe, cream — thin layers, always; the pleasure is in the count, not the thickness. Finish with the reserved crêpe.
6. Chill at least four hours, ideally overnight, so the layers fuse into something sliceable. Just before serving — never earlier, or it dampens and darkens — dust the top generously with sifted matcha. Slice with a sharp knife wiped between cuts, and admire the stripes.

**The tricky part:** patience at the pan, in two forms. The first crêpe is nearly always a sacrifice — wrong heat, wrong amount — and its only job is to teach you; eat the evidence and carry on. After that, the discipline is evenness: same ladle of batter, same swirl, same minute, twelve times, because one thick crêpe reads as a stripe of dough in an otherwise silken cross-section.

**One variation:** spread a thin layer of black sesame paste on two of the middle crêpes — the deep roasted darkness against the green is the same completing contrast chapter thirty-two promised, drawn in stripes.

Six cakes, five treaties with the oven and one glorious defection — and did you notice where the road has been leading? The mille-crêpe never met the oven, and it was the greenest, most fragrant thing in the chapter. That is not a coincidence; it is an argument, and the next chapter is its conclusion. When there is no heat at all, nearly everything survives: the color, the perfume, even the shy top notes that every bake in this chapter surrendered through the oven vent. Chapter thirty-five is the reward for all this defending — panna cotta, nama chocolate, a no-bake cheesecake, and, at last, the tiramisu I have been promising you since chapter one. I told you then I was unreasonably proud of it. Chill your bowl. It is time to show you why.
