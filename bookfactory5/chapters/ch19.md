# The Business of Green

The sign is small, handwritten, and apologetic, and it is taped to the glass of a tea counter that has been selling matcha since before anyone alive can remember. It says, in Japanese and then in careful English, that the shop is very sorry: the tins everyone came for are sold out. A second sign, printed this time, adds that when stock returns there will be a limit — so many tins per customer, no exceptions, thank you for your understanding.

Outside, the queue does not shrink. It wraps along the storefront in the Kyoto morning heat: tourists with rolling suitcases, a couple consulting a list on a phone, a man who has clearly been sent by someone at home with strict instructions about which exact tin to bring back. Some of them will wait an hour to be told the thing the sign already told them. A few will buy whatever remains — a grade they have never heard of, a tin of hōjicha as a consolation — because coming away with nothing feels wrong after a queue like this.

Stand there for a moment and take in how strange this is. Tea is one of the oldest commodities on earth. It grows on multiple continents, ships in containers, sits on every supermarket shelf in the world. And yet here, at the source, at some of the most famous tea houses in Japan — shops that survived wars, fires, and centuries of changing taste — the shelves are intermittently empty, and rationing signs have become common enough that regulars barely blink at them. In recent years, sold-out notices and per-customer limits at celebrated Kyoto and Uji shops have repeatedly made the news, and airport shelves have been stripped by departing visitors the way duty-free whisky used to be.

Nobody planned this. It is what happens when the world falls in love with something that cannot be hurried.

This chapter is the business story of matcha — told, I promise, without a single invented statistic. You do not need numbers to understand it. You need three ideas: a supply that moves in years, a demand that moves in weeks, and a long chain of hands between the field and your kitchen, each of which adds something and takes something. Hold those, and every headline about matcha shortages, every eyebrow-raising price tag, and every suspiciously cheap pouch will make sense.

## A shortage measured in seasons

Start with the question the queue outside that shop is silently asking: why can't they just make more?

Everything you learned in Part Two is the answer, and it is worth lining the reasons up in one place, because together they form one of the least elastic supply chains in the food world.

- The leaf that becomes matcha is not generic green tea. It is tencha — shaded for weeks before harvest, picked from the tender spring flush, steamed, dried, sorted. A farmer cannot simply redirect ordinary leaf into the matcha stream and have it deserve the name.
- Shade does not improvise. The trellises and covers over a tencha field are infrastructure, built and paid for; converting an ordinary tea field to proper shaded production is a project of seasons, not weeks, and the bushes themselves take years from planting to real production. A farmer who decides today to answer the world's demand will be answering it with leaf that arrives years from now — a slow-motion reply to a question the market asked long ago.
- The best leaf comes overwhelmingly from one harvest window in spring. Supply does not flow continuously like milk; it arrives once, in a rush, and must last the year. If the spring is unkind — a late frost, a heat wave at the wrong moment — there is no second chance until next year.
- The farmers are aging. This is the quiet, human fact underneath every matcha headline. Tea farming in Japan, like small farming almost everywhere, has a succession problem: the average grower is old, the work is hard, and many fields have no obvious next pair of hands. Some acreage leaves production not because demand fell but because a farmer retired and nobody followed. New demand has slowed and in places reversed that decline — shaded tea now earns better than commodity leaf, which is genuinely good news for the regions — but you cannot conjure a generation of skilled growers as fast as you can open cafes.
- And at the end of it all waits the granite. You stood in the milling room two chapters ago; you know what a stone mill produces in an hour, and that the only way to mill more is to buy more mills, more floor, more of the same slow cost. Mills can be added faster than fields can be shaded, but "faster" is a relative term in a room where output is measured in grams.

Now set against all this the demand side, which you also already understand: a drink that photographs like nothing else, a latte that made the taste approachable, a wellness halo, and social media that can send millions of people toward the same product in a season. Demand for matcha can double its enthusiasm in the time it takes a video to trend. Supply answers in years.

When a fast-moving demand meets a slow-moving supply, the textbook offers only a few outcomes: prices rise, queues form, rationing appears, and quality claims come under pressure. Look back at that handwritten sign on the Kyoto counter. Every one of those outcomes is taped to the glass.

None of this means the sky is falling, and it is worth saying so plainly. The world is not running out of matcha; it is running short of the finest, slowest, most place-bound matcha, while producing more of the everyday kind than ever. The squeeze is real, but it is a squeeze at the top of a pyramid — which is a good moment to look at the pyramid itself.

## The ladder of green

All matcha is powdered shaded tea, but the market sorts it into rungs, and it pays to see the ladder whole — because the higher you climb, the faster the price rises and the smaller the quantities get.

On the bottom rung is commodity powder: machine-ground, blended broadly, sold in bulk to factories and bakeries. This is the matcha in your convenience-store ice cream and the green cookies at the chain cafe. There is nothing shameful about it — you learned in the milling chapter why it is the correct tool for those jobs — but it competes mostly on price, and price competition is a hard way to make a living, which is why this rung increasingly draws in newer, lower-cost origins.

The next rung up is cafe grade: powder good enough to be the star of a latte, bought in large pouches by coffee shops. It needs robust flavor that survives milk, dependable color, and above all consistency, batch after batch, because a cafe's regulars notice drift.

Above that sit the named tins: matcha sold under a region's name — Uji, Nishio, Yame, Kagoshima — or a producer's, in small tins meant to be whisked and drunk as itself. Here the vocabulary changes from "product" to "tea": harvest, cultivar, blend. Here the stone mills earn their hour, and here is where the shortage bites hardest, because this is the rung that cannot be expanded quickly and the rung the queue in Kyoto is waiting for.

At the top, in quantities small enough to be almost symbolic, are the competition and connoisseur teas — single cultivars, prize-lot leaf, teas made the way a great vineyard makes its flagship wine, less as a business than as a proof of what the house can do. They anchor the prestige of everything below them.

The commercial logic of this ladder is the same one you find in wine, chocolate, and coffee: the bottom rungs move the volume, but the value — the margin, the reputation, the reason a brand exists at all — concentrates near the top. A blending house's everyday pouch is sold partly on the reflected light of its finest tin. That is why producers protect the top of the ladder so fiercely, and why, when supply tightens, the fight over genuinely top-rung leaf becomes the whole industry's quiet obsession. It is also why the words on labels drift upward over time, each rung reaching for the vocabulary of the rung above it — a drift you will learn to see through in the next chapter.

## Why every cafe wants a matcha menu

Walk the ladder's middle rung into an actual cafe, and the enthusiasm for matcha stops being mysterious in about thirty seconds. Consider what a matcha program asks of a cafe, and what it gives back.

What it asks is remarkably little. The espresso side of the counter is a small industrial installation: a machine that costs as much as a used car, a grinder or three, water treatment, maintenance contracts, and a staff member trained for months to pull consistent shots. The matcha side is a bowl, a whisk or a small electric frother, a sieve, a scoop, and a fridge that was already there for the milk. The training is real but measured in days, not months. Rarely does a premium product walk through the door asking for so little capital.

What it gives back is a drink that sits naturally at the top of the menu's price range — customers accept that matcha costs more, because everything in this book so far is dimly understood even by people who have never read a word about it; the sense that "this powder came a long way and took great effort" has soaked into the culture. The margins on a matcha latte are, qualitatively, the kind cafes dream about: a few grams of powder, milk, and attention, sold at a price that flatters the most elaborate coffee on the board.

And then there is the color. Do not underestimate the color. A matcha drink is a flagship in the old naval sense: the vessel that announces the fleet. It is the drink that gets photographed, the green that stops the scroll, the item that tells a certain customer this cafe speaks their language. Espresso, whatever its virtues, is brown. Cafes discovered — first in Japan, then everywhere — that one luminous green drink on the menu earns attention that spills over onto everything else they sell. The latte, as the last chapter argued, carried the leaf; the cafe economics in this one explain why so many hands were eager to do the carrying.

There is one honest caveat to this rosy picture, and you can taste it. Because the barrier to entry is so low, nothing stops a cafe from serving matcha badly: stale powder from an oversized pouch opened months ago, boiling water, a sad unsifted clump at the bottom of the cup. The same low threshold that put matcha on every menu also put mediocre matcha on many of them. The cafe boom runs on the leaf's reputation; it does not automatically protect it. Remember that the next time a green latte disappoints you — the failure is usually in the last thirty seconds of the chain, not the first three years.

## The layer you never see

Here is a fact about the matcha on the world's shelves that surprises almost everyone the first time they hear it: most companies selling matcha grow no tea.

The brand whose tin sits on your counter — with its serene fonts and its talk of tradition — is, in most cases, not a farm. Between the fields and the shelf lies a layer the customer rarely sees: the blending and finishing houses, some of them centuries old, and the modern brands that buy from them. The classic pattern in Japan looks like this: farmers grow and steam the leaf and dry it into rough tencha; refiners sort and finish it; blending houses — the great tea merchants of Uji and elsewhere — taste, grade, buy, and above all blend, composing lots from many farms into consistent products; and only then does matcha get milled, tinned, and dressed in a brand.

It is tempting, in a culture that romanticizes "direct from the farmer," to read that chain as a list of middlemen skimming value. Resist the temptation, because the blender's craft is real and old. Single-field tea is like single-cask whisky: sometimes glorious, always variable. A given farm's leaf changes year to year with the weather; a blending house's job — the craft called gōgumi (合組) — is to compose, from dozens of sources, a tea that tastes like itself every single year. When you rebuy a favorite matcha and it greets you like an old friend, you are tasting a blender's skill as surely as a farmer's. The famous tea houses of Kyoto guard their master blenders the way restaurants guard chefs.

But the invisible layer also means something a buyer should sit with: the words on the tin are the brand's words, not the farm's. A company that grows nothing can still be a superb curator — many are — or it can be a marketing office with a logo, buying whatever powder is cheap this season and printing "ceremonial" on it, and from the outside the two look identical. The tin will not tell you which you are holding. In a tight market this matters more, not less: when genuine top-grade leaf is scarce, the temptation to stretch definitions grows in exact proportion to the shortage. The skill of telling curators from costumes — reading origin claims, price logic, and label language like an insider — is precisely the next chapter's business, so hold the question; you will have tools for it shortly.

For now, take the consumer's rule of thumb: a brand that tells you where its tea comes from, and when it was harvested and milled, is behaving like a curator. A brand that offers only adjectives is asking for trust it has not earned.

## Three ways the decade can go

So: demand that can surge in a season, supply that answers in years, and a long chain under strain. If the world's appetite for matcha keeps growing — and nothing in the cafes, the feeds, or the queues suggests otherwise — the pressure has to escape somewhere. There are only three directions it can go, and the most likely forecast is all three at once.

The first escape valve is price. The straightforward one: the finest leaf gets bid up, and the tins at the top of the ladder cost more than they used to. In one sense this is the system working — higher prices are what finally make shading a field, and inheriting a tea farm, look like a good idea to a young grower, and new shaded acreage and new milling capacity are in fact being added in Japan's tea regions. But fields planted today answer questions asked years ago; price is the fast messenger between them. For a buyer, this valve is at least honest. You can see it. The tin costs what it costs.

The second valve is quieter and more corrosive: the words stretch. When there is not enough tea that deserves the top-rung vocabulary, the vocabulary drifts down to meet the tea that exists. Later-harvest leaf gets milled and sold with first-flush language. Powder that would once have been called culinary gets promoted a rung. "Ceremonial," which you already know has no legal definition, gets applied with ever more generosity. No single actor has to behave scandalously for this to happen; a hundred small optimistic labelings, each barely defensible, add up to a market where the same word quietly means less each year. For a buyer this valve is the dangerous one, because the price stays premium while the substance slides. Watch for it. The whole next chapter is your defense against it.

The third valve is new origins. Matcha's methods — shading, steaming, fine milling — are knowledge, and knowledge travels. Within Japan, regions beyond the famous names have been expanding shaded production for years. Beyond Japan, China — where whisked powdered tea was born, as Part One told — has built serious modern matcha capacity, and other tea countries are experimenting. History suggests how this goes: new origins enter at the bottom of the ladder, get dismissed by purists, improve faster than the dismissals are updated, and eventually produce their own genuinely fine lots — exactly the road Kagoshima walked within Japan, from bulk-leaf reputation to prizewinning teas. For a buyer, new origins are mostly good news: they take pressure off the everyday rungs, keep lattes affordable, and eventually add new fine teas to the world. The caution is only that origin claims become another thing labels can fudge, and that "Japanese-style" and "Japanese" are different words.

Price, words, geography. When you read matcha news this decade — a shortage headline, a rationing sign, a suspiciously affordable "ceremonial" pouch, a new origin's debut — you are watching one of those three valves hiss. Sometimes all of them in the same story.

## What this means for the person holding the tin

Come back one last time to the queue in Kyoto, because everyone in it is holding this chapter's lesson without knowing it.

The shortage they are queuing under is not a scandal. It is the honest arithmetic of the thing they came for: a leaf that must be shaded for weeks, picked in one brief spring window, by a shrinking number of farmers, and ground by stones that refuse to hurry — colliding with a world that discovered it all at once. The sold-out sign is what integrity looks like under pressure. The shop that limits tins rather than quietly refilling them with lesser leaf is protecting the only thing it truly owns, which is the meaning of its own name.

And that, for you, is the practical takeaway of the whole business story. In a market where supply is tight and the words are under strain, the label has become a battlefield — the place where honest scarcity, stretched definitions, and new origins all make their claims in the same serene typeface. The queue's mistake is not wanting good tea; it is believing that fame alone still guarantees it, in an era when even the famous counters run empty and a hundred new brands have arrived to catch the overflow.

You now know how the green business actually works: where the leaf comes from, why there is not more of it, who blends it, why the cafe loves it, and which way the pressure leaks. What you need next is the last skill of an insider — the ability to stand in front of a shelf, read past the poetry, and know in a minute what the tin is and is not promising you.

That is the next chapter. Bring a skeptical eye. The tea deserves it, and so do you.
