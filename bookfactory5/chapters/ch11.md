# Uji, Nishio, and Beyond

Come stand on the Uji Bridge at dawn, in the hills south of Kyoto, and look down at the river. You will mostly see mist. It rises off the Ujigawa in slow white sheets, slides over the banks, and settles into the folds of the surrounding hills the way milk settles into tea — filling every hollow, softening every edge. Somewhere under that white blanket are tea fields, and they are the reason you are here, because the mist is not scenery. The mist is the product.

By mid-morning the sun will have burned it off and Uji (宇治) will look like what it also is: a small, pleasant tourist town with a famous temple, a very old bridge, and an improbable number of shops selling green ice cream. But at dawn you can see the older truth. This narrow river valley holds moisture like a cupped hand. The mist shields young leaves from frost in the cold months and from harsh light in the warm ones; the days here run a little gentler than the plains, the humidity a little higher, the swings a little smaller. Tea plants, which build their sweetness slowly and lose it under stress, are very much creatures of gentle circumstances. People noticed. They have been noticing for roughly eight hundred years.

This chapter is about *where* matcha comes from — the regions, what makes each one distinct, and how much any of it should matter to you when you are standing in front of a shelf. The honest answer to that last question is "less than the marketing implies, more than zero," and getting you to that answer properly is the work of the next few thousand words.

## What terroir honestly means for a leaf

Wine people have a word, *terroir*, for the idea that a place — its soil, slope, weather, mist — ends up in the glass. Tea has the same idea without always using the French for it, and with matcha the claim is real but needs handling with both hands.

The real part: climate and soil genuinely shape a tea leaf. Cooler, mistier growing conditions slow the leaf down, and a slow leaf accumulates more of the amino acids that read as sweetness and umami in the bowl. Warmer, sunnier conditions push faster growth and, with it, more of the catechins that read as bitterness — which is exactly why, as we saw two chapters ago, farmers build shade over their fields: shading is portable mist, a way of manufacturing a gentle climate on demand. River-valley fog was doing that job in Uji before anyone thought to imitate it with reed screens.

The part that needs honesty: with matcha, the place where the leaf grew is only one voice in a choir. The cultivar, the shading regime, the pluck, the steaming, the grinding, the freshness of the tin by the time it reaches you — every one of those can shout louder than geography. A carelessly made matcha from a famous valley will lose, in your bowl, to a carefully made one from a place you have never heard of. Keep that in your pocket as we tour the map. Regions are real. They are just not destiny.

## Uji: the name that means tradition

Begin where the prestige lives. Uji sits where the Ujigawa comes down out of the hills toward Kyoto, and you have already met it in this book: this is the valley at the end of the seed-legend chain we traced in the chapter on Eisai — the place tradition says received tea within a generation of the monk's return, and the region that rose over the following centuries to become the supreme name in Japanese tea. Whatever you make of the legends, the rise itself is documented fact. By the age of the great tea masters, Uji tea was the tea; warlords fought over its jars, and the shoguns had its harvest carried to them in an annual procession.

Two things built that reputation, and both still matter.

The first is the microclimate you watched from the bridge: mist, shelter, mild days, well-drained hillside soils along a river. Good tea country by any measure.

The second is the one people underrate: accumulation. Uji has been solving the problems of fine tea, continuously, for something like eight centuries. Covered cultivation — the shading that defines matcha and gyokuro — was refined here. Generations of growers, blenders, and stone-mill operators have handed down judgments about pluck and steam and grind that never made it into any book. When you pay for Uji matcha, a large share of what you are paying for is not soil. It is institutional memory — the compounded interest of forty generations of people caring about the same narrow problem. That is a real thing, and it is why the region's best teas remain the reference point against which everything else is measured.

Now for the part of the Uji story that surprises people, and I want to present it fairly, because it is usually either hidden or told as a scandal, and it is neither.

If you look up how "Uji matcha" (宇治抹茶) may be labeled, you will find that the accepted trade definition — the one used by Kyoto's own tea industry — does not require the leaf to have been grown in the town of Uji, or even in Kyoto Prefecture. Leaf from the wider region, including neighboring prefectures with their own long tea histories, qualifies, provided it is finished by Kyoto's processors in the Uji tradition. In other words: on a tin, *Uji* names a lineage of craft with a geographic heart, not a fence around a field.

Is that a swindle? I honestly do not think so, and here is why. The town of Uji is small; its actual tea acreage is modest and has been for a long time, while its blending houses and finishing craft are the region's true engine. The convention grew out of how the trade has genuinely worked for generations — surrounding valleys growing leaf, Uji's houses selecting, blending, refining, and grinding it. Champagne draws its famous line around vineyards; Uji, in practice, draws its line around a tradition of finishing. Neither approach is dishonest, but they are different promises, and you deserve to know which one the label is making. What "Uji matcha" reliably tells you is *who finished this and in what tradition*. What it does not tell you is the GPS coordinates of the bush. A seller who volunteers more detail than the label requires — naming the growing area, the harvest, even the farm — is showing you a kind of confidence worth rewarding.

## Nishio: the other heartland

Travel east from Kyoto into Aichi Prefecture, near the coast south of Nagoya, and you reach the second name every matcha drinker should know: Nishio (西尾).

Nishio does not have Uji's fame, and it has never much needed it, because it has something rarer: focus. Most Japanese tea regions grow tea broadly — sencha above all — and make tencha, matcha's raw leaf, as a sideline. Nishio went the other way. From the late nineteenth century onward, its growers bet heavily and specifically on tencha, and the district became something close to a company town for matcha's raw material: fields shaded on schedule, factories built for the steam-and-dry tencha process, the whole local economy arranged around powdered tea. For much of the modern era, a very large share of Japan's tencha has come from this one compact district — bulk culinary grades, yes, but genuinely fine teas as well.

The place fits the work. The tea fields sit on deep, fertile alluvial soil laid down by the Yahagi River, in a mild coastal climate, and the district has its own medieval origin story — tea raised at a local Zen temple, in the same wave of monastery planting we followed in the Eisai chapter — so the roots are old even if the industrial focus is newer. Nishio matcha has a reputation for a rounded, mellow character, and the region's specialization means that even its everyday grades tend to be made by people who think about nothing but tencha all year.

If Uji is matcha's cathedral, Nishio is its workshop — and I mean that as a compliment. Cathedrals get the postcards. Workshops feed everyone. There is also a small consumer lesson hiding in the comparison: because Nishio's name carries less romance, its label is borrowed less often, and a tin that says Nishio is usually telling you something rather plainer and more literal than a tin that says Uji. Less poetry, more paperwork. Some days that is exactly what you want.

## The rest of the Japanese map

Three more names will cover nearly every tin you are likely to meet.

- **Yame (八女), Fukuoka.** In the hills of northern Kyushu, Yame is a small region with an outsized reputation, built above all on gyokuro — the shaded, whole-leaf luxury tea that is matcha's closest cousin. A district that has mastered shading for gyokuro has, almost by definition, the skills that fine tencha demands, and Yame's matcha, though produced in small quantities, tends to carry the same signature: deep sweetness, thick umami, prices that reflect hand-work. Think of it as a boutique.
- **Kagoshima (鹿児島).** The far south of Kyushu is the map's fast-moving frontier. Kagoshima is one of Japan's largest tea producers, and its farms look nothing like the postcard: broad, flat, efficient fields, often machine-harvested, on volcanic soils in a warm climate that allows the earliest harvests in the country. For years that industrial profile earned it a certain snobbery — big, cheap, southern. The snobbery is aging badly. Kagoshima's growers have invested seriously in shading, in tencha capacity, and in a wide palette of cultivars, and the best Kagoshima matcha now competes on quality, not just price. If the twentieth century's prestige map was written in Uji and Nishio, the twenty-first's will have Kagoshima on it in large letters.
- **Shizuoka (静岡).** The slopes around Mount Fuji form Japan's most famous tea landscape and its longtime volume champion — but overwhelmingly for sencha, the ordinary (I say that fondly) steeped green tea of daily Japanese life. Shizuoka makes matcha too, and some of it is good, but tencha has historically been a small part of its enormous portfolio. If sencha is Japan's daily bread, Shizuoka is the national bakery that also, on occasion, makes pastry.

There are smaller regions besides — pockets of tencha in Nara, Shiga, Mie (some of which, remember, may legitimately end up in an Uji-labeled tin), and elsewhere — but with these five names you can read almost any label you will encounter.

## A word about cultivars

Underneath geography sits a quieter variable that tea people discuss the way wine people discuss grapes: the cultivar, the specific propagated variety of the tea plant. You do not need to memorize a catalogue. Three names will make you more literate than most matcha drinkers.

- **Yabukita (やぶきた).** The workhorse. Selected in Shizuoka about a century ago and planted so widely that it dominates Japanese tea fields as a whole, Yabukita is hardy, reliable, and good at nearly everything — bred with sencha in mind, but perfectly capable of making respectable tencha under shade.
- **Samidori (さみどり).** An Uji classic, long favored there for gyokuro and matcha; its association with shaded tea runs deep, and it remains one of the names most often seen on single-cultivar matcha from the Kyoto region.
- **Okumidori (おくみどり).** A late-budding variety whose name contains the word for deep green, which is convenient, because a deep, even green is what it is loved for; it shades well and turns up in fine matcha from several regions.

Most matcha, it should be said, is a blend — of cultivars, fields, and often harvests — because blending is how a tea house keeps its signature taste steady from year to year. A single-cultivar matcha is not better; it is more *legible*, a chance to taste one plant's personality unblended. Try one when you are curious. Rely on blends when you want consistency.

## The world tour, honestly

Now for the question Japanese labels prefer not to raise: matcha is no longer only Japanese, and the world map deserves the same honesty as the domestic one.

Start with China, where this whole story began — powdered, whisked tea was a Chinese invention centuries before it was a Japanese one, as we saw in Part One. Today China is the source of most of the world's inexpensive matcha-labeled powder: the green dust in bargain bins, in many mass-market lattes, in a great deal of what food manufacturers buy by the barrel. And here I want to be precise, because there are two lazy stories available and both are wrong. The first lazy story is that Chinese matcha is fake by definition. It is not; China has real tea plants, real shading where producers choose to use it, real grinding, and some producers there make genuinely creditable matcha. The second lazy story is that origin does not matter at all. In practice, much of what China exports under the name is made to a price: less shading or none, coarser processing, older leaf, sun-driven bitterness hidden by the word "culinary." The honest summary is the one this book keeps returning to: **process matters, not passport.** A leaf that was properly shaded, steamed, and stone-ground is matcha wherever it grew; a leaf that skipped those steps is just green tea powder, whatever its label and whatever its nationality. The reason "Japanese" functions as a quality signal is not magic soil — it is that the expensive process is, statistically, far more likely to have actually happened there.

Korea deserves its own sentence rather than a footnote. Korean tea culture is old and serious, and powdered green tea — 가루차, *garu-cha*, "powder tea," sometimes called *malcha*, the Korean reading of the same characters as matcha — has its own lineage there, with its own temple history and its own modern producers, notably from the green tea regions of the south. Good Korean powdered tea is not an imitation of anything; it is a parallel branch of the same ancient tree, and if you meet one from a reputable maker, taste it with curiosity rather than suspicion.

And beyond? Tencha-style production has been attempted in a scattering of other places — elsewhere in Asia and, experimentally, further afield — as the matcha boom makes the economics interesting. Some of it will come to something; most early efforts at any craft are tuition. The test for a new-origin matcha is the same as for an old one: was the leaf shaded, was it handled quickly and gently, was it ground fine and fresh — and does the seller tell you these things plainly, or hide behind a green photograph?

## How much should region matter to you?

Time to bring the map back down to the shelf, because you did not read this chapter to become an examiner of prefectures. You read it to buy better tea. So, plainly:

I have watched people stand in front of a tea shelf holding two tins, one stamped with a famous valley and one without, trying to taste geography through the packaging. It cannot be done, and a simple ranking is the way out of the trap.

If you are new to matcha, region should be roughly the *fourth* thing you weigh. Ahead of it, in order: whether the seller is honest and specific (real harvest information beats poetic vagueness every time); whether the tea is fresh (matcha fades in months, not years, and no famous name survives staleness); and whether the style suits your use (a tin for daily lattes has a different job than a tin for quiet bowls). A fresh, honestly sold matcha from Kagoshima will beat a tired, vaguely sold "Uji" every single time, and the difference will not be subtle.

Where region earns its keep is later, at the point where you already whisk a decent bowl and want to understand what you are tasting. Then the names become genuinely useful — not as rankings but as styles, the way a music lover uses labels and eras. Put an Uji and a Nishio and a Kagoshima side by side some quiet afternoon, same water, same whisk, and taste what eight hundred years of mist, a century of specialization, and a generation of southern ambition each do to a leaf. That comparison will teach you more than any chapter can, including this one.

One caution to carry out of the chapter: a region name on a tin is a claim, and claims are only as good as the person making them. The famous names attract exactly the borrowing you would expect, and no tea police will intervene between a vague label and your wallet. The defense is not memorizing geography. It is choosing sellers who tell you more than they legally must — and knowing what the words on the tin do and do not promise, which by now you do.

Which raises the practical question this whole part of the book has been walking toward. You know how the leaf is shaded, picked, steamed, and ground; you know what the grade words mean and now what the place names mean. What remains is the last mile: how to actually choose a tin, pay a fair price for it, and — this is the part almost everyone gets wrong — keep it alive once it is open, because the freshest matcha in Japan can be ruined in a fortnight on a sunny kitchen shelf. That rescue mission is the next chapter.
