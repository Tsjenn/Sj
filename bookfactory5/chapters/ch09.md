# Thirty Grams an Hour

It is two in the morning in a milling room in Uji, and the stones are still turning.

Nobody is watching them do it. The workers went home hours ago, after checking the hoppers and brushing down the chutes, and now the room belongs to the mills — a few dozen of them in rows, each one a squat cylinder of grey granite about the size of a birthday cake, each one rotating with the unhurried patience of a thing that has nowhere better to be. The sound they make together is hard to describe to someone who has not stood in that room. It is not grinding, exactly. It is lower and softer than that: a deep, granular hum, like distant surf, or like a building purring. Under the hum, if you listen, each mill adds its own faint dry whisper — stone conversing with leaf.

And the smell. The air in a milling room is green in a way that air almost never is — sweet and vegetal, somewhere between fresh edamame, warm nori, and the inside of a just-opened tin of the best tea you ever bought. It settles on your clothes. People who work in these rooms stop noticing it; visitors never do.

All night, from the bottom of each mill, matcha creeps out onto the collecting tray in a ring of impossible green — so slowly that if you stare at any one mill you will swear it is producing nothing at all. Come back in an hour and there is a little more. Come back at dawn and each stone has made, roughly, one small tin's worth.

That is the fact this chapter is named for, the one I promised you back in the first chapter: a granite mill, turning all hour without rest, produces on the order of thirty or forty grams of matcha. Not per minute. Per hour. There are single espresso machines that move more coffee in a lunch rush than one of these stones moves tea in a day.

Every serious conversation about what matcha costs, and what the words on the tin mean, eventually comes back to this room. So let us stay in it a while and understand what is actually happening inside those stones.

## A machine with two moving parts

The mill is called an ishiusu (石臼) — literally "stone mortar" — and it is one of the oldest food machines still doing daily commercial work anywhere. The design that grinds your matcha is, in its essentials, the design that arrived in Japan from China centuries ago. It has been refined, motorized, and multiplied, but never fundamentally improved upon, for reasons we will get to.

An ishiusu is two circular stones of hard granite, stacked face to face like a pair of thick coins. The bottom stone stays put. The top stone rotates. In the center of the top stone is a hole, and into that hole falls the raw material: tencha (碾茶), the shaded, steamed, dried, de-stemmed and de-veined leaf we made in the last chapter. Look at that word closely, because it contains a small poem. The character 碾 means to mill or grind with a stone. Tencha is, literally, "milling tea" — the leaf is named not for what it is but for what it is destined to become. It spends its whole life waiting for this room.

The magic is in the faces of the stones — the two surfaces you never see, pressed against each other. They are not flat and they are not smooth. Each face is carved with a pattern of shallow grooves radiating out from the center at an angle, dividing the surface into sections like slices of a pie, each slice combed with fine parallel channels. When the top stone turns, the grooves of the two faces cross each other continuously, like the blades of scissors closing over and over, thousands of times per rotation.

A flake of tencha falling through the center hole is drawn outward between the faces. As it travels, the crossing grooves shear it — not crushing it so much as slicing and re-slicing it, finer and finer, while the weight of the upper stone presses the work onward. The channels get shallower toward the rim, so the leaf is cut progressively smaller the farther out it moves. By the time it reaches the edge of the stones and drifts out into the collecting tray, it is no longer a flake of anything. It is powder — powder so fine that describing it will take a whole section of this chapter.

Two stones. One of them turns. That is the entire machine. The genius is not in the mechanism; it is in the carving, and in the speed. Especially the speed.

## Why slow is the whole point

The top stone of a matcha mill rotates at somewhere around thirty to sixty revolutions per minute — figures vary by workshop and machine, so hold the range loosely, but the spirit of it is: about once a second, give or take. You could comfortably follow a single point on the stone with your eyes. A kitchen blender spins hundreds of times faster.

This is not nostalgia. It is thermodynamics.

Grinding is friction, and friction is heat. Anyone who has run coffee beans through a cheap blade grinder has smelled this — that hot, slightly scorched edge the grounds pick up when the blades run too long. Now consider what tencha is: a shade-grown leaf whose entire value lives in fragile things. Its color is chlorophyll, which dulls with heat, sliding from that electric spring green toward olive and khaki. Its aroma is a cloud of volatile compounds — the fresh, sweet, marine notes — and "volatile" is chemist's language for *leaves at the first excuse*. Its sweetness comes in part from the amino acids the shading concentrated so carefully, three chapters of effort ago.

Grind that leaf fast, and you cook it, gently and invisibly, in the very act of powdering it. The powder comes out warmer, duller, flatter — nothing dramatic, no smoke, no alarm, just a quiet tax collected on everything the farmer spent a season building.

Grind it slowly, between stones whose sheer mass swallows and dissipates what little heat the work creates, and the leaf comes out of the mill essentially as cool as it went in, its color and aroma still intact. Granite is well suited to this job: hard enough to hold a carved edge for a long time, massive enough to act as its own heat sink, and slow to wear into the product.

So the slowness of the ishiusu is not a quaint inefficiency that nobody has gotten around to fixing. The slowness is the specification. The mill is not slow despite producing the best matcha; it produces the best matcha because it is slow. Once you see that, the arithmetic of the next section stops looking absurd and starts looking inevitable.

## The arithmetic of patience

Let us take the famous figure seriously for a moment and do what it invites us to do: multiply.

One mill, one hour: roughly thirty to forty grams. The number is approximate — it moves with the mill, the leaf, the fineness being asked for — and anyone who quotes you a precise figure is being more confident than the stones themselves. But the range is real, it is small, and it is the industry's own rough rule of thumb.

One mill, running a full day and night without a pause: somewhere under a kilogram. A single busy cafe can whisk and steam its way through that in a weekend.

This is why a milling room contains ranks of mills rather than one big one, and why the mills run overnight, tended lightly, filling their trays in the dark. You cannot make one stone go meaningfully faster — faster is precisely the thing the stone exists to refuse. You can only add more stones. A producer who wants to double output must double mills, floor space, granite, and maintenance. Scale, in stone-milled matcha, is not a discount. It is a multiplication of the same slow cost.

Now put that against the sticker on a tin. Twenty or thirty grams of first-rate matcha — a tin that fits in your palm — represents the better part of an hour of one machine's entire existence, before you count the shading, the hand-picking, the steaming, the sorting, and the farmer's whole year standing behind those. When people gasp at matcha prices, they are usually comparing it to bagged tea by weight, which is a little like comparing a hand-bound book to newsprint by the kilo. The honest comparison is per bowl, and we will do that sum properly in a later chapter, because it comes out far more reasonably than the tin price suggests.

But the deeper point is this: nothing about that price is mysterious once you have stood in the milling room. You are not paying for mystique. You are paying for an hour of granite.

## Finer than flour

What does all that patience actually buy? Fineness — a specific, extreme fineness that is the difference between drinking matcha and drinking green dust.

Stone-milled matcha comes off the tray at a particle size measured in microns — thousandths of a millimeter — with the individual grains commonly described as a few microns to around ten across. Numbers that small mean nothing to human hands, so try these instead. It is far finer than table salt. It is finer than powdered sugar. It is meaningfully finer than wheat flour — flour, which we already treat as the definition of a fine powder. A human hair is roughly ten times wider than the larger matcha particles. You are down at the scale of talc, of cosmetic pigment.

Remember the first chapter: matcha is a suspension, not an infusion. The whole enterprise depends on convincing solid leaf to hang in water and stay there long enough to be drunk, and to slide over your tongue without announcing itself as solid at all. Particle size is what makes that possible. Fine enough, and the powder suspends into something with the texture of thin silk, holds a foam, and reads to your palate as pure liquid with an improbable body to it. Too coarse, and the same leaf turns gritty on the tongue, settles out into sludge at the bottom of the bowl, and refuses to foam — the difference between a green cloud and green sand.

This is also where the feel test comes from, the oldest quality check there is, and you can do it the day your next tin arrives. Take a pinch of matcha and press it firmly between your fingertip and thumb, then rub slowly. Truly fine matcha does not feel like a powder at all. It packs into a smooth, dense, almost clay-like film, clings to the ridges of your fingerprint, and vanishes into the skin like the eyeshadow I compared it to in chapter one. What it must never do is feel granular. If you can feel individual particles — a fine sandiness, a whisper of grit — the grind is coarse, and no amount of whisking technique will fully rescue it. Your fingers, it turns out, are a respectable laboratory: skin can detect textures far finer than the eye can resolve.

One caution, in fairness: the feel test screens for fineness, and fineness alone. A well-milled mediocre leaf will pass it. It tells you the grinding was done right, not that everything before the grinding was. For that you need your eyes and nose too — which is the next chapter's business.

## The people who carve the stones

Here is the part of this story I find most moving, and it happens nowhere near a tea field.

Those grooves — the radiating, angled channels that do the actual cutting — do not last forever. Granite is hard, but the work is endless, and over months and years of rotation the edges of the grooves slowly round off and dull. A worn mill does not stop working; it works *worse*, in ways an accountant might not notice for a while. It grinds a little coarser, a little slower, a little warmer. The powder loses its silk by degrees. So every working mill must periodically be taken apart and have its faces re-cut, groove by groove, by hand — a craft called, plainly enough, dressing the stone.

The people who can do this well are specialists, and there are not many of them. Cutting a functional groove in granite is masonry; cutting a pattern of grooves that will shear tea leaf to a uniform handful of microns, at the correct rate, without hot spots, is something closer to instrument-making. The carver works with hammer and chisel and long experience, reading the wear in the old faces the way a cobbler reads a heel — because the wear pattern is a record of how that particular stone has been running. A milling room full of quietly turning granite depends absolutely on these few hands, the way an orchestra of violins depends on the one luthier in town.

Why has nobody automated the carving, or replaced the whole apparatus with something modern that never wears out? People have certainly tried alternatives — and honesty requires us to look at them squarely, because they are not the villains of this story.

## The honest case for the machines

Walk into an industrial facility that produces matcha for the world's cafes, ice-cream makers, and bakeries, and you will mostly not find rooms of granite. You will find modern grinding machinery — most commonly ball mills, in which the tea tumbles inside a rotating drum with ceramic balls that pound and rub it into powder, and in some operations jet mills and similar systems, which hurl particles into each other or against surfaces with blasts of air until they shatter to size, with the whole apparatus often chilled to keep temperatures down.

These machines out-produce a stone mill by an enormous margin. That is their entire purpose, and here is the part that matcha romantics do not always say out loud: *most of the matcha the world actually consumes is ground this way*, and for most of what the world does with matcha, that is the correct choice.

The differences from stone milling are real. They are two. First, heat and force: machine grinding is more violent, and even with cooling, the leaf generally has a rougher ride than it gets between slow granite faces — with the same quiet consequences for color and aroma that we met earlier, to a degree that varies with the equipment and the care taken. Second, particle character: different grinding methods break the leaf differently, producing particles of different shapes and a different spread of sizes, which subtly changes how the powder suspends, foams, and feels.

Now the honest ranking of stakes. In a bowl of straight usucha — powder, water, whisk, nothing else — these differences have nowhere to hide. Fineness, foam, aroma, and the exact shade of green are the entire experience, and stone-milled tea, made from leaf good enough to deserve the stones, wins on those terms. This is the one place where the granite genuinely earns its hour.

In a latte, milk's fat and sweetness pave over the subtleties; a good machine-ground powder makes an excellent latte, and many drinkers, in an honest blind tasting, would struggle to fund the difference. And in baking, the case for stone milling collapses almost completely: the oven's heat dulls delicate aromatics and darkens the green regardless of provenance, flour and sugar and butter shout over the whispers, and what a cookie needs from matcha — robust flavor and strong color — is exactly what good culinary powder delivers, at a fraction of the price. Buying premium stone-milled matcha to bake with is not connoisseurship. It is burning an hour of granite to color a biscuit.

So let me say the balanced thing plainly, because you will meet plenty of people online who will not. Machine-ground matcha is not fake matcha. It is not a scam, an insult, or a lesser truth; it is whole shaded leaf, ground fine, exactly as the definition requires, made efficiently for uses where efficiency is the right value. The scam, when there is one, is mislabeling — coarse or dull or stale powder sold with words that promise otherwise. The stone mill is not a moral category. It is a tool with one specific, irreplaceable talent: preserving the very finest leaf, at the very finest grind, cool and intact, for the one preparation where every micron of that care lands directly on your tongue.

Pay for the stones when you will drink the tea straight. Skip them, with a clear conscience, when you will not.

## Where the powder goes next

By dawn the milling room has finished its shift. The trays are ringed with green, the hoppers are low, and somewhere a worker with a soft brush is gathering the night's few hundred grams with the care of someone sweeping up currency — which, in a sense, they are.

But look at what has actually happened here. The stones did not make quality; they *revealed* it, or preserved it. Everything that will make one tin transcendent and another tin merely pleasant was already locked into the leaf before it ever fell through the center hole — the weeks of shade, the pick, the steam, the sorting. The mill is where tea's value is finished, not where it is created.

Which brings us to an awkward question, and it is standing right outside the milling-room door. All of this powder — the overnight granite batches and the industrial tonnage alike — now gets packed into tins and pouches, and words get printed on them. *Ceremonial. Premium. Grade A. Barista.* Words that sound like law and are, in fact, mostly poetry. What do they actually promise? Who checks? And how do you, standing in front of a shelf, tell the tea that deserved the stones from the tea that merely hired the vocabulary?

That is the next chapter, and it may be the most money-saving one in this book.
