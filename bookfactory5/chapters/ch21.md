# The Ten-Ringgit Bowl

There is a receipt in my wallet from a cafe I like very much. It is for one iced matcha latte, and the number at the bottom is roughly what a plate of decent noodles costs in the same neighborhood. I do not resent it. The cafe made the drink beautifully — sifted powder, properly cool water, milk foamed to velvet, a room worth sitting in — and rent, wages, and washing-up are real things that someone must pay for. The receipt is honest.

At home, on a shelf away from the stove, sits a small tin. It weighs thirty grams, which sounds like nothing and looks like less. It cost more than that receipt — noticeably more, in fact, because I bought it well and it is genuinely fine tea. And every morning I open it, level out about two grams with a scoop, and whisk a bowl that I would put up against the cafe's version without a flicker of embarrassment.

The tin has been open for two and a half weeks. It is not yet half empty.

This chapter is about what those two pieces of paper — the receipt, and the little label on the tin — say to each other when you put them side by side. It is the home economics chapter, which sounds dry, and it is anything but, because hiding inside the arithmetic is one of the most cheering facts about matcha: it is one of the very few luxuries where the genuinely good version is within reach of an ordinary budget. But we will get to that. First, the sums.

## Arithmetic you can do on a napkin

I promised at the start of this book that I would not print prices, and I will keep that promise here of all places, where the temptation is greatest. Currencies wobble, cities differ, and any number I typed this year would be quietly wrong by the time you read it. Fortunately the argument does not need numbers. It needs ratios, and ratios travel well.

Start with the cafe side. Wherever you live, a matcha latte costs about what every other specialty drink costs — the flat white, the single-origin pour-over, the seasonal thing with the syrup. Cafes price by the category of the drink, not by the leaf, and matcha sits firmly in the "special" category. The title of this chapter calls that a ten-ringgit bowl, because ten ringgit is the round number a Malaysian reaches for when they mean *one nice drink out* — but the ringgit is a stand-in. Fill in your own currency and your own cafe. You know your number. It is printed on a receipt in your wallet right now.

Now the home side. A standard tin of matcha holds thirty grams. A proper bowl of usucha uses about two grams; a homemade latte, being milk-forward, often uses a touch more. Call it fifteen to twenty servings per tin — fifteen if you are generous with the scoop, twenty if you are exact. That figure is not marketing; it is just division, and it holds whether the tin cost a little or a lot.

So here is the napkin. Take your cafe number. Then take the price of a genuinely good tin — not the cheapest thing on the shelf, but real first-harvest, vividly green, delicious-straight matcha, the kind Part Two taught you to find — and divide it by fifteen. Do it with your local prices, today's prices, the actual tin in the actual shop.

I have done this sum in every city I have visited long enough to buy tea in, and the answer always lands in the same territory: per bowl, matcha made at home costs a fraction of the cafe version — and usually a small fraction, even when the tin itself made you inhale slightly at the till. The tin *feels* expensive because you pay for fifteen bowls at once and hold them in one hand. The cafe drink *feels* cheap because you pay for one bowl at a time. Feelings are not arithmetic. Per bowl, it is not close, and it is not close even before you notice that the home bowl was made from better tea than most cafes use — because a cafe, sensibly enough, buys robust culinary matcha by the kilogram to survive the milk, while your tin was chosen to be drunk straight.

And if your habit is daily, the ratio stops being trivia and starts being money. One drink a day is thirty drinks a month. Run both columns for a month — thirty receipts against a tin and a half — and the difference is no longer the price of a snack; it is the kind of sum you could do something else with. Run it for a year and it quietly becomes the most expensive thing about the cafe habit: not any single visit, but the multiplication.

Let me be careful here, because this is exactly where a lesser chapter would start moralizing, and I refuse. The cafe is not a mistake. You are paying for craft, for a third place that is neither home nor work, for someone else doing the washing-up, for the pleasure of being served. Those are real goods and buying them is not weakness. The point of the napkin is not *never go out*. The point is narrower and more useful: **the everyday bowl — the one you drink on an ordinary Tuesday because you simply want matcha — is dramatically cheaper to make than to buy, with no sacrifice in quality whatsoever.** Keep the cafe for the days the cafe is the point. Bring the Tuesdays home.

## What you actually need

The second half of the home-economics story is equipment, and it is where tea shops make their margin and beginners lose their nerve. Walk into a well-stocked shop, or worse, open a "matcha starter" listing online, and you will meet the full array: the whisk, the whisk stand, the bowl with the artist's seal, the bamboo ladle, the linen cloth, the ceramic whisk rest, the little canister spoon, the tray to arrange them all on. Photographed together on pale wood, they look like the entry fee to a beautiful life.

They are lovely. Most of them are also, for making tea in your kitchen, unnecessary — and I say that as someone who owns several and regrets none, because I bought them *later*, for love, knowing exactly what they were. Chapter twenty-two ranks all of this properly, but the short version fits in four lines. Here is the honest starter kit, complete:

- **A small fine-mesh sifter.** The single biggest upgrade to your tea, and the least glamorous. Any little strainer works.
- **Something to whisk with.** The bamboo chasen (茶筅) is genuinely the best tool for the job and a decent one is modest money — or a small electric milk frother, which makes a respectable bowl and an excellent latte.
- **Any wide bowl.** Wider than your spread hand, deeper than your thumb. A cereal bowl passes. A mug fails — the walls trap the whisk.
- **One tin of honest matcha.** The only item on this list where quality truly lives.

That is everything. Two of the four are probably in your kitchen already, and the whole kit — tea included — commonly costs about what a week of daily cafe drinks does. After that, the tin is the only recurring cost you have. The ladle, the stand, the sealed bowl: those belong to the ceremony, which is a genuine and beautiful thing that Part Seven will treat with respect, and precisely none of them changes what ends up in your mouth. Buy them the way you would buy flowers — because they please you — and never because a checkout page implied your tea would be lesser without them.

## Where spending more stops working

There is a ceiling on useful spending in matcha, and it sits lower than the market would like you to believe. We met the tea version of it in Part Two: past the point where a matcha is genuinely first-harvest, well ground, fresh, and delicious straight, further money buys refinements a beginner's palate cannot yet cash. The middle of the shelf is where the great leap in pleasure lives; the summit can wait until your tongue has learned enough to enjoy the view.

The equipment version of the ceiling is even blunter: **a more expensive chasen whisks the same.** The eighty-tine whisk carved by a famous workshop is a finer object than the plain one — better made, longer-lived, nicer to hold — but the foam on your usucha cannot tell them apart, and neither, blindfolded, could you. The same goes for the bowl. A chawan by a known potter is art, and art is a fine thing to spend on, knowingly. But it holds tea exactly as well as the sturdy bowl from the housewares shop, and the tea does not know the difference. In matcha, once past the honest minimum, extra money spent on *tea* buys diminishing but real returns; extra money spent on *equipment* buys aesthetics and durability only. Know which purchase you are making, and make it on purpose.

## The two-tin answer

So where should the money actually go? After all the sums, the optimum turns out to be almost comically simple, and I gave it away in Part Two: keep two tins.

- **A drinking tin.** Genuinely good, vividly green, first-harvest matcha that you enjoy straight. This is where your quality budget belongs, because every dimension of what you paid for lands directly on your tongue.
- **A culinary tin.** Robust, honest, later-harvest matcha for lattes, smoothies, cookies, and everything in Part Six — typically a fraction of the drinking tin's price per gram, and not a compromise but the correct tool, because milk and ovens erase exactly the subtleties the expensive tin carries.

Two tins cover every recipe in this book between them. The drinking tin, at a bowl most days, lasts two to three weeks; the culinary tin stretches further because baking sessions come in bursts. Buy small and buy again — matcha fades faster than coffee once opened, as chapter twelve explained, so the thirty-gram tin is not stinginess but engineering. A large bag of dying matcha is worse economics than a small tin of living matcha at twice the price per gram, because you will actually finish the tin while it is still worth drinking.

Notice what the two-tin system quietly does: it ends the standing-in-the-shop paralysis. You are no longer searching for one tea that must be good enough to sip and cheap enough to bake with — no such tea exists, which is why the search feels bad. You are buying two tools for two jobs, each excellent at its own, and the combined cost of both, spread across the bowls and bakes they yield, still lands far below the price of buying those same pleasures ready-made.

## The affordable summit

Now for the part of this chapter I have been looking forward to, because it is the thought that turns the arithmetic into something worth keeping.

In most of the world's pleasures, the genuinely good version is fenced off. The wine that critics describe with awe costs more per bottle than most people spend on wine in a season, and the gap between it and the everyday bottle is a canyon you mostly cross with money. Watches, audio equipment, restaurant tasting menus, first-growth anything: the pattern repeats. There is a version for ordinary budgets and a version for extraordinary ones, and the ceiling of the first sits far below the floor of the second. We are so used to this that we assume it is a law of nature: *the really good one is not for me.*

Matcha breaks the pattern, and it is worth pausing on how strange that is. The tea in your drinking tin — shaded three weeks, hand-picked in its first flush, steamed within hours, ground on granite at thirty grams an hour — is not the budget imitation of some unreachable original. It *is* the original. It is the same category of leaf, made the same slow way, as the tea whisked in the tearooms this book began in. The summit tiers above it exist, as we said, but they are refinements on this, not different things entirely. When you whisk a bowl from a genuinely fine tin, nobody anywhere is drinking a *fundamentally* better version of what you are drinking. There is no velvet rope. And the per-bowl cost of this — of the actual, unfenced, genuinely good thing — is a fraction of a cafe drink. Roughly the price, per day, of a small habit you would barely notice.

I find this quietly radical. We are told, correctly, that many of life's best things are free, and we notice, also correctly, that many of the rest are ruinous. Matcha lives in the neglected middle: a true luxury — slow to make, centuries deep, sensorially rich — priced, per serving, like a small everyday kindness. If you are the sort of person who builds their days on purpose, who believes that a life is mostly made of Tuesdays and that Tuesdays deserve furnishing, this is exactly the kind of asset you are looking for: a ninety-second ritual of genuine quality, repeatable daily, that compounds into pleasure rather than expense. The cafe habit compounds into receipts. The tin habit compounds into a skill, a shelf that smells faintly of spring, and a green bowl every morning that costs less than the worst coffee in town.

Ten ringgit, or whatever your local note is, buys one such bowl made by someone else. The same note, pointed at a tin, buys the better part of a week of them made by you — better tea, your own hands, no queue. Both are fair trades. Only one of them is a bargain.

## Closing the ledger

And with that, Part Four's work is done. We have followed the leaf through the marketplace: watched the latte carry it around the world, traced the pressure on the farms that grow it, learned to read a label like an insider instead of a target, and now settled the household accounts in its favor. You know what matcha costs, why it costs that, what it should cost you, and where every extra unit of your money does and does not work. The consumer-defense portion of this book is complete. You are, I am confident, no longer anyone's target.

Which means the fun can start.

Because there is a limit to how long a person should stand in the shop, reading tins, doing napkin arithmetic — and we have reached it. You own a sifter, or you will by the weekend. You have a whisk or a frother, a bowl wide enough to work in, and two tins with two jobs. Everything from here on is making things: the proper bowl first, whisked slowly and correctly, because all sixty-something recipes that follow are variations on it — then the lattes, the coolers, the sparkling things, the evening bowls with the caffeine dialed down, and on into the cakes.

Part Five is the kitchen. Warm the bowl. It is time to make tea.
