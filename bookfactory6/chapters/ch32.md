# The Long Watch

The coffee came at seven, as it had every working morning for nine years, and Mei Foong drank it standing at the end of the counter where the stool was. The stool lived pushed under the overhang of the glass and squared to the edge now, which took four seconds a morning to arrange and about the same to look at.

She laid the small tray out at three o'clock on Tuesdays and Thursdays for a month, out of some animal in her that would not learn, and then put the saucer of cheap opaque beads back in the drawer.

The airing came in the second week of January, as it did every year, because damp gets into thread and beetle into everything. She went up for the camphor chest last: blankets, tissue, the tin, and under the tin the indigo cloth with the wedding slippers in it.

There was one. She unwrapped the cloth to its corners and there was one — the left, worn to the shape of her own foot, sixteen years old that month.

She turned the shop out twice, and again at nine at night with a torch, on her knees; and then sat back on her heels and remembered, in order: a girl asleep on the shop floor with a flag in her hand; a household packing under a tarpaulin in the mansion yard; and the third of September, and a girl on the tailboard of the second lorry waving with both arms until the goldsmith's grille took her out of sight.

She never once, in the forty-six years remaining to her, asked the question out loud or in a letter.

*Dear husband. It takes me eleven minutes less to close the shop than it did in August, and I have counted them, being what I am. Somebody in this town has half a pair of my wedding shoes, and I hope whoever took them could not say why, because that is the only version I can live in. — Your wife, who is not short of work.*

---

She went up to Kuala Lumpur twice in 1958, twice in 1959, once in 1960, and after that when she was sent for.

It was a day's work each time: the early train with a basket, third class; the new house off the Ipoh road with its concrete pineapples on the gateposts, so much less handsome than the house of seven doors and so much easier to live in. Tea in the back sitting room with Bee Nio. Then twenty minutes of the girl, produced.

The twenty minutes were always shorter than the girl was. At fifteen Mei Lan had an accent with the corners taken off it and a great deal to say about a debating society and a beach at Port Dickson; and then somebody in another room wanted her, and she said, "Auntie Foong, don't go till I come back," and did not come back, twice out of four visits. At seventeen she wrote three letters a term, then two, then one at Christmas; at nineteen the letters became cards, and the cards became a printed card with a name signed under the printing. Mei Foong put each one flat in the drawer with the awl and the thread, and by 1963 the bundle was three-quarters of an inch thick and had stopped growing.

She understood the machinery of it and never said so. A girl moved to a capital at fourteen makes a new life, because that is what fourteen is for; and a woman given somebody else's child to keep alive spends her days arranging the furniture so that the child never stands near a mirror. Bee Nio managed the visits kindly and completely, and neither of them ever discussed it.

So the amah of the Lim household went from a Tuesday to a season to a name on a list, and became, somewhere in there, in her own daughter's memory, a woman from Malacca who had been let go when the family moved.

*Dear husband. Our daughter's accent has gone smooth in the middle, like a coin. She is reading economics, which is your father's revenge on all of us. When she was small she talked to me four hours a day; she now talks for twenty minutes, and I write down on the train what she said in case it is the last set. In that house I am introduced as the old amah from Malacca, which is accurate. — Your wife, third class, back the same day.*

---

Tan Hock Lye died in the March of 1961, in a chair, of a heart that had been a going concern for seventy-five years. They brought him back to Malacca, because the ground was here. Mei Foong walked in the procession where she had walked in his wife's, and stood where she had stood, which was near; and afterwards she cleaned her shop top to bottom for eleven hours.

The will was read in the capital and reported to her four months later by a woman pouring tea and pretending to be amused by it.

"There is a clause that has the cousins in a state. A fund in trust, for the education of the descendants of my son."

"His son."

"My brother. Who died in 'forty-two, unmarried, and left nobody. The cousins say his mind was going and the words should be read as his line generally; the trustees have decided that is the sensible construction and are paying school fees out of it, so my daughter's university is being paid for by a dead man's slip of the pen." She set the pot down. "He was eighty-one when he made it. It is a slip of the pen. Isn't it."

Mei Foong turned her cup on the saucer.

"I kept that man's books for nineteen years. He wrote loose in speech. He never wrote a loose word in a document in his life. Twice I brought him a draft with a word that could be read two ways, and twice he made me copy the whole sheet again and charged me the paper."

"He never knew about her," said Bee Nio, very low. "He was told the small half and no more."

"Yes. And then he went home and did arithmetic for thirteen years, and left a fund that fits exactly one child on earth, and made it look like an old man's mistake so nobody would ask which child. That is not a slip. That is how that man said sorry, and it is the only way he had, and he would have died before saying it in a room."

*Dear husband. Your father is dead and buried in the ground he chose, and I stood where I always stand. He has left money for the education of the descendants of his son. There are none, say the cousins, so they are having the sentence corrected by lawyers, and out of the correction our daughter's fees are paid, which is the joke, and I laughed alone in a kitchen. He got there without a word, and made sure it would be spent before anybody worked it out. — Your wife, who audited him for nineteen years and never caught him in a slip.*

---

The invitation came in 1965 in an envelope typed by a secretary, addressed to Miss Ho Mei Foong, Heeren Street, Malacca; and she read it twice and set it on the counter and did not go. A Malacca amah at a KL wedding, in a hall full of people who counted for entertainment; and an afternoon that belonged to the girl.

She sent the shoes instead — peony and phoenix, rust into gold, the pattern the town had begun to call the Malacca pattern as though nobody had made it up. In the third row of the phoenix tail on the left one, where no eye but hers would ever go looking, she set two beads of a green that belonged to no part of the design, and told nobody.

She sent the pen with them: the green mottled fountain pen that had been in her keeping since a February afternoon in the storeroom of another house, and that nobody in her shop had ever been permitted to touch. In the note she wrote: *This was your uncle's. A person who is going to run a household should have something to write with that she is not allowed to lose.*

The photographs came back in September: a bride in white at a hotel, and a smaller one from the tea ceremony, in the slippers.

*Dear husband. Our daughter was married on Saturday to a decent-seeming man in spectacles whose family are in transport. I made the shoes and I did not go. Do not argue with me; you were the one who wanted things said out loud, and I have had twenty-three years of proving what that costs. I have sent her your pen and did not say whose hand it came out of. She will keep it because it is good, and her children because she kept it, and by the time anybody asks, the story will have been sanded off it and it will simply be a pen a family keeps, which is how most of the past survives. — Your wife, who works with what is on the counter.*

---

There was a daughter in the KL house by the end of that decade, and then, some years on, a daughter's daughter, and Mei Foong learned of both by letter.

She was invited to the full-moon party of the second, and went, and was very nearly the first to arrive, having come up on a train that only ran once. It was in the garden off the Ipoh road: a marquee, a caterer, red eggs dyed a modern colour unlike any dye Third Aunt had owned. Bee Nio took her round the lawn by the elbow and introduced her four times, and by the second time the sentence had set: an old family friend, from the Malacca days.

She was a small straight-backed woman in grey at the edge of a group photograph, in a garden containing every living person she was related to by blood.

Her daughter crossed the marquee in a good dress, said, "Auntie Foong! You came all that way," put a hand on her arm for exactly as long as such a hand stays, and was called away about the ice.

She held the baby once, in the corridor between the kitchen and the sitting room, where the nurse carrying the child back from a feed had to set down a bottle and a cloth and a bag, and said, without looking properly at who was standing there, "Auntie, can you take her a moment."

She was three weeks and some days old, and heavier than she looked, in the way of dense work. She had a great deal of black hair standing up off her head like something startled, and her eyes were open, and she looked up at the ceiling with the terrible seriousness of the newly arrived, taking, it seemed, the inventory of a house.

Mei Foong stood in a passage in a stranger's house with her husband's great-granddaughter in her arms for the length of time it takes a woman to gather up a bottle, a cloth and a bag, which is about the length of a staircase; and said nothing at all; and gave her back. She left before the food, caught the last train, and was under her own shutter by half past eleven.

Then she took the tin out of the camphor chest and put it in order for the last time, because the order would have to do the work when there was nobody left to explain it. The photograph at the bottom, face down, with the fold in it, where a person going through an old woman's tin for something useful would never turn it over. Above that the drawstring bag she meant to put her mother's ring into when the time came; above that the wedding slipper in its indigo cloth, the left, worn to a foot; and on top of everything the letters in their bundles, tied by year with red thread, oldest at the bottom, because that was how an account was kept.

Before she laid the photograph in, she turned it face down on the counterpane and wrote on the back of it with a forty-cent school pen, in her small upright hand, the only instruction she ever left with anybody:

*For Mei Lan, when she asks.*

*Dear husband. Your line got to the fourth floor tonight and I was in the corridor when it happened. She has hair like a startled cat, she weighs what good work weighs, and she looked at the ceiling as though she meant to have it valued. I have been trying since the train to decide whether the length of a staircase is a great deal or nothing at all; it depends on whether you were expecting one. The tin is in order and I shall not disturb it again. The picture is at the bottom, face down, where nobody tidying will find it and anybody looking will. — Your wife, who had one instruction left in her and has written it down.*

---

The mansion was sold in the seventies to a company that wanted the land and not the house. Bee Nio came down for the clearing, and on the last afternoon she came into the shop carrying, with both arms and no dignity whatever, a birdcage: dark rattan, domed, an ivory ring at the crown, a carved base gone smooth as skin, older than the man who had been born in that house.

"They were going to give it to the man with the lorry," she said. "The lorry. Where do you want it."

They hung it in the airwell, where the rain came down the shaft in a fine mist, and Mei Foong wired the little door back, standing open.

"You are not going to put anything in it."

"No. There is no lock and nothing in it, and if something wants to sit in it for an hour it may, and if it wants to go it goes. That was the whole of the argument. It took the two of us three years to have, and we never finished it."

Bee Nio looked at the cage a while in the rain-light, with her handbag over her arm. "He wouldn't shut up about that bird," she said.

She came down monthly after that, and then oftener: mahjong on Thursdays with Siew Ean and two of the sisterhood's old beaders, lost with enormous grace, the fares miscounted in her own favour. She was the only person alive who could make Mei Foong laugh with her mouth open, and did it perhaps six times a year, and kept a tally.

---

The cancer was found in the January of 1979, and Bee Nio, who had been vain at sixteen and brave at twenty and complicit at twenty-one, dealt with it as she had dealt with the occupation, by saying the worst figure first and then getting on.

Mei Foong shut the shop for the last six weeks and went up. She would not have the guest room; she took the small room at the back with the ironing board in it, which was nearer. She did what she had done for the Bibik and for Ah Yoke: the sponging, the propping, the half-spoon of water, the numbers written in a red book on the hour. Mei Lan came at weekends and held her mother's hand and did not know that the two women in that room had held each other's line across thirty-six years.

It was ten past four in the morning of a Tuesday, with rain on the porch roof, and they were alone, the nurse having gone down for the flask. Bee Nio's hand moved on the blanket, and Mei Foong took it, and the grip that came back was the grip of a woman rowing.

"Foong."

"I am here."

"I am giving it back." Her eyes were shut and her voice was almost nothing, and every word of it arrived level, the way she had always been able to deliver a thing in a room when it mattered. "I return your daughter. Tell her yourself. I was never brave enough."

Mei Foong sat holding the hand. Outside, the rain went on doing what rain does on a porch roof at ten past four.

"You were frightened every day for thirty-six years," she said, "and you did it anyway. That is not the same as never."

"Tell her yourself," said Bee Nio again; and that was all; and about half an hour later she stopped, between one breath and the one that did not come, with her hand in Mei Foong's hand.

She kept the permission as she had kept everything: folded small, carried, not spent. At the funeral Mei Lan, thirty-six years old and running the whole thing with a clipboard, thanked her for coming all that way and said, "Mother was very fond of you," and Mei Foong said, "And I of her," and the two of them stood side by side a moment, looking at the same coffin from two different countries.

*Dear husband. Your sister died on Tuesday at about twenty to five with her hand in mine. At the end she gave me back a thing she was never able to owe, and told me to go and say it myself, and she was the only person entitled to say either sentence. So I have the permission now, signed by the only signature that ever mattered, and I have not spent it. Our daughter has two children, a mortgage and a mother she has just buried, and what I hold would take all of that apart and put nothing in its place. Perhaps in a year. — Your wife, who was never brave enough either.*

---

Dr Lim followed in 1983, at eighty-four, in his sleep, having outlived his own prognosis by thirty-one years by the method of ignoring it in eleven committees. He had written to her twice a year since 1957 on the practice's paper, about nothing: the weather, a colleague, a page of the newspaper he thought she would enjoy being angry about. In the last of them he wrote: *You and I are the only two left who were in the room. I find I do not want to be the last. It is a discourtesy to leave a colleague with the whole file.* He never mentioned the file otherwise, in twenty-six years, in either direction.

Afterwards there was a house full of people eating, and Mei Foong, who did not eat at these things, stood in the front hall out of the way of the caterers, and was not alone in it. A girl of about twelve in a black pinafore dress stood where the hall met the stairs, with the flat of her hand on the wall.

"It's cold," she said, to nobody; and then, discovering she had an audience, without embarrassment: "It's cold here and warm over there. Why?"

"Because there is nothing behind it there," said Mei Foong, "and something behind it here."

The child considered that, then went down the hall pressing her palm flat every two feet, mapping it, and came back and said, "There's a bit near the kitchen that sounds different if you knock it," and Mei Foong said, "Then somebody has closed a door up in that wall, and the plaster remembers, and it will go all the way to the ceiling." The two of them stood knocking on a wall in a house full of funeral guests until an adult came and took the child away to eat something.

*Dear husband. Your brother-in-law was buried on Thursday and there is nobody alive now who was in that room in 'forty-three but me. He wrote to me twice a year about the weather, and once, at the end, about the thing itself, in one line, like a man leaving a key on a table. At the funeral a child of twelve read the front hall with the flat of her hand and found a door bricked up since before her mother was born, and told me as though I would be interested, which I was. Nobody in that house knows what she is, including her. — Your wife, the last keeper, holding a permission she cannot spend.*

---

The trade went thin in the eighties — factory shoes, plastic uppers, brides in the capital who wanted white satin and got it — and then came back, thinly and strangely, on the arm of the tourists: a woman from a museum with a list of questions about a craft Mei Foong had never once thought of as a craft while she was feeding four people on it. Sister Kam had died in 1960 and her tobacco tins were still in use; Poh Choo never retired and never would; and at the window table sat Yen Ling, who had hands like Kwai Lan's in 1951.

Siew Ean died in 1996, at seventy-five, in an armchair with a book. She had kept the shop's books for forty-six years and raised its prices eleven times over its owner's objections, and she left Mei Foong her spectacles, which fitted, and her opinion of those prices, which did not.

That was the year Mei Foong walked up the street to Devan and Sons and made her will. The son had had her papers since 1964, when his father's hands went; he sat with a fountain pen and did not hurry her, and they dealt with the small sums in forty minutes.

"And the house," he said.

"The house goes to my husband's great-granddaughter." She had said it before she had decided how to say it; and the room did not fall in, and the man wrote a note on his pad.

"I shall need the name as it appears on her identity card."

She gave him the name, and the family, and where they lived, and watched him write it down without a flicker, the way his father had once written a deed and asked nothing.

"You may put down that she is the great-granddaughter of a friend."

"And the reason? Not for the will. For me. I shall be explaining this to a room."

Mei Foong looked past him to the window, where a trishaw man was arguing with the afternoon.

"Because houses need someone who reads them," she said. "The rest of that family will see a property with a bad roof three hours from where they work. That one puts her hand on a wall to find out what is behind it, and has done since she was twelve." She straightened her bag on her knees. "And it goes to her exactly as it stands. Nothing thrown, nothing sold, nothing put straight. Untidied — write that word down, please. I have spent sixty years making that house tidy, and I do not want the last of it carried off by a woman with a rubbish sack."

She bought her ground the same month, at Bukit Cina, wanting neither the good aspect nor the cheap corner but a few specific feet at the outer edge of an old family's cluster, below the last of the Tan stones and not among them. Near. Never inside. It cost more than the plot beside it and she paid without discussion.

*Dear husband. My friend died on Tuesday with a book in her lap and no fuss, which is exactly how she read a column and exactly how she did everything. I have made my will; the house goes to the child who knocks on walls. I gave the lawyer a reason, and it is true, and it is not the whole of it, which has been the arrangement of my entire life. I have also bought a piece of ground, and you would laugh at where: good money for the same six feet I have stood on since I was twelve, and this time nobody can move me out of them. — Your wife, who has outlived her auditor and does not recommend it.*

---

She kept the shop open until the August of 2004 and died in the November, at eighty-two, in the room above it, over four days that she conducted like the closing of a set of books — the ledger squared, the door off the latch; Poh Choo, seventy-one, sleeping on the floor by the bed and refusing to be argued with about it; Yen Ling reading the orders aloud because the light had gone; the drawstring bag with her mother's ring in it going into the tin under the letters on the second day, by her own hand, while she could still square a corner; three of the sisterhood down from the vegetarian hall, who said the words and did not fuss; and Raju, eighty, sending up the seven o'clock coffee each morning until there was no one to drink it. She went at about ten at night with her hand in an apprentice's hand, having outlived every person who had ever known what she was.

Mr Devan the younger opened his file the following week and did what was in it, which took eleven months and a search for a woman in Kuala Lumpur who had resigned from a firm and postponed a wedding in the same season; and on a wet afternoon he set a ring of keys with a worn leather tab on the desk in front of her, passed on the instruction that the house was to be given untidied, and offered no opinion, his father's firm having spent two generations not offering one.

The last sheet in the tin lay on top of the bundles, folded once, tied into no year, and alone of all of them unsigned.

*Dear husband — I am the last account open. I am closing the books. Our daughter is a grandmother. The slippers never fit anyone else. I checked.*
