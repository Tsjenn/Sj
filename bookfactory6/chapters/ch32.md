# The Long Watch

The coffee came at seven, as it had come every working morning for nine years, and Mei Foong drank it standing at the end of the counter where the stool was. The stool had been pushed under the overhang of the glass and squared to the edge, which was how it lived now, and which took, she had discovered, about four seconds a morning to arrange, and about the same to look at afterwards.

"My father says the shop is quiet," said Raju, setting the cup down. He was thirty-three and had a wife and a till of his own and still delivered the seven o'clock cup himself, on the grounds that it was the only errand in the day that could not be spoiled by a boy.

"The shop is not quiet. There are four of us in it."

"He did not say the room was quiet," said Raju, and went back down the five-foot way before she could think of an answer, which was a habit of his family's that she had never been able to break in either generation.

She had kept the Tuesdays and Thursdays for a month out of some animal in her that would not learn: the tray laid out at three, the small frame set beside the large one, the cheap opaque beads that were for mistakes turned out into their saucer. In February she stopped laying the tray and put the saucer back in the drawer, and the day went on being a day.

The airing of the beadwork came in the second week of January, as it did every January, because damp gets into thread and beetle gets into everything, and the whole shop's stock and the whole shop's history came out onto clean cloth in the light of the airwell for a morning. She went up for the camphor chest last, as she always did: the blankets, the tissue, the tin, and under the tin the indigo cloth with the wedding slippers in it.

There was one. She unwrapped the cloth to the corners and there was one — the left, worn to the shape of her own foot, sixteen years old that month.

She turned the shop out. She turned it out twice, with the girls sent home at noon on full pay and no explanation, and went through the camphor chest layer by layer, and the press, and the space behind the counter where a thing might slide, and the trunk of hired-work patterns, and the drawer, and then, at nine at night, the whole of it again in the dark with a torch, on her knees.

Then she sat back on her heels on the floor of her own upstairs room and remembered, plainly, in order: the thirtieth of August, a girl asleep on the shop floor with a flag in her hand; the two days after that of a household packing under a tarpaulin in the mansion yard; and the third of September, and the lorries, and a girl on the tailboard of the second one waving with both arms until the goldsmith's grille took her out of sight.

Everything was accounted for. She did not report the loss, because there was no one in Malacca to report it to who would not have wanted a name; and she never once, in the forty-six years remaining to her, asked the question in a letter or out loud.

*Dear husband. It takes me eleven minutes less to close the shop than it did in August, and I have counted them, being what I am. Somebody in this town has half a pair of my wedding shoes. I hope they are kept in tissue and I hope the person who took them cannot say why, because that is the only version of the story I can live in. — Your wife, who is not short of work.*

---

She went up to Kuala Lumpur twice in 1958, and twice in 1959, and once in 1960, and after that when she was sent for.

It was a day's work each time: the early train with a basket, third class, the country going by in rubber and then in tin and then in cement; a bus at the other end that she learned the number of; the new house off the Ipoh road with its lawn and its porch and its concrete pineapples on the gateposts, so much less handsome than the house of seven doors and so much easier to live in. Tea in the back sitting room with Bee Nio, an hour and a half of the family's business conducted at a good rate by two women who had learned to talk while working. Then twenty minutes of the girl, produced.

The twenty minutes were always the same and always shorter than the girl was. At fifteen Mei Lan had an accent with the corners taken off it and a satchel of examination papers and a friend called Doris whose father had a Ford Anglia, and she talked at speed, standing up, about a debating society and a chemistry mistress and a beach at Port Dickson, and then somebody in another room wanted her, and she said, "Auntie Foong, don't go till I come back," and did not come back, twice, out of four visits.

At seventeen she wrote three letters a term, on lined paper, in a hand that came down the page at an angle, and then two, and then one at Christmas. At nineteen she was at the university, and the letters became cards, and the cards became a printed card with a name signed under the printing. Mei Foong put each one in the drawer with the awl and the thread, flat, in order, and by 1963 the bundle was three-quarters of an inch thick and had stopped growing.

She understood the machinery of it perfectly and never said so, which was the only way she had ever been able to hold anything. A girl who has been moved to a capital at fourteen makes a new life, because that is what fourteen is for. And a woman who has been given somebody else's child to keep alive spends the rest of her life arranging the furniture so that the child never stands too near a mirror; and the woman who kept her before is a piece of the furniture that gets moved to the back of the house. Bee Nio managed the visits the way she managed everything, kindly and completely, and the arrangement was never once discussed by either of them, because both of them knew what it was, and the naming of it would have cost more than it bought.

So the amah of the Lim household went from a Tuesday to a season to a name on a list, and became, somewhere in there, in her own daughter's memory, a woman from Malacca who had been let go when the family moved.

*Dear husband. Our daughter's accent has gone smooth in the middle, like a coin. She is reading economics, which is your father's revenge on all of us. When she was small she talked to me for four hours a day and I did not once wonder why; she now talks to me for twenty minutes, and I have taken to writing down what she said on the train home, in case it is the last set. I am introduced in that house as the old amah from Malacca, which is accurate. — Your wife, who travels third class and comes back the same day.*

---

Tan Hock Lye died in the March of 1961, in a chair in a house he had lived in for four years, of a heart that had been a going concern for seventy-five.

They brought him back to Malacca to be buried, because the ground was here. Mei Foong walked in the procession where she had walked in his wife's, and stood where she had stood, which was near, and afterwards she cleaned her shop, top to bottom, for eleven hours, and the girls learned that week which questions were free.

The will was read in the capital and reported to her in the back sitting room off the Ipoh road, four months later, by a woman pouring tea and pretending to be amused.

"There is a clause," said Bee Nio, "that has the cousins in a state. There is a fund. It is to be kept in trust and it is for the education of the descendants of my son."

"His son."

"My brother. Who died in 'forty-two, unmarried, and left nobody. The lawyers have written three letters about it. One of the Muar cousins says the old man's mind was going at the end and the words should be read as his line generally, and the trustees have decided that is the sensible construction and are paying school fees out of it, so everyone is content, and my daughter's university is being paid for by a dead man's slip of the pen." She set the pot down. "He was eighty-one when he made it. It is a slip of the pen. Isn't it."

Mei Foong turned her cup a quarter turn on the saucer.

"I kept that man's books for nineteen years," she said. "He wrote loose in speech. He never wrote a loose word in a document in his life. Twice I brought him a draft with a word in it that could be read two ways and twice he made me copy the whole sheet again, and charged me the paper."

The two of them sat with it. Somewhere at the front of the house a boy was cutting the lawn with a hand mower, badly.

"He never knew about her," said Bee Nio at last, very low. "He never knew. He was told the small half and no more. You were there. You gave him the small half yourself."

"Yes," said Mei Foong. "And then he went home and did arithmetic for thirteen years, and left a fund that can only fit one child on earth, and made it look like an old man's mistake so that nobody would ever ask which child. That is not a slip. That is the way that man said sorry, and it is the only way he had, and he would have died before he said it in a room."

*Dear husband. Your father is dead and buried in the ground he chose, and I stood where I always stand. He has left money for the education of the descendants of his son. There are none, say the cousins, and they are having the sentence corrected by lawyers, and out of the correction our daughter's fees are being paid, which is the joke, and I laughed at it alone in a kitchen. He got there in the end, and he got there without a word, and he made sure it would be spent before anybody worked it out. I have thought about it for a week and I find I am not able to be angry with him this year. Next year, possibly. — Your wife, who audited him for nineteen years and never once caught him in a slip.*

The invitation came in 1965 in an envelope typed by a secretary and addressed to Miss Ho Mei Foong, Heeren Street, Malacca, and Mei Foong read it twice and set it on the counter and did not go.

She had considered it for one afternoon, honestly, on the grounds that a woman may be permitted to see her own child married. Then she had done what she always did, which was to walk the thing out to its far end and look at what stood there: a Malacca amah at a KL wedding, at a table with the household of a house that no longer existed, in a hall full of people who counted for entertainment; and a bride of twenty-two, and a photographer, and an afternoon that belonged to the girl.

She sent the shoes instead.

The order came through Bee Nio in the correct way, with a paper tracing of the foot and a deposit that was returned; peony and phoenix, rust into gold, the pattern the town had begun to call the Malacca pattern as though nobody had made it up. She worked them at night for six weeks. On the left one, in the third row of the phoenix tail, where the shading changed and no eye but hers would ever go looking, she set two beads of a green that belonged to no part of the design, and told nobody, and thought about it for the rest of her life.

She sent the pen with them.

It went in its own tissue in the corner of the box, the green mottled fountain pen that had been in her possession since a February afternoon in the storeroom of another house, and that no one in her shop had ever been permitted to touch; and in the note she wrote, in her small upright hand: *This was your uncle's. He gave it to me to keep. It has been used every night since, and it is a very good pen, and a person who is going to run a household should have something to write with that she is not allowed to lose.*

The photograph came back in September: a bride in white at a hotel, and beside it a smaller one from the tea ceremony, kneeling, in the slippers.

*Dear husband. Our daughter was married on Saturday to a decent-seeming man in spectacles whose family are in transport, and I made the shoes, and I did not go. Do not argue with me about it; you were always the one who wanted a thing said out loud, and I have had twenty-three years of proving it costs too much. I have sent her your pen. I did not say whose hand it came out of. She will keep it because it is good, and her children will keep it because she kept it, and by the time anybody asks, the story will have been sanded off it, and it will simply be a pen that a family keeps, which is how most of the past survives in most families. I am writing this with a school pen from the stationer's that cost forty cents. It does not mind. — Your wife, who has always been able to work with what is on the counter.*

---

The KL house had a daughter in it by the end of the decade, and then, some years on, a daughter's daughter, and Mei Foong learned of both by letter, one from Bee Nio and one from a secretary.

She was invited to the full-moon party of the second one, and she went, and she was very nearly the first to arrive, because she had come up on a train that only ran once.

It was in the garden off the Ipoh road: a marquee, a caterer, forty relatives of one kind and eleven of another, and red eggs dyed a modern colour that had nothing to do with any dye Third Aunt would have owned. Bee Nio, who was in her fifties and had gone handsome instead of pretty, took her round the edge of the lawn by the elbow and introduced her four times, and by the second time the sentence had set: an old family friend, from the Malacca days.

Nobody there had any reason to look at her twice, and nobody did. She was a small straight-backed woman in grey at the edge of a group photograph, holding her handbag in both hands, in a garden containing every living person she was related to by blood.

Her daughter was on the far side of the marquee in a good dress, being told about somebody's air-conditioning business, and caught her eye across it and came over and said, "Auntie Foong! You came all that way," and put a hand on her arm for exactly as long as such a hand stays, and was called away by a caterer with a question about the ice.

She held the baby once. It happened in the corridor between the kitchen and the sitting room, where the nurse was carrying the child back from a feed and had to set down a bottle and a cloth and a bag, and said, without looking properly at who was standing there, "Auntie, can you take her a moment," and put her into her arms.

She was three weeks and some days old, and heavier than she looked, in the way of dense work. She had a great deal of black hair standing up off her head like something startled, and a crease across the bridge of her nose, and her eyes were open, and she looked up and to the left at the ceiling of the corridor with the terrible seriousness of the newly arrived, taking, it seemed, the inventory of a house.

Mei Foong stood in a passage in a stranger's house with her son's great-granddaughter in her arms for the length of time it takes a woman to gather up a bottle, a cloth and a bag, which is about the length of a staircase, and did not say anything at all, and gave her back.

She left before the food, and caught the last train, and was in her own shop by half past eleven at night with the shutter down.

Then she went up and took the tin out of the camphor chest and set it on the bed under the window and put it in order for the last time.

The letters went in first, in their bundles, tied by year with red thread off the hank in the drawer; but she lifted them out again, because the order was wrong, and she wanted the order to do the work when there was nobody left to explain it. So: the photograph at the bottom, face down, with the fold in it, where a person going through an old woman's tin for something useful would never turn it over. Above that the drawstring bag she meant to put her mother's ring in when the time came, which was not yet. Above that the wedding slipper in its indigo cloth, the left, worn to a foot. And on top of everything the letters, which were the whole account, in order, oldest at the bottom, because that was how an account was kept and she had never in her life kept one any other way.

Before she laid the photograph in, she turned it over on the counterpane and took the forty-cent pen and wrote on the back of it, in her small upright hand, the only instruction she ever left with anybody:

*For Mei Lan, when she asks.*

*Dear husband. Your line got to the fourth floor tonight and I was in the corridor when it happened. She has hair like a startled cat and she weighs what a good piece of work weighs, and she looked at the ceiling as though she meant to have it valued. I held her while a nurse picked up a bag. I have been trying since the train to decide whether the length of a staircase is a great deal or nothing at all, and I find it depends entirely on whether you were expecting a staircase. I have put the tin in order tonight and I do not intend to disturb it again. The picture is at the bottom, face down, under everything, where nobody tidying will find it and anybody looking will. — Your wife, who has one instruction left in her and has now written it down.*

---

The mansion was sold in the seventies to a company that wanted the land and did not want the house, which stood empty afterwards for a long time with a hoarding over its face.

Bee Nio came down for the clearing, in a car with a driver, and took three days over what the auctioneers had left, and on the last afternoon she came into the shop carrying, with both arms and no dignity whatever, a birdcage.

It was dark rattan, domed, with an ivory ring at the crown and a carved base gone smooth as skin, and it was older than the man who had been born in that house and older than his father. The brass hook was in her handbag.

"They were going to give it to the man with the lorry," she said. "The lorry. Where do you want it."

They hung it in the airwell of the shophouse, on a hook driven into the beam by Raju's nephew, in the place where the rain came down the shaft in a fine mist and the light was best at eleven. Mei Foong took a length of soft wire out of the drawer and wired the little door back, standing open, and left it so.

"You are not going to put anything in it," said Bee Nio.

"No."

"Then what is it for?"

"It is for whatever comes," said Mei Foong. "There is no lock on it and there is nothing in it, and if something wants to sit in it for an hour it may, and if it wants to go it goes. That is the whole of the argument. It took the two of us three years to have it and we were seventeen and we never finished it."

Bee Nio looked at the cage for a while, in the rain-light, with her handbag over her arm.

"He wouldn't shut up about that bird," she said.

They were both fifty-odd by then; the street had a new name on a blue plate at the corner that neither of them ever used; the tourists had begun to come for the antiques two streets over, and Mei Foong had begun to be photographed at her own counter, twice a year, by young men from magazines who wanted the hands and not the face. Bee Nio came down monthly after that, and then oftener. She was better company as a widow-in-waiting than she had ever been as anything else. She played mahjong on Thursdays with Siew Ean and two of the sisterhood's old beaders and lost with enormous grace and cheated at counting the fares. She was the only person alive who could make Mei Foong laugh with her mouth open, and did it perhaps six times a year, and kept a private tally.

---

The cancer was found in the January of 1979 and Bee Nio, who had been vain at sixteen and brave at twenty and complicit at twenty-one, dealt with it the way she had dealt with the occupation, by saying the worst figure first and then getting on.

Mei Foong shut the shop for the last six weeks and went up. She would not have the guest room. She took the small room at the back with the ironing board in it, which was nearer, and she did what she had done for the Bibik and for Ah Yoke and for a child in a fever in a country house in 1945: the sponging, the propping, the half-spoon of water at the corner of the mouth, the numbers written down in a red book on the hour. Mei Lan came at weekends with her children and sat holding her mother's hand and did not know that the two women in the room had held each other's line across thirty-six years.

It was ten past four in the morning of a Tuesday, and there was rain on the porch roof, and they were alone because the nurse had gone down for the flask.

Bee Nio's hand moved on the blanket and Mei Foong took it, and the grip that came back was the grip of a woman rowing.

"Foong."

"I am here."

"I am giving it back." Her eyes were shut and her voice was almost nothing and every word of it arrived, level, the way she had always been able to deliver a thing in a room when it mattered. "I return your daughter. Tell her yourself. I was never brave enough."

Mei Foong sat holding the hand. Outside the rain went on doing what rain does on a porch roof at ten past four.

"You were brave in nineteen forty-three," she said, "and you have been frightened every day since, and you did it anyway for thirty-six years. That is not the same as never."

"Tell her yourself," said Bee Nio again, and that was all, and about half an hour later she stopped, between one breath and the one that did not come, with her hand in Mei Foong's hand.

She kept the permission the way she had kept everything: folded small, carried, not spent. At the funeral she stood where she stood. Mei Lan, thirty-six years old and running the whole thing with a clipboard, came and thanked her for coming all that way, and said, "Mother was very fond of you," and Mei Foong said, "And I of her," and the two of them stood side by side for a moment looking at the same coffin from two different countries.

*Dear husband. Your sister died on Tuesday at about twenty to five with her hand in mine. At the end she gave me back a thing she was never able to owe, and told me to go and say it myself, and she was the only person on earth entitled to say either sentence. So I have the permission now. It is thirty-six years old and it has been signed by the only signature that mattered and it is in my pocket and I am fifty-seven years old and I have not spent it. Our daughter has a clipboard and two children and a mortgage and a mother she has just buried. What I have is a story that would take all of that apart and put nothing in its place. Perhaps in a year. — Your wife, who was never brave enough either, and now knows it in her own handwriting.*

---

Dr Lim followed in 1983, at eighty-four, in his sleep, having outlived his own prognosis by thirty-one years by the simple method of ignoring it in eleven committees.

He had written to her twice a year since 1957, on the practice's paper, about nothing: the weather, a Seremban colleague, a page of the newspaper he thought she would enjoy being angry about. In the last of them, which came in the July, he wrote: *You and I are the only two left who were in the room. I find I do not want to be the last one. It is a discourtesy to leave a colleague with the whole file.* He never mentioned the file otherwise, in twenty-six years, in either direction.

His funeral was a large one, because a town doctor of fifty years' standing takes half the state with him. Afterwards there was a house full of people eating, and Mei Foong, who did not eat at these things, went and stood in the front hall of the KL house out of the way of the caterers, and found that she was not alone in it.

A girl of about twelve in a black pinafore dress was standing at the corner where the hall met the stairs with the flat of her hand on the wall.

"It's cold," she said, to nobody, and then, discovering she had an audience, without embarrassment: "It's cold here and it's warm over there. Why?"

"Because there is nothing behind it there," said Mei Foong, "and something behind it here."

The child considered that with enormous seriousness. Then she went down the hall pressing her palm flat every two feet, all the way to the door, mapping it; and she came back and looked up and said, "There's a bit near the kitchen that sounds different if you knock it," and Mei Foong said, "Then somebody has closed a door up in that wall, and the plaster remembers, and you will find it goes all the way up to the ceiling," and the two of them stood there, one of them eighty-one years old between them, knocking on a wall in a house full of funeral guests until an adult came and took the child away to eat something.

*Dear husband. Your brother-in-law was buried on Thursday and there is now nobody alive who was in that room in 'forty-three except me. He wrote to me twice a year for twenty-six years about the weather, and once, at the end, about the thing itself, in one line, like a man leaving a key on a table. At the funeral a child of about twelve read the front hall with the flat of her hand and found a door that has been bricked up since before her mother was born, and told me about it as though I would be interested, which I was. She is our daughter's granddaughter. Nobody in that house knows what she is, including her. It has begun to strike me as improbable that a family can lose a fact this size four times over. — Your wife, the last keeper, carrying a permission she cannot bring herself to spend.*

---

The trade went thin in the eighties, the way everyone had said it would since 1955 — factory shoes, plastic uppers, brides in Kuala Lumpur who wanted white satin and got it. Then it came back, thinly and strangely, on the arm of the tourists: a heritage association, a photograph in a magazine, a woman from a museum in the capital with a tape recorder and a list of questions about a craft that Mei Foong had never once thought of as a craft while she was feeding four people on it.

She was seventy that year and had two apprentices and a great-apprentice, Yen Ling, who was Poh Choo's sister's granddaughter and had hands like Kwai Lan's in 1951. Sister Kam had died in 1960 and her tobacco tins were still in use. Kwai Lan retired at sixty-two, with a shop of her own out at Klebang and a wall of lasts. Poh Choo never retired and never would.

Siew Ean died in 1996, in the school holidays, at seventy-five, of a stroke, in an armchair, with a book. She had kept the shop's books for forty-six years and had raised the prices eleven times over its owner's objections, and she left Mei Foong her spectacles, which fitted, and her opinion of those prices, which did not.

That was the year Mei Foong walked up the street to Devan and Sons and made her will.

The son had had her papers since 1964, when his father's hands went. He was not young himself by then and he sat with a fountain pen and did not hurry her, and they dealt with the small sums and the shop stock and the trust for the sisterhood's old beaders in about forty minutes.

"And the house," he said.

"The house goes to my son's great-granddaughter." She had said it before she had decided how to say it; and the room did not fall in, and the man wrote a note on his pad and looked up.

"Miss Ho. I will need the name as it appears on her identity card."

She gave him the name, and the family, and where they lived, and watched him write it down without a flicker, the way his father had once written down a deed and asked nothing.

"May I ask the relation, for the file?"

"You may put down that she is the great-granddaughter of a friend."

"And the reason?" he said. "Not for the will. For me. I shall be the one explaining this to a room."

Mei Foong looked past him at the window, where a trishaw man was arguing with the afternoon.

"Because houses need someone who reads them," she said. "The rest of that family will see a property with a bad roof three hours from where they work. That one puts her hand on a wall to find out what is behind it. She has been doing it since she was twelve." She straightened her bag on her knees. "And when it is done, the house is to go to her exactly as it stands. Everything in it. Nothing thrown, nothing sold, nothing put straight. Untidied. Write that word down, please. I shall have spent sixty years making that house tidy and I do not want the last of it taken away by a woman with a rubbish sack."

She bought her ground the same month, at Bukit Cina, and was particular about it in a way that surprised the man who sold it to her, since she wanted neither the good aspect nor the cheap corner: she wanted a specific few feet at the outer edge of an old family's cluster, near the low wall, some distance below the last of the Tan stones and not among them. Near. Never inside. It cost more than the plot next to it and she paid it without discussion, and told nobody, and had a photograph of the arrangement taken and put in the drawer with the awl and the thread.

*Dear husband. My friend died on Tuesday with a book in her lap and no fuss, which is exactly how she read a column and exactly how she did everything. Forty-six years she kept my accounts and she never once let me undercharge a rich woman. I have made my will. The house goes to the child who knocks on walls; I have given the lawyer a reason, and it is a true one, and it is not the whole one, and that has been the arrangement of my entire life so I see no cause to change it in the last chapter. I have bought a piece of ground. You would laugh at where. Near, never inside — I have paid good money for the same six feet I have stood on since I was twelve, and this time nobody can move me out of them. — Your wife, who has outlived her auditor and does not recommend it.*

---

She kept the shop open until the August of 2004, and died in the November, at eighty-two, in the room above it, over four days that she conducted like the closing of a set of books: the girls in and out, the ledger squared, the door left off the latch in the afternoons; Poh Choo, seventy-one, sleeping on the floor by the bed and refusing to be argued with about it; Yen Ling reading her the beading orders aloud because the light had gone; the sisterhood sending three of its own from the vegetarian hall on the last day, who came and sat and said the words and did not fuss; and Raju, eighty, who could not manage the stairs any more, sending up the coffee at seven each morning until there was no one to drink it. She died about ten at night with her hand in the hand of an apprentice, having outlived every person who had ever known what she was.

Mr Devan the younger opened his file the following week and did what was in it, which took eleven months and a search for a woman in Kuala Lumpur who had, it turned out, resigned from a firm and postponed a wedding in the same season; and on a wet afternoon he put a ring of keys with a worn leather tab on a desk in front of her, and passed on the instruction about the house being given untidied, and did not offer any opinion of his own, his father's firm having spent two generations not doing so.

The last sheet in the tin lay on top of the bundles, folded once, not tied into any year and not, alone of all of them, signed.

*Dear husband — I am the last account open. I am closing the books. Our daughter is a grandmother. The slippers never fit anyone else. I checked.*
