# Two Cradles

The pains came at dusk with the rain, and the child was not born until the rain of the second dawn, so that for the whole of one day and two nights the only sounds in the country house at Ayer Keroh were water on the atap roof and Mei Foong learning what her body had known all along it would one day be asked. The lamp was kept low and hooded, not for the aeroplanes now but for the neighbours; the Malay midwife in the kampong half a mile off was not sent for, because a midwife is a register with legs; and so there was only Ah Yoke, sleeves bound to the elbow, with the scalded string and the scalded scissors laid out on a clean cloth beside the smallest of the estate's tapping knives, scalded twice, because Ah Yoke did not trust a birth to fewer than two edges.

She had done this before. In Guangdong, in the sisterhood's house, in years she did not speak of, she had caught other women's children with these same hands, and the hands remembered, though they were seventy now and had spent the spring learning to hold cups again after the pneumonia. What the pneumonia had taken was not the hands but the wind behind them: twice in the first night she had to sit on the stool with her fists on her knees and breathe like a woman counting money, and Mei Foong, from inside the long red weather of her own body, heard the breathing and was more frightened of it than of anything happening to herself.

The child lay wrong. Ah Yoke found it out with her hands in the second night and said so in the flat voice she used for burst pipes and short weights, and then for an hour that Mei Foong afterward could not make lie straight in her memory — it had the shape of a fever, all middle, no ends — the old woman worked, kneading, turning, ordering her when to be still and when to push and once, sharply, when to stop pushing altogether, which was the hardest instruction ever given her in that house or any other. The rain went on. The lamp fluttered its small hooded flutter. Somewhere very far inside the pain Mei Foong heard herself asked if she wished to scream and heard herself answer, sensibly, that the walls were board and the night was quiet, and Ah Yoke said, "Then hold my wrist and break it if you like, I have another," and that wrist was what she held.

At dawn on the tenth of November the child came into Ah Yoke's hands, and was silent one heartbeat, two, three — the lamp-hood, the rain, the whole dark house leaning in — and then was not silent, and the sound she made was small and furious and workmanlike, the cry of someone arriving with a grievance and a list, and Ah Yoke laughed once, which Mei Foong had heard perhaps four times in her life.

A girl. Whole, loud, slicked and cross. They weighed her the only way the place allowed, in a sarong sling hung from the hook of the spring scale in the smoke shed, the scale that had weighed scrap rubber all its working life: five catties and something the needle couldn't decide. She fed the first time as if she had read about it and disapproved of amateurs. And when she was clean and wrapped and quiet, in the grey light coming up through the rain, Mei Foong looked into the small furious face and saw, laid down over the eyes as plainly as strokes on paper, her father's brow — two dark lines put down fast and certain, a quick writer's hand — and she did not weep, because she had done her weeping in another year, but she lay for a long time very still, the way you are still when someone has come into the room.

Ah Yoke looked at the brow too. She said nothing at all, and went to scald the knife again, which was itself a sentence.

In the exercise book that evening, below the day's number, Mei Foong wrote: *Born the tenth of November, in rain. Five catties. Strong.* And under it, after a while, a name. She gave the child the first character of her own name, because it was the only thing of hers the child would ever be able to carry in the open, and joined to it the character for orchid.

Mei Lan.

In Malacca that same month, the cradle came down from the storeroom behind the seventh door.

It was namwood, black with four generations of oil, carved along the hood with peonies, and it had held the Bibik's own children in its time — Bee Nio had slept her first year in it, and before her Boon Siew — and the Bibik supervised its descent down the back stair herself, one tread at a time, past the chalk marks the billeting inspections had left on the wall like a tide line. She had it washed with rainwater and oiled twice and set in the east room of the Lim house where the light came in the morning, and she laid one of her own old sarongs folded in the bottom of it, because in her practice a cradle should not stand waiting empty.

Bee Nio was in her sixth month then and carrying, Dr. Lim said, like a textbook. She had come through the terror-spring lighter by one grief already and had made of this second hope a thing she guarded the way the town guarded rice: without discussion. She walked every morning, ate what the ration and the black market and her mother's ingenuity could assemble, and sat still while her husband listened at her belly with the instrument going cold on her skin, watching his face for the change that came into it — every time, reliable as the tide — when he found the heartbeat: a man opening a letter and finding the money still inside.

The occupation went on around the waiting in its administered way. The firm's office had its supervisor, the administrator with his beautiful boredom, who initialled what he was given and pocketed what he was offered; the queues had their hours; the banana notes bought less each month with perfect regularity, so that even inflation felt like a schedule. The Bibik, who could not send love eight miles up a country road, sent dried longans instead, monthly, by the estate's bullock cart, packed with her own hands around a core of things not named on any list; and in the east room the cradle stood in the morning light with the folded sarong in it, waiting, the way furniture waits, with its whole attention.

---

At Ayer Keroh the story had been told once, to the kepala's wife, and then left to walk on its own, which good stories do. A cousin of the Malacca kitchen, widowed by the war, sent to the country to have her child away from the town's eyes and the town's prices. Nobody pressed it. The war had made so many such women that one more was arithmetic, not gossip; the estate fringe took her in the way it took in everything that year, without ceremony, on the understanding that everyone worked.

She had told them she was a widow, and did not know whether she was lying. That was the whole of her grief now, that not-knowing, and she kept it the way she kept the tin: closed, dry, within reach.

Everyone worked. The estate itself had stopped — rubber had no buyers, the tappers' knives no mornings — and the discipline of the rows had been handed over to hunger: tapioca planted down every avenue between the trees, sweet potato on the cleared banks, hill padi where a burned patch allowed it, so that the plantation's great green cathedral now had a floor of vegetables, aisle after aisle. The tapping knives, out of work like their owners, were reground on the estate stone into kitchen edges and weeding edges and one, the smallest, into the blade that had been scalded twice on the tenth of November. Ah Yoke traded needlework and quinine-knowledge and the deep majie credit of a woman visibly honest, and kept accounts of every borrowed cupful in her head to the grain. It was a life of small blades and smaller margins, and Mei Foong, who had audited a fortune in gold by lamplight, found that keeping a household alive on tapioca and barter used every skill the fortune had taught her and forgave none of the old errors. She was, for the first time since she was twelve, nobody's servant. It had taken a war, a false name, and eight miles of bad road.

The child slept in a sarong slung from the middle beam, with a cord run to Mei Foong's mat so that a hand could rock without a body rising. She grew the way strong children grow in poor years, against the odds and out of her mother's own substance: at one month she could hold a finger so hard it had to be peeled away joint by joint — "Greedy hands," said Ah Yoke, approving. "Weak babies are polite" — at three months she had opinions about lamplight and the smell of rain, and the brow above her eyes, those two fast strokes, had only settled deeper, so that certain expressions crossed her face like a hand Mei Foong knew crossing a page.

The mornings were hers. That was the shape of that year, though she never said it aloud, even to herself, in words that could be quoted back by fate: in the hour before the kampong stirred, in the grey when the rows of trees stood out of the mist like the pillars of some patient ruin, she fed her daughter on the back step with no eyes on her but Ah Yoke's, and was, entirely and in the open air, her mother. She did not sentimentalize the hour. She counted it. She counted everything — it was how she had been made — and she entered each morning in the ledger she kept where no auditor could go, and carried nothing forward, because some part of her, the part that had read the Towkay's contracts, understood that this was not income. It was principal, and she was living on it, and no one had told her the term of the loan, and everything she knew about the world said that terms are always shorter than the borrower believes.

In the evening of the hundredth day she took out the fountain pen and wrote the letter.

She had written to him once before, in another life, on a night in 1941 — written what could not be said aloud in that house, while he slept under the same roof and the saying was merely impossible, not yet absurd. This was different. This went where no post ran, and she wrote it anyway, in the small upright hand, because the accounts were in her name now and an account must render statements.

*Dear husband. You have a daughter. She was born on the tenth of November, in rain, and weighed five catties on the scale they use here for rubber, and she has your brow — do not be vain about it. She is called Mei Lan. She eats like your father drives a bargain and sleeps like your mother forgives, which is to say briefly, on conditions. I have opened an account in your name and I enter her in it daily. You told them to tell me the accounts are mine now. Very well. I am keeping them for two.*

She read it once, folded it small, and put it in the tin, where the letter of 1941 lay. The red thread went round twice. It was not sent, because there was nowhere to send it, and it was not unsendable the way people used the word, meaning too full of feeling to post. It was a statement of account, rendered to the other partner of the firm, wherever his office now was.

In Malacca, in the second month of the new year, Bee Nio's son stopped moving.

She knew it the way women know it, hours before she permitted the knowledge to have words: a stillness where a schedule had been, a morning appointment not kept, then a day of sugar-water and lying on her left side and pressing her own hands to her own body like a woman listening at a door. When she finally spoke it, in the evening, she spoke it to her husband in the level voice of the household she was born to — sentiment announcing itself as logistics — and Dr. Lim put his instrument to her skin, and moved it, and moved it again, and his face did not change at all, which was how she knew.

The hospital was half a hospital that year: wards requisitioned, medicines under lock and countersignature, the corridors patrolled by orderlies of the new administration with lists of their own. There was no other doctor free that night whom he trusted, and one whom he did not, and so Lim Cheng Hoe did what the textbooks in two languages advised against and delivered his own wife of his own son, at month eight, in the third hour of the morning, in a delivery room lit like a checkpoint.

He did everything in its correct order. The sisters who assisted said so afterward, said it for years, in the way the street keeps such accounts: that his voice never rose, that his hands never hurried, that he asked for each thing by its name and thanked the sister who passed it, that he attended to Bee Nio first and wholly and only then to the still small weight that had been laid, wrapped, in the cold scale-pan, because he was her doctor before he was anyone's father, and he did not once confuse the two. He wrote the record himself, sitting at the ward table with the form squared to the table's edge; and only once did his composure break, in the space where the form asked for the physician attending, where his pen laid down the first stroke of the word *father* and stopped, and was lifted, and wrote the correct word instead.

He carried the child to the mortuary himself, because the orderly on that corridor was the one he did not trust.

Bee Nio did not weep where the hospital could hear. She asked the necessary questions, put her signature where signatures were needed, and was brought home on the third day upright, correct, answering kindly — and was, to anyone who truly looked, a house with the roof gone: walls plumb, doors answering the hand, and everything inside standing open to the weather. The servant sent to carry the cradle back up to the storeroom came down again white-faced, having been spoken to on the stair; the cradle stayed where it stood, in the east room, in the morning light, with the folded sarong in it.

The Bibik was not seen at the market for a month. When she was seen again, the women who knew her reckoned privately that she had aged ten years between two new moons: the back that had been the house's plumb line had gone out of true, and the voice that had run seven doors' worth of household by decree now gave its orders in twos and threes, like a woman spending down a currency. She burned incense morning and evening. She said, to the altar, nothing that anyone was allowed to overhear. Of her two children's children who should by now have filled that house, three were ash or air, and the arithmetic stood on the altar with the smoke and would not be argued with.

In March, Dr. Lim came up the Ayer Keroh road on a borrowed motorcycle, with a travel pass that called him a doctor on his rounds, which was true, and a purpose he called checking on the household, which was most of the truth.

He listened for a long time to Ah Yoke's chest, made her walk the verandah twice, and told her what she already knew and had already dismissed: that the pneumonia had left a flag planted in her lungs, and that the flag would stand there now for good, and that stairs, wet clothes, and the month of December were to be treated as creditors. He left quinine, and a paper of sulfa powder folded like a pastry, and instructions she would obey in thirds. All this Mei Foong watched from the doorway with the baby on her hip, because there is no hiding a four-month-old child from a house guest, and because they had decided — she and Ah Yoke, in one exchanged look the night before — that the widow-cousin's baby had nothing to fear from a family doctor.

He examined Mei Lan last, on the table by the window, with the same order and gentleness he gave every child: hips, spine, the fontanelle under two fingers, the heart, the grip — she seized his thumb and he nodded to her gravely, colleague to colleague. He weighed her in his own sling scale and said the number aloud for the exercise book. And then, in the good light of the window, he looked at her face, and Mei Foong stood in the doorway and watched a man who had signed twenty years of birth dates do the small subtraction — November backward to February, February of 1942, the month the island fell, the month of the dawn tea and the hoarded dollar — and watched the two fast strokes of the brow close the account, all of it in one glance, in less time than it takes to say a name.

He looked at the child one beat too long. Then he did not look at Mei Foong at all, which was how she knew.

"A strong child," he said, to the room. He wrote nothing in his book. He drank the coffee they could offer him, praised the tapioca rows like a man praising a well-kept ledger, kissed the baby's head at the door because he kissed every baby's head at every door, and rode back down the red road with his bag on his knees; and for three months, to his wife, to the Bibik, to the Towkay, to anyone, he said nothing at all.

Mei Foong spent the first week of the three months listening for hoofbeats on the road, and then stopped, because she had grown up in the same house he had married into and she knew the sound of a kept silence. It was not mercy and it was not blindness. It was a man carrying something carefully, the way he carried everything, looking for the level place where it could be set down.

---

The talk in the firm's office that season was of tidiness.

The administrator had grown tidier as the war news worsened — the wireless said nothing, but the harbour said less and less, and men of his sort read harbours — and his tidiness expressed itself in inventories, in courtesies, and in a new attention to the shapes of households. He sat across the desk from the Towkay in April with the firm's books open between them and a bottle of pre-war brandy, lately a gift, standing unremarked at his elbow, and he turned the pages with one finger and spoke, as he liked to, in the tone of a man thinking aloud about nothing.

A fine family, the Tans. Old name, correct behaviour, accounts in order. And yet — the finger turned a page — untidy, if one stood back. The husband's house here, the doctor's house there, the old ones in the big house, and women scattered up-country besides; he understood there was a country property, servants, some cousin. Scattered women, said the administrator, with the mildness of a man reporting weather, made irregular households, and irregular households made work for clerks, and work for clerks became lists, and a list — here he closed the ledger gently, as if putting a child to bed — was a door that anyone might open after him, men with less patience for old names than he had. He said it without menace of any kind, which was how everyone in the room understood it to be a warning, and possibly, in his fashion, a kindness. Then he initialled the quarter's accounts, took up the brandy by the neck as an afterthought, and went to lunch.

---

The hardening reached the district in June, the way such things reached everywhere that year: not as an event but as a thickening. A checkpoint appeared at the junction where the estate road met the trunk road, sandbags and a barrier pole and two men with all day; the bullock carts learned new papers. Word walked up the rows from the kampong that the registration parties were working the district at last, house to house, heads counted against names, names against papers, a family a page; the kepala, who was a kind man with a wide definition of his flock, mentioned to Ah Yoke at the well, in the direction of no one, that the counting men wrote slowly and asked twice.

That night Mei Foong sat with the lamp hooded and audited her own story like a hostile clerk, item by item, and found that every entry held but one. The widow held: the war had made widows by the townful, and no clerk alive could disprove a husband taken by the screening. The cousin held; the kitchen court of the house of seven doors would swear to it, and had. She herself held — she had held for nine years under the Bibik's eye, which was sharper than any list. What did not hold was asleep in the sarong cradle with her fists above her head. A baby is a document; she had learned that much from the registry queues of the occupation, where every human being had been converted, methodically, into paper that must reconcile. A baby is a document issued by two parties, and hers named one, and the other line stood blank, and blank lines are what clerks exist to fill. A child with no lawful father was not a scandal now — the war had burned off scandal like morning mist. It was worse. It was an entry that did not balance, in a country that had begun again to check its sums; and a document like that does not survive a checkpoint.

She fed her daughter the next dawn in the grey between the pillared trees as she had fed her every dawn of her life, and the child, seven months old and eleven catties by the smoke-shed scale, gripped the cloth of her blouse with both fists now, a two-handed hold, and looked up at her under the two fast strokes of the brow with an expression of settled ownership. Mei Foong entered it all. She was aware, without any words attaching themselves to the awareness, that she had begun to memorize what she was doing while she was still doing it, which is a thing the body knows how to do before the mind consents to know why.

Dr. Lim came up the road again on the last Sunday of June.

He examined Ah Yoke, and the baby, and pronounced on both with his usual order, but the visit had another weight in it and nobody in the house pretended otherwise; and after the coffee he sat on the verandah with the two women in the smell of the evening — woodsmoke, wet red earth, the green breathing of the rows — and laid it out the way he laid out a diagnosis, worst first, plainly, without cruelty and without hope where hope would be a lie. The administrator's remark, retold word for word, because he had made the Towkay repeat it twice. The checkpoint at the junction. The registration parties, a fortnight away at their current pace, perhaps less. The country house had stopped being far away; distance was a paper thing now, and their paper was thin. Mei Foong must come back to Malacca — the household gathered, correct, one address, one list — and he did not say the rest of it, and the rest of it sat on the verandah with them in the dusk: that she could walk through any checkpoint in Malaya alone, and could not carry her daughter through a single one.

Nobody spoke for a while. Inside the house, off in the dark, the cord creaked once against the beam.

He stood at last, and took up his bag, and went down the steps to the motorcycle, and stopped, one hand on the handlebar, with his back half to them, in the exact posture of a man who has carried a thing for three months and found the level place. When he turned, his face was the face he wore in the delivery room, and he looked at Mei Foong and paid out his silence all at once.

"There is a way every one of you survives this," he said. "You will hate it. Come back as my patient."
