# Epilogue: The Amah's Daughter

The flags went up along the street on the twenty-ninth, two days early, because the man with the ladder was going to his daughter in Muar for the holiday itself and would not be back in time to do it properly. So by the morning of the thirty-first the whole terrace was already dressed — the little paper flags strung above the five-foot way, a big one over the kopitiam, three on the goldsmith's grille where two would have done — and Su-Lin rolled up the shutter at ten past seven into a street that looked like a street in a photograph of itself.

Merdeka Day, 2006. Forty-nine years. The radio in the coffee shop had been at it since six.

She swept the five-foot way because it was hers to sweep, and because Uncle Raju's coffee came at half past seven whether the shop was open or not and she liked to be found working. He sent it down with the boy — the boy being fifty-one — in the thick glass with the saucer over the top, and she drank it standing in the doorway looking at the house from outside, which she still did most mornings and expected to go on doing.

It was a modest thing, in the end. That had surprised everyone but her. The front room was the shop: the teak counter with the beads in their glass, the lasts on the wall, the window table where the light came in over the shoulder. Behind it the hall, and the altar, and the niche in the wall to the left of it standing open behind glass, with the paired slippers and the photograph above; then the air well, and the kitchen, and the stair. Twelve ringgit to come in and see it if you were a visitor and nothing at all if you were from the street, an arrangement Su-Lin had thought fair and Uncle Raju had thought insulting on both counts. The takings paid the electricity and some of Wei Ling's wages. The slippers paid the beaders. The trust's grant had paid for the roof, and the rest was time, and time she had.

She was thirty-five. She had a half-week in Kuala Lumpur that was still, technically, a career, and a half-week here that was not technically anything, and she had stopped explaining the arrangement to people who asked. Hafiz was in Kelantan until Friday looking at a mosque roof and had sent, at midnight, a photograph of a beam and no message, which was his entire correspondence style and which she had come to find restful.

At eight, Kwai Lan came, seventy-five and steady on the step, and after her Poh Choo, who was younger by six years and made a great deal of that. They hung their bags on the nail that had always held bags, sat down at the window table in the order they had sat in it for fifty years, and began. At half past eight Wei Ling arrived with her earphones in and the collar of the aunties' disapproval already braced for. At nine the first tour group of the holiday came past the door and looked in, and Kwai Lan did not raise her head, and the group photographed the two of them anyway, and Poh Choo told them ten ringgit each, and they laughed, and she did not.

At a quarter past nine her grandmother came down from the room above the shop and took the third chair at the window, which was hers, and which nobody sat in.

Mei Lan was sixty-three. She came down for four days a month and stayed sometimes longer, and had lately begun to arrive with a small bag of her own beads, which the aunties considered a criticism of the shop's stock and treated accordingly. She wore reading glasses now on a chain she despised. She put her left hand flat on the black cloth with the thumb tucked under, and Su-Lin had watched Kwai Lan see that, once, in March, and put down her needle and go out to the air well for a while.

---

The Ipoh people came at eleven, which was an hour after they had said and an hour before Su-Lin had expected.

There were four of them: the bride, her mother, an aunt, and a cousin with a video camera who filmed the shopfront, the shutter, the five-foot way tiles and eventually the ceiling fan. They had driven down the night before and stayed at one of the new hotels on the next street, the aunt reported, with a shower like standing under a monsoon. The bride was twenty-six, an accountant, married in nine days.

Su-Lin had the box out on the counter already. She checked the name in the order book before she lifted the lid, the way she did every time, in the green ink of a fountain pen that had come home to her in a shoebox from Kuala Lumpur, with its story sanded off and then sanded back on again: *Khoo. Ipoh. Peony-and-phoenix, rust and gold, black ground. Ten weeks.*

Khoo, of Ipoh. She had thought about it for one whole night when the order came, and had gone out the next morning and made polite enquiries of the mother by telephone about tin and about grandfathers, and had learned that the family had been in Ipoh a long time, and that there were an enormous number of Khoos in Ipoh, and that the great-grandfather had been a clerk. So: nothing. A common name from a tin town, arriving at a bead shop in Malacca as such names had arrived here for a hundred years. She had written it in the book and said nothing to anybody, and had thought, once, on the drive up the following week, that she would have liked to be able to tell one particular person, and that the woman in question — a schoolteacher with a tongue like a bill of lading, gone these many years — would have said something unforgivable about it and then paid for the coffee.

"Wah," said the aunt, at the counter. "So small the beads."

They always said that. Su-Lin lifted the tissue.

The slippers lay in the box the way finished work lies, faces up, the pattern reading true across the pair: the peonies opening rust into gold, the leaves in jade, the phoenix climbing away over the night-blue ground with its tail making the turn. Poh Choo had done the right vamp and Kwai Lan the left, which was a thing they had arranged between themselves without consulting anyone and which Su-Lin had learned not to interfere with. Mei Lan had sewn the linings down, both, because she was the fastest hand in the room now at a seam and everyone had privately agreed to be astonished about that in a year or two rather than immediately.

The bride's mother put her hand flat on her chest.

"Aiyoh," she said, and then, to her daughter, in a voice that had gone unreliable, "you see or not."

The bride was quiet. She looked at them for a good while. Then she said, "Can I put them on?" — and the whole window table put down its needles, because that was the question the room existed to be asked.

They brought the low stool. The cousin filmed. The bride sat with her mother holding her handbag and her shoes, and she put them on the way everybody does, one hand on the counter, the sole finding the shape of the last, and she stood up.

"Now walk," said Kwai Lan from the window, not unkindly. "To the door. Slow."

She walked. Down the long room past the glass cases with the beads sorted red to rose, gold, green, the deep blue, to the doorway and the white flat glare of the five-foot way, and turned, and came back with her chin lifting because it is impossible not to, over the same floor, under the same fan, where brides from Muar and Seremban and once from Penang had taken their first steps in this house for sixty years; where a woman who was never called a bride by anybody had knelt with a tape and a pencil, measuring the daughters of the mothers she had measured; where the tally of it all had been kept in a green pen by a person the family it belonged to had called staff. The bride went up and down twice. Her mother cried at the counter, comfortably, the way people do at things that have gone right.

Nobody in the room said what was under the linings. It was the house's habit now and it was written into the order book: every pair signed on the underside of the silk, two characters in night-blue, the maker's own, in the dark between silk and leather where no eye would meet them again. Wei Ling had asked, in her first month, what the point was of signing where nobody would see. Mei Lan had said, without looking up, that it was not for seeing, it was for the account, and had gone on counting; and the girl had accepted this the way the young accept the customs of a place they intend to stay in.

---

The bride's mother came to the window table afterwards, while the box was being tied.

She had recovered herself and she wanted, in the way of mothers at such moments, to distribute her gratitude thoroughly. She admired Poh Choo's work, and Kwai Lan's, and the tray of jade greens, and the lasts on the wall. Then she stopped at the third chair, where the old woman in the good grey blouse was seating beads along a leaf, and she looked from her to the young one at the counter with the pen, and she said, warmly, the obvious thing:

"And you — you are the owner's grandmother?"

Su-Lin, four steps away with the string in her hands, did not look up. She had learned that much.

Her grandmother's needle went in, and came out, and went in.

"No," Mei Lan said. She did not raise her eyes from the cloth. "I am the amah's daughter."

There was a small pause of the kind that opens when a sentence has more in it than the room has been given. The bride's mother said, "Ah," and then, uncertainly, kindly, "your mother worked here also, before?"

"My mother made slippers in this room for forty years," Mei Lan said. "Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen." She reached for the rust. "Whoever taught you to bead like that on your daughter's kebaya, tell her the picot is loose. It will not last the dinner."

"That was me," said the bride's mother, delighted to be found out.

"Then bring it tomorrow," said Mei Lan, "and I will show you."

They went at last with the box under the aunt's arm and the cousin filming the shutter again from outside, and the sound of them getting into the car came in off the five-foot way, and then the street noise closed over it. Kwai Lan said something to Poh Choo in Cantonese that Su-Lin did not catch and Poh Choo laughed until she had to take her glasses off. At the window her grandmother sat straight-backed in the light with her chin at an angle Su-Lin had seen in exactly one photograph, taken in a studio in the February that Singapore fell, of a young woman folded back out of her own wedding.

Nobody said anything about it. That was the house style, and it had been arrived at honestly, and it held.

---

They shut at four, because it was a holiday and because Uncle Raju had announced that everyone was eating at his place at five, an instruction rather than an invitation, and at eighty-one he had reached the age where you simply told people. Wei Ling swept. The aunties went off arm in arm to be scandalised by the price of something. Mei Lan went up to change out of the good blouse.

Su-Lin did the round she did every evening: cases locked, lamps checked, the fan off, the altar oil topped and the wicks trimmed with the little knife from the black-and-red box. In the hall she stopped at the glass, as she still did, and looked at the two slippers side by side on the shelf, left and right, and the photograph above them unfolded at last with both faces in it and the sixty-year crease running down between them like a seam that had been sewn rather than broken. Behind the glass in the open niche, on its square of indigo cloth, sat the tin.

Then she went out into the air well for the light.

It came down the shaft the way it had come down every evening of that house's life, over the pots and the drain and the wet brick, going the colour of weak tea as it fell. High up on the far side, on its hook, the bamboo cage turned very slightly in the moving air, its little door lashed open in a figure-eight of copper wire that had gone dull green over the year, the way she had tied it herself on the shop counter with a man watching and asking nothing.

She heard it before she saw it. The bubbling coo came off the roof line, unhurried, the tap-tap-tap and then the long low well of the note, and the merbok dropped down the shaft in three short falls and went in through the open door as though it were a doorway in a street it had always lived on. It drank from the porcelain cup that Wei Ling filled every morning under instruction and did not know the reason for. It shifted along the perch. It looked at the open door with one eye and then the other, the way birds must consider everything, in halves.

Then it sang, and went, and would be back in the morning or would not; it came and went as it pleased, and sang anyway, which was the whole of the arrangement.

Su-Lin stood in the air well until the light went, and then she locked the front, and left the one lamp burning over the photograph, and crossed the road to eat rice with the street on the country's birthday.

Behind her, in the dark hall, in its case, the tin sat closed. Its work was done: every letter in it read, transcribed, corrected in red ballpoint by an old teacher who had outlived four thousand students; every instruction in it carried out. Some inheritances are money, and some are houses. The good ones are instructions.
