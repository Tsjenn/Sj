# Rice and Ash

The new money came into the house in the hand of Third Aunt, who set it down on the kitchen table the way she would have set down a fish she did not trust. Three notes, damp from her waistband, printed in a brown that was almost the brown of dried blood, and on the largest of them a banana tree, drawn handsomely, with fruit.

"Money," she said, in the voice of a woman quoting somebody else's opinion.

Mei Foong picked the notes up and did with them what she did with every paper that came into the house that year: held them to the light. There was no watermark. There was a row of letters where a number should have been, and on one of the three notes no letters at all, and when she laid the two side by side she could find nothing to say which was the true one — and understood, standing in the kitchen court with the morning rice smell around her, that this was not a flaw in the money but the whole nature of it. Money that could not be told from its own forgery was money that had given up making the old promise. It did not say *the bank will honour this*. It said *spend me quickly*.

The town called it banana money before the paste was dry on the proclamations, and the town was right about it from the first week the way towns are. The old Straits dollars were called in — so much of the new for so much of the old, a rate announced with great correctness on the pasted notices — and the Towkay's firm changed its cash over at the appointed office and received, in exchange for money that had been backed by a hundred years of the world working a certain way, bricks of the brown notes bound in paper bands. Mei Foong carried them back through the streets in a market basket under a cloth, and nobody troubled her, and that too told her what the money was.

She was nineteen, and she had kept the household book since she was fourteen, and the book that year began to read like something written in a fever. Eggs at four cents, then nine, then fourteen by the Hungry Ghost month. Cloth doubling between the measuring and the paying. A cake of soap worth more in the afternoon than the morning. The columns still added; that was the terrible elegance of it; her figures were as neat as they had ever been, and they meant less every week, sums done perfectly in a language going deaf. In the end she did quietly what the market women had already done aloud. She ruled a second column beside the dollar column and kept the true accounts in rice — a thing was worth what it would fetch in kati of rice, and rice was worth your life, and that arithmetic held all the way down. Whoever found the book would see a servant girl's housekeeping. She knew it for what it was: the ledger of a currency, kept in the only coin left that could not be printed.

The queues she entered in neither column, though they governed the house more than money did. Rice came now by card, from the appointed shop, through the new associations that controlled it — a family registered, a card stamped, a measure handed over that had a way of weighing less each month while the stamp grew more elaborate — and the queue formed before light and stood through the heat, and the house of seven doors stood in it like everyone, because to be absent from a queue was itself a kind of statement, and the times punished statements. Ah Yoke's hoard behind the kitchen wall was drawn down grain by grain, secretly, folded into the ration so gradually that not even the kitchen girls could have sworn the house ate better than the street. That was Ah Yoke's law: survive quietly, and let the queue see you queue.

---

The firm was given its supervisor in the spring, and his name was Nagata.

He was a civilian, a man of perhaps fifty in a grey suit gone shiny at the elbows, and he had been nine years a clerk in a trading house in Singapore before the war, which meant he spoke the English of invoices and knew the price of smoked sheet rubber to the cent, and it was never clear to anyone whether those nine years had been what they seemed. He arrived on a Tuesday with a letter of appointment, bowed the exact depth the letter entitled him to, took the manager's room at the front of the firm's office, and hung his hat on the hook as a man does who has hung his hat on many hooks and expects to hang it on many more. He was not a monster. He was not a friend. He was a man serving out a war the way other men serve out a sentence, and what he wanted from Malacca, as far as Mei Foong could ever determine, was to leave it alive, somewhat richer, and without ever once having been made to hurry.

He stole with the regularity of a tide. Rubber stock was condemned as spoiled and left the godown at night, unspoiled; the book was instructed to say *destroyed by damp*, and said it; a portion of what the firm earned went into a drawer of his desk before any figure was written anywhere, and the figure that was written had been agreed between him and the drawer. He received gifts — whisky while there was whisky, quinine, once a Sunbeam bicycle lamp he was childishly pleased by — and gave in return the only currency he controlled, which was his signature, and his signature turned out to be worth more than the banana notes: passes, permits, exemptions, a stamp that made trouble look elsewhere.

But he came to the account room every Tuesday and sat for two hours over her books, and this, she came slowly to understand, was not supervision. It was rest. He checked her columns down and across with a dry finger, found them true, and something in his face settled that was never settled otherwise. "All day," he said once, capping his pen, "everyone lies to me. My superiors lie downward and my inferiors lie upward and I lie in both directions, and that is the work. This book adds up." He tapped it twice. "One honest thing in Malacca, and it is a girl's arithmetic."

Once, and only once, he took from his breast pocket a photograph and set it on the ledger between them: a small girl in Japan, solemn in school uniform, holding a certificate. "Also quick at figures," he said. "Second in her prefecture." Then he put it away, and never spoke of her again, and was Nagata again by the time the pocket was buttoned.

And once, early, going back through the pre-war volumes, he stopped at a page from the flood year where a second hand ran beside hers in the columns — a fast, definite hand, the strokes like a quick writer's brows — and asked whose it was.

"A clerk of the firm," Mei Foong said. "He is gone."

"February?" said Nagata, not looking up.

She did not answer. He turned the page and smoothed it flat.

"I was lent to the Johor side in February," he said, in the voice in which he complained about the heat and the train timetables. "For the lists. Very tiring work. In the end they give everything to clerks." And he went on down the column, finger by finger, to the total.

She sat quite still until he had finished, and made the tea, and poured it. That evening she washed the teacup twice, and could not have said what she was washing off, and did not try to say. He was survivable. That was the whole of him. In the second year of the occupation she would understand that *survivable* had become the highest word in her vocabulary, and that she had learned its grammar at that table, on Tuesdays, from a bored man who liked her books because they were the one thing in his day that did not lie.

---

The house learned to live around the hole in it, which is not the same as healing.

No word followed the fountain pen. Week upon week, no lorry returned, no list was posted with his name on it in either direction, and the house existed in the particular cruelty of that: grief without a date, mourning that could not be declared without making it true. The Bibik burned incense morning and evening and would not permit a tablet — a tablet was for the dead, and she would not have her son dead by inference. The Towkay went out three times more to the men who knew men, and came back the third time and did not go again. His gold had crossed every palm between Malacca and the holding camps, and what it had bought, at the end of all the buying, was certainty that there was nothing to buy. Some of the taels came back — the honester of the intermediaries returned what had purchased nothing — and Mei Foong unpicked the mattress seam by candle-end and entered them again in the exercise book, *returned: eleven*, and that was the saddest line she ever wrote in any ledger, because it meant the last door had been walked away from. The Towkay was correct at the checkpoints, correct with Nagata, correct at table, and quiet in a way that was new and had no bottom to it. He had spent his life believing everything had a price, and the belief had been the engine of him; and now he sat in the evenings by the silent wireless with his hands flat on his knees, a man whose one god had failed him in the only prayer he had ever made it.

And in the air well, in its cage, the merbok went on singing.

It was Boon Siew's bird; it had been his since he was fourteen, since the banana shared through the stair rails; he had fed it every morning of its life when he was in that house, and the first morning after the lorry nobody fed it at all, because feeding it was his, and taking the office of it was a sentence no one could bear to pronounce. By the second day Third Aunt fed it, scowling, without being asked, and after that it was fed the way shameful things are done in good houses — by whoever got there first, quickly, not spoken of. The song went up the air well all through that terrible spring, bright, ordinary, unbearable, the bird singing because it could do nothing else, and Mei Foong watched the household flinch from it one by one: the kitchen girls falling silent under it, the Bibik's hands stopping on her beads, the Towkay turning his face slightly away as he crossed the court, as a man turns from a draught.

On a morning in June the Bibik came down before the earliest of them, and stood in front of the cage for a time, and opened its door.

She told no one and explained nothing. The little door stood open on its wire hinge, and the bird looked at the open dark of it and did not go, and for three days it did not go, hopping to the gap and away again, singing from inside its open cage — and the house endured that too. On the fourth morning the cage was empty. For a while afterwards a merbok sang somewhere in the neighbour's rambutan tree, and every head in the kitchen court would lift, and then between one week and the next there was no song from anywhere, and that was all. The cage was not taken down. A kitchen girl, new that year, asked once whether it should be cleared away, and no one answered her, and it hung in the air well with its door standing open, and it was still hanging there, door open, sixty years later, for other hands to find.

The house's grief did not come singly. In the last week of March, in the raw weeks after the screening, Bee Nio lost the child she had carried since the autumn — the child she had refused Ayer Keroh for, the first grandchild, a month short of its name. Dr. Lim attended his own wife with a steadiness that fooled nobody, and the house did what that house did: closed a door, boiled what should be boiled, said the word *rest* many times because the true words were unsayable. Bee Nio lay two weeks in the shuttered room and came out of it thinner, older about the eyes, and precise, and if she wept she wept where no one held the account of it. The terror-spring had taken the brother and the child inside a single month. Nobody in that house ever set those two griefs side by side aloud. There was no need.

---

Mei Foong knew before she allowed herself to know, the way one hears rain long before one says *it is raining*.

It was the smell of belacan frying that told her first — the shrimp-paste reek she had lived beside for eight years turning, between one morning and the next, into a fist; she left the kueh bench with a steadiness that deceived everyone but Third Aunt's eyes and Ah Yoke's, and was sick, neatly and silently, in the drain by the well, and stood up and finished the pounding. Then it was the tiredness, a tide going out in her every afternoon. Then it was the almanac, consulted by candle-end in the amahs' room, and the dates counted on her fingers under the quilt, twice, in both directions, the way she checked any figure that could not be allowed to be wrong.

February. The one night, in the godown room, in the world's brief seam between the fall of one order and the arrival of the next. She lay in the dark with her hands flat on herself, on the future, and what she felt first, before any fear — she was honest in her own ledgers always — was a fierce, blazing, unlawful joy. Not everything on the lorry, then. Not everything gone north into silence. The accounts were in her name now, and here, under her two hands, was the principal.

Fear arrived in the morning, and stayed, because fear that year was simply accuracy. She told Ah Yoke on the stair, in four words, and Ah Yoke stood very still with the lamp in her hand and then said, "Sit," and the two of them sat on the stair in the dark and did together the arithmetic of it, item by item, as their kind did everything.

An unwed girl with child, in an ordinary year, in that street: shame, the Bibik's fury, dismissal at the worst — ruin of a survivable size. But the year was not ordinary. The town was a town of registrations now, of household lists pasted by the door, of inspections; a house answered for its members and a discrepancy was a hook, and hooks were what the informers fished with. She was married — married before the Guan Yin altar at dawn, tea offered to heaven and earth, witnessed, ringed, photographed — and her marriage existed before heaven, not before law; and the men who administered the law now did not answer to heaven, and the one witness to the vow was a servant, and the husband could never be named, because to name him was to hand the collaborator clerks the discovery that the traced and taken man had a wife and a child — a thread to pull, a family to squeeze, a hostage the length of her life. And below all of it, under everything, in the place where Ah Yoke's eyes would not go and went: the Bibik. The child was the house's own blood twice over, and to say so was to open the older grave, the erased sister, the oath — the truth was a set of doors each locked from the far side, and behind the last of them sat a girl of nineteen, pregnant, in an occupied town, in a house that must not know.

"So," said Ah Yoke at last, and the word had eight years and two countries in it. "We count what we hold. You hold the child. I hold you. It has been enough before."

That night, by candle-end, Mei Foong took his fountain pen from the account drawer and unscrewed the cap, and on a half-sheet of the firm's paper began the first of the letters that would fill a tin for sixty years.

*Dear husband. The accounts are in my name now, so I will render them. Hear the first entry.*

She wrote what could be said nowhere else on earth, and folded the sheet small, and put it — there being no address in this world to send it to — into the emptied English biscuit tin that had held Ah Yoke's needles, roses and huntsmen on the lid, and pressed the lid home. The post office of the dead. It took no stamp and lost nothing, ever.

---

Bee Nio was carrying again by the middle of the year, and the house lifted its head.

She told the Bibik in July and the Bibik wept, briefly and furiously, the only tears anyone had seen from her since the lorry, and burned so much incense that week that Third Aunt spoke darkly of the ration. Hope was rationed too that year, and here was hope on no card at all: a grandchild coming after the terror-spring, life doubling back on the house that death had been eating. Dr. Lim prescribed rest and was ignored, prescribed it again and was ignored with affection.

And the two young women of the house were sick together in the mornings, and that was the door it came through.

It began as kitchen mercy: ginger for two boiled in one pot, dry biscuits left on two shelves, Third Aunt's flat refusal to fry belacan before noon issued as general policy so that it protected without pointing. Bee Nio, week by queasy week, sought Mei Foong out — in the account room, in the queue where they stood together with their cards, on the back stair in the evenings — and the friendship that had run under a lifetime of *my girl*, under wedding slippers and top-to-toe nights and all the old half-ownership, came up at last into open water. Sickness is a leveller; grief is a greater one; they had shared both now. They talked in the new way of that year, in half-things. Bee Nio told her, in pieces, over weeks — once, in the worst piece, that in April there had been an hour when she was glad the first child would never see this world, and that she had been paying for the gladness every night since; Mei Foong took that piece and held it and gave her back no absolution, because Bee Nio wanted none, only a place to set it down. And Mei Foong, who could give nothing of her own truth away, gave what edges of it she could — that she grieved Boon Siew past a servant's portion; that she had a fear she could not name; that the fear had a future tense. Two women trading halves, each keeping the other's without asking for the whole. It could not last, and she knew it could not, because Bee Nio had her father's eyes for a column.

It ended, or began, on an evening in August, in the account room, under the one shaded lamp. Mei Foong was closing the week and Bee Nio sat across the table shelling the last of some little green beans for the kitchen, and had been quiet a long while, and then said, in the tone of a woman checking an invoice:

"You were sick Tuesday morning. And Friday week. Third Aunt curses the fish, but I ate the fish." A bean went into the bowl. "And you count on your fingers under the table when you think I am not looking, which you never do, because you are the only person in Malaya who does not need to. You are counting weeks." The last bean went in; she set the bowl aside; she looked up. "I have been sitting here deciding whether to ask you whose it is. I find I do not need to ask."

The lamp hissed. Mei Foong did not move. Everything she was stood still at once, the way the whole town had stood still behind its shutters, listening to bicycles.

Bee Nio's eyes went, slowly, deliberately, to the fountain pen lying in the spine of the open ledger — the pen the whole house had heard come back from the camp with its message, hand to hand, *tell Miss Ho the accounts are in her name now* — and Mei Foong watched her read it, all of it, the message that had been a love letter and a death notice and was now, in this lamplight, a third thing: a will.

"My brother's accounts," Bee Nio said, and reached across the ledger and took Mei Foong's hand.

Neither of them wept then; that came after, and briefly, and was not the point. Bee Nio held on with both hands, hard, the grip of someone catching a thing falling from a height, and what she said next she said as family head, though she was nineteen and it was not her office: "Then this is mine to keep. Do you understand me? Whatever must be done, whatever must be built around you — this is my house's account and I will keep it." And then, quietly, with the first unbroken smile since the spring: "He got something past Father after all. He would be unbearable about it."

She stood, and went out, and Mei Foong heard her on the stair, and could not imagine — sat frozen, imagining everything. But Bee Nio came back alone, carrying the camphor box, and set it on the table among the ledgers and lifted the lid: the wedding kasut manek, peony and phoenix, rust and gold on black, in their nest of tissue. She looked down at them for a moment — at the tissue, Mei Foong understood suddenly, at the folds of the tissue, which Bee Nio had made with her own hands after her own wedding and which some other hand, one dawn in February, had unmade and remade — and she said nothing at all. She took the slippers out and put them into Mei Foong's hands and closed Mei Foong's fingers over them, and the two women looked at each other across sixty thousand beads and one borrowed morning, and everything that was known was known, and none of it was said, and that was the vow.

---

Dr. Lim was told on a Sunday, in the back room of his clinic, with the shelves of dwindling bottles around them, and he listened the way he read a chart, and then polished his spectacles for a long time, which those who loved him knew was his way of clearing a space to be equal to something.

"Very well," he said at last. "Then hear it as I must hear it. A first confinement, in November, in a town where the hospital is theirs, under a name that invites questions, in a house the billeting officers walk through counting rooms." He set the spectacles back. "Medicine I can answer for. It is the paperwork of this world that kills now. So we will treat the paperwork."

The billeting inspections had come to Heeren Street twice that season — an officer, an interpreter, a clerk with a folder, walking the house of seven doors from front hall to kitchen court, counting rooms aloud, noting them, while the household stood in rows and the young women of the street, warned ahead by the swift telegraphy of the five-foot way, went quietly over back walls and through side doors with their hair bound up. Ah Yoke's old drill had grown a new door: at a certain knock, the kitchen court emptied itself of daughters. A pregnant girl could not go over walls. A birth in that house would enter registers, invite the clerk's folder, put a date beside a name for anyone who cared to count backwards from November to February and sideways to a traced man on a lorry. The house itself, the beloved house, had become the danger: too visible, too listed, too often walked through.

The answer stood a dozen miles inland, up on its piers among the young rubber, swept weekly by the watchman's wife: the bungalow at Ayer Keroh, bought in the year of the slippers, offered once to Bee Nio, empty since — waiting, as it turned out, for the one it had been waiting for. Quiet air, a working estate around it, no billets, no lists worth the paper, a district where the firm's name opened doors and closed mouths. Mei Foong would go for safety, as young women were sent for safety all over Malaya that year — the sentence explained itself; the times had made it ordinary. Ah Yoke would go with her, because an amah of her years went where her charge went, and no one would look twice.

The story they built for the world was built the way Ah Yoke built everything, out of true pieces set in a false wall. A marriage in the terror-lull, before an altar, in haste, as so many had married that spring — true. A husband taken by the war — true. His name a northern name, a dead clerk of the sisterhood's far kin, a man real enough to have existed and gone enough to answer no questions — and the girl a widow before she was a wife, sent inland to carry her child away from the town's eyes. The Bibik heard it from Ah Yoke, the two of them alone over the sewing, and asked the three sharp questions she would ask, and received Ah Yoke's answers, and believed them, because in forty years Ah Yoke had never once lied to her; and Mei Foong, told this afterwards, closed her eyes at the size of the payment being made on her behalf. The Towkay was told only what was safest and truest: safer for the young women, away from the inspections. He nodded, not lifting his eyes from his flat hands, and signed nothing, and asked nothing; asking had cost him what asking costs, and he was done with it.

The travel passes came from Nagata's drawer, in exchange for a bottle and a morning's patience. "Family matters," he said, stamping twice without reading. "The only kind left." He looked at her briefly over the papers. "The book will come back Tuesdays?" It was arranged that the book would come in Tuesdays with the estate cart. He seemed satisfied. It was the nearest he ever came to asking after her.

---

She left on a Thursday at first light, on the estate lorry with the charcoal burner on its tail, her rattan case in her hand — the same case, the very one, that had come up from the jetty in 1934 with everything she owned in the world, and went inland now with the tin in the bottom of it, under the quilt with the book sewn in its seam.

The household made no ceremony, because the story forbade one; a widowed seamstress-girl sent to the country did not get the seven doors opened for her. But the kitchen court was somehow all awake and all in the court at that hour, at their tasks, every task within sight of the gate: Third Aunt at the well, thunderous, pressing on her a parcel of kueh 'for the journey' that would have provisioned a junk; the kitchen girls; the syce; Bee Nio at the foot of the stair in her house-clothes, one hand resting flat and still on her own future, who said nothing at all and touched Mei Foong's wrist as she passed, twice, like a knock at a door between two rooms.

The Bibik was at the long table, packing dried longans.

She had been there some time; the paper twists lay ranked like taels; and she was doing it herself, with her own hands — the Bibik, who commanded and did not pack, filling a basket as her own mother must have filled baskets: longans for blood, red dates, a knot of old ginger, dried lily, a folded packet of the good gula melaka, each thing wrapped, each thing placed, the tenderness that had no door out of her anywhere else in this life going into the wrapping the way gold had gone into seams. She did not look up until it was done. Then she set the basket into Mei Foong's arms, and settled the cloth over it, and patted the cloth flat with both hands, and looked at her — one beat too long, as she had looked at a twelve-year-old at a gate — and said:

"Come back with a strong child. This house is short of blood."

She did not know what she was saying. She turned back to the table and began squaring away the unused paper, briskly, an old woman restoring order to a morning, while behind her the lorry took the girl, the case, the basket, the tin, and the house's whole unwitting blessing out through the gate and east into the young light towards Ayer Keroh; and Mei Foong rode with the basket in her lap and the sentence where the times had taught her to carry everything of weight — close, hidden, told to no one; like the book against her spine; like the child under her heart; like a stone.
