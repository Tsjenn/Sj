# The Merbok Cage

They climbed Bukit Cina in the dark, because the dead must be attended before the heat and the living must carry everything the attending required. Mei Foong had the second-heaviest basket, the one with the roast pork in its paper, and the smell of it rose past her face all the way up the hill like a soul going the wrong direction.

It was the third morning before Cheng Beng proper, the tomb-sweeping days, and the whole slope was awake. Lanterns and torch-beams moved among the graves above her, other families at other tombs, and the first smoke was already standing up straight in the windless dark the way smoke only stood at that hour. The Tan graves lay two-thirds of the way up, nine of them in a rough crescent, the oldest with its inscription worn to a shadow of a shadow, the newest — the Towkay's father — still sharp-edged, its red characters repainted every year by a man hired for the purpose. The graves were shaped like armchairs, like laps: a curved arm of plaster embracing a mound, a stone table set before each as though the dead kept house and might receive visitors. Which, one morning a year, they did.

The family came up behind the baskets in an order Mei Foong could have recited asleep. The Towkay first, in white sharkskin already going grey at the back with sweat, then the Bibik in a dark kebaya with her keys left home for once, then Boon Siew carrying the box of candles because the only son carries fire, then Bee Nio complaining about her shoes at a volume calibrated to be heard and not answered. Then the aunts. Then the amahs. Ah Yoke walked last of everyone with the teapot, because she trusted no one alive with the teapot.

The work came before the praying; that was the part Mei Foong liked. Three years now she had climbed this hill with them, and each year the same: the lalang grass to be cut back where it crept in with its sly edges that opened your fingers if you pulled it barehanded, the year's grey wash of mould to be scrubbed from the plaster arms, the little roof of each headstone swept with a stiff brush. Bee Nio deigned to press the coloured papers onto the mounds, red and yellow and green and white, weighting each with a pebble, until the graves looked flagged for a festival — which, Ah Yoke said, was the point: from the town below, a swept hill told everyone whose dead were loved. Boon Siew cut grass beside Mei Foong with his school-ruined hands, doing it badly, and she moved along behind him doing it again properly, and neither of them said anything about that, because it was the arrangement of their whole lives and needed no comment.

Then the food was set out on the stone tables — the pork, a boiled chicken with its head tucked under its wing like a sleeper, five kinds of kueh from the Bibik's own kitchen, little cups of tea and littler cups of samsu spirit — and the candles were lit, and the joss, and the family arranged itself by rank facing the ancestors, and the servants stood back among the trees, and the Towkay began the roll.

He was good at it. Mei Foong would grant him that always: whatever else the Towkay was, on this hill once a year he was a son. He named each grave aloud, who lay in it, what they had done, what they had come from — the great-great-grandfather who had come south with nothing and died owning two ships' worth of debts and one shop; the grandmother who had buried six children and raised five more; on and on, the family said backwards, wealth unwinding into hunger the further up the line he went. Boon Siew planted joss sticks at each mound. Bee Nio bowed with her eyes open. The paper gold went into the fire in sheaves — kim, the pressed gold-leaf squares that were money where the dead lived — and the heat of it pushed against Mei Foong's face from ten feet away, and the ash rose and unmade itself over Malacca.

Her own dead had no chair on this hill. Her mother lay in Kwangtung under whatever the village had afforded in the worst year anyone remembered, and no one would sweep her this morning, or press bright papers on her, or say what she had done and what she had come from. Mei Foong stood with her hands folded in front of her apron and did the sums a servant's face was built to hide. Fifteen years old; three of them on this hill with someone else's ancestors. She was not bitter about it, or she was so used to the bitterness that it had become a kind of furniture; but every Cheng Beng, at exactly this moment, the smoke found her eyes.

It was Ah Yoke who moved first, as she did every year. Off to the side, downhill of the family fire, the old amah crouched and built a small blaze of her own with an economy that would have shamed a match-seller, and burned a modest sheaf of silver paper — silver, not gold, the humbler currency — and said nothing over it at all. *For those far away,* was all she had ever offered when Mei Foong asked, the first year. *The dead can change money at the border. It is the living who cannot.* Mei Foong crouched beside her now and fed the little fire a sheet at a time, and thought of a grave she could not picture in a village she had been carried out of at twelve, and the two fires burned on the hill, the great one and the small one, and from the town below, she supposed, they would look like one smoke.

It was while the family fire was dying that she first heard the war mentioned that year. Two men at the neighbouring tomb, waiting for their own paper to burn down, were talking the way men talked at the edges of ceremonies, half-voiced, and the words that drifted over were *the north* and *Peiping* and *this year, next year* and then the oldest phrase in the world, *it will not stop there.* The Towkay heard it too. He looked out over the strait, where the light was coming up grey-pink behind the anchored ships, and said to no one in particular, in the voice of a man closing a ledger: "China's troubles are China's. We buried that debt when we buried him," and nodded at the oldest grave, and told the amahs to pack the baskets, the pork to be sliced at home for the servants' table, nothing wasted, the dead having eaten their share of the smell.

---

The war kept its appointment anyway. It arrived in July, in the papers, out of a place none of them had heard of — a bridge near Peiping with a foreign name, shooting in the dark, and by the time the Malacca papers had taught everyone to say *Lukouchiao*, the news had already outgrown the bridge. Through the wet heat of that month the town read standing up: at the kopitiam counters, at the medicine hall where the sinseh weighed out herbs with the Chinese daily spread beside the scales, in the arcade of the five-foot way where the newsboys had stopped calling headlines because the headlines called themselves.

In the house of seven doors, the news came in twice a day by different doors, and Mei Foong, who checked the invoices and so was allowed near the front hall's paper rack without explanation, watched the two arrivals like two tides. The English paper came in the morning and spoke of *incidents* and *spheres* and was read by the Towkay with his coffee, calmly, as a man reads weather happening to other people's ships. The Chinese paper came in the afternoon and was not calm. And in August the fighting reached Shanghai, which was not a bridge with a foreign name; Shanghai was a place half the street had walked, traded through, had cousins in. The photographs began then. Mei Foong stood in the back doorway of the front hall with a tray she had no urgent reason to be holding and looked at a picture of a burning street full of people carrying exactly what she had once carried out of a village, the bundle, the child, the nothing, and Boon Siew, seventeen, looked up from the paper and saw her seeing it, and neither of them arranged their faces in time. That was the first thing they ever shared that was not a banana through a stair rail or a sum in a margin. It was not a nice thing to share.

After Shanghai the town stopped only reading. Malacca was a Chinese town under an English flag with a Malay sea at its feet, and its China was not an idea; its China was grandmothers, creditors, ancestral villages, bones on hills. The relief drives began the way the monsoon began — a first scattered drumming, then everywhere at once. Collection tins wired to the counters of the kopitiams. Schoolgirls in white selling paper chrysanthemums on Saturday mornings, and woe to the shopkeeper seen not wearing one. A benefit wayang at the theatre street, tickets at ten times the price and the hall full anyway. The clan associations and the Chamber met and subscribed, and the Chinese paper printed the lists, name after name after name down whole columns, because the lists were the point: the money went to China, but the names stayed in Malacca, and everyone read them.

The Towkay gave five hundred dollars.

He gave it at the association hall on a Sunday, standing up when his name was called, in the white sharkskin suit, to applause that Mei Foong did not hear but heard about four separate times before nightfall, once from each aunt and twice from Bee Nio. The paper printed his name the following day in the second-highest tier of donors, and the page was cut out, and Mei Foong herself pasted it into the book where the household kept such things, between a wedding announcement and an advertisement for the firm. And on the Monday, because she now kept the small ledgers, the five hundred passed through her hands a second time as ink: *donation, China relief, $500* — entered, initialled, blotted. It sat on the page between the account for the Bentley's upkeep and the account for the August wages, and she ruled the line under it herself, and made no observation, even inside her own head, that a fifteen-year-old had any business making. The Towkay was not a mean man. He was a man who knew what everything cost and what everything bought, and five hundred dollars had bought China a little rice and Malacca a long memory, and both were real, and she ruled the line.

The kitchen court gave nothing that was printed anywhere.

She was part of it before she understood she was; that was how the kitchen did things. It was Third Aunt who kept the tin — an old condensed-milk tin with a slot cut in the lid, kept not on a counter but inside the rice bin, under the scoop, where no one idle would find it — and it filled the way a well fills, without witnesses. But a house is small at night. Mei Foong saw the washerwoman, who was owed money by half the street and paid by less than half, stand at the bin for a long moment and then put in the pair of gold-washed earrings she had worn every day of her working life, first wrapping them in a twist of paper so they would not ring against the coins, because the kitchen's rule, unspoken, was that giving must not make a sound. She saw one of the gardeners put in folded notes the day after she had heard him decline, on grounds of expense, to see the sinseh about his cough. And she was awake, pretending otherwise, on the night Ah Yoke sat on the edge of the pallet they shared, unpicked with a bodkin the waistband seam that Mei Foong was never supposed to have noticed, and counted out onto the blanket, in the moon-coloured light, twenty silver dollars.

Twenty. Mei Foong knew — because she knew every number in that house — what an amah kept of her wages after remittances, and how many years of keeping made twenty dollars. She watched through her lashes as Ah Yoke stacked them, unstacked them, sat with her hands over them for the space of perhaps thirty breaths — the only hesitation Mei Foong ever saw her make about anything — and then rolled eighteen of them into a cloth and sewed two back into the seam.

In the morning Mei Foong brought her own money to Ah Yoke rather than to the bin, because she wanted, in a way she could not have explained, a witness. It was three dollars and forty cents, tied in a handkerchief: the whole of what the Towkay's occasional small mercies for invoice-work had left her after her own twice-yearly letter to the village temple that saw to her mother's grave. Ah Yoke untied the handkerchief, counted it without touching it, took the three dollars, and pushed the forty cents back across the mat.

"The last coin is not yours to give," she said. "The last coin belongs to the day you run. Every woman keeps one." She said it the way she said *scrub the doorstep* — as procedure, as law long since settled elsewhere — and sewed the three dollars into the roll with her eighteen, and never spoke of it again. The list in the Chinese paper grew all that autumn, columns and columns of the front halls of Malacca. The rice bin was not in the paper. Mei Foong ruled no line under it in any book, and understood, without yet having words for the understanding, that she had now seen both kinds of giving there were, and that one of them was a name, and the other was a seam.

The argument happened at dinner in late August, on a night the wireless had been on in the front hall before the meal — the Towkay had bought the set in June, a cabinet of walnut and authority — and the bulletin out of Singapore had said *Shanghai* enough times that the word came to the table with them like an uninvited guest.

Mei Foong was serving. She had the rice ladle, which meant she stood the whole meal at the sideboard, refilling bowls as they were held out, present the way the lamp was present. She heard it all.

It began with the Towkay in a good mood, which was the dangerous kind. Rubber was up again; the war, he observed, spreading soup, was terrible for China and no bad thing for prices; a man could grieve and profit, the two accounts were kept in different books. Boon Siew put down his chopsticks.

He had been quiet for weeks in a way the house had misread as studiousness. Now he said, evenly enough at the start, that they should not talk about prices, because the war would not stay a price; that a country which was eating China would be hungrier afterwards, not less; that everything Japan's ships burned coal to fetch — the tin, the rubber, the oil — lay here, south, under a flag that was thin in exactly the ways this family knew flags could be thin. "We are the larder," he said. "Nobody spares the larder. They will come here, Father. Not this year. But they will come."

The table did what tables do. An aunt found something urgent in her bowl. Bee Nio's eyes went bright and interested, the way they did at any breakage. The Bibik said his name once, softly, meaning *water finds its level, boy, sit down in yours.*

The Towkay laughed. It was not a cruel laugh; that was the worst of it; it was comfortable. "The Straits are British," he said. "You might as well tell me the moon will fall on the padang. They are building at Singapore the greatest naval base under heaven — your own school takes you boys to admire it, I pay the subscription. Japan cannot swallow China in fifty years; you say so yourself every time you rattle that collection tin. And while she chokes on China she will cross an English sea, to fight an English fleet, for my rubber?" He held out his bowl, and Mei Foong stepped forward and filled it, and he was already looking away from his son. "Schoolboy talk. They teach you geography and you learn fright. Eat."

"Geography is the argument," Boon Siew said, but the table had moved on, the Bibik was asking an aunt about a wedding, the wireless mood was being tidied away like a stain, and he did not say anything else. Mei Foong, filling his bowl last, saw that his ears had gone red and that he did not eat what she gave him, and she found that she was angry, out of all proportion, at everyone present including him — at the Towkay for laughing, at the boy for stopping, at herself for standing there with a ladle while the only true thing said in that room all year was buried under wedding talk. She was fifteen. She had seen a country come apart once already. She knew which of them at that table had read the photographs right.

---

She met him on the back stair because of the bird.

It was past midnight, the house shut, and she had come down for the beans Third Aunt wanted soaked by morning, walking the dark by memory the way all the back of the house did — and at the turn of the amahs' stair, where it opened onto the air well of the kitchen court, there was a shape sitting on the third step with the merbok cage on the flags before him, and she nearly dropped the basin.

The cage lived by day at the top of a bamboo pole in the back court, hoisted up into the light the way the bird liked, the way all Malacca's merbok hung above its yards — the little zebra doves prized for their cooing, bought and bred and argued over by men at kopitiam tables, a good singer worth more than the month's wages of the woman who scrubbed under its pole. This one had belonged to Boon Siew's grandfather. The cage was older than the bird; it was older than Boon Siew; dark rattan ribs, a carved base gone smooth as skin, a brass hook polished by three generations of hands. The bird inside was the third bird to live in it. Boon Siew had fed it since he was a boy — had fed Mei Foong through the stair rail with its banana, her first week in that house, the first thing anyone in Malacca had ever given her.

He had lowered the pole and brought the cage in, and was sitting looking at it in the dark.

"It sang all afternoon," he said, quietly, as though they were already mid-conversation; and in some way that she understood better later, they were; they had been having this conversation in margins and stair rails and left-out newspapers for three years, and this was only the first time it had been said aloud, alone, in the dark, with no one to serve. "Did you hear it? While we were all listening to Shanghai on the wireless. It sat up there over the whole street and sang like the world was well."

"It sings every day," Mei Foong said. She set the basin down. She should have gone; every law of the house said go; she sat down on the second step, below him, with the cage between them and the small grey bird a soft blur behind the ribs of it.

"Because it can do nothing else," he said. "That's what the song is. It can't raise its young where it likes, can't cross the yard, can't do one thing its own shape was made for, so it does the one thing left and everyone calls it a singer." He said it without heat, which made it worse; he had had all evening to burn the heat off. "My father thinks it loves him. It doesn't know him from the pole. He thinks the singing means the bird is happy, and he thinks the same about everything under this roof, and he is wrong the same way about all of it." He looked at her for the first time. "I'm not going to live like that bird, Ah Foong. Sing nicely on the pole, marry where I'm hoisted, say the moon won't fall because it has never fallen on us before. I won't."

Mei Foong looked at the bird for a while. It had woken; it shifted on the perch, made the small contained sound a merbok makes at night, a coo folded down to almost nothing.

"The bird eats every day," she said.

He started to answer and she went on, because she had waited three years and now discovered the whole argument standing in her ready, like water behind a board. "Every day, it eats. It has never once been rained on. Nothing has ever hunted it. When it was sick last year the whole house turned upside down and your father paid the bird-seller to come and it was better in a week — there are children on this street who get less doctoring, I can name them. You look at the cage and see everything it cannot do. I came here on a ship because in my village we had a sky with no bars in it anywhere, all the freedom you like, and nothing to eat under it." She had never said this much to anyone in that house, not even Ah Yoke, and her own voice frightened her slightly, and she kept going. "You call the song a prison song. Maybe. But it is fed, and it is safe, and the cat in the next yard would take it in one afternoon of your freedom. That isn't nothing. Don't tell me it's nothing. Only someone who has never missed a meal can say that cage is nothing."

The air well held the silence the way it held rain, straight down between the walls, a column of it with the two of them at the bottom.

"You're right," he said at last. Not conceding; setting a stone down carefully next to hers. "And so am I. That's the trouble with it. That is exactly the trouble."

"I know," she said.

They sat with the trouble between them in its rattan ribs. Then Boon Siew leaned forward, and with one finger, slowly, so as not to startle the bird, he unlatched the little door of the cage and swung it open. An inch of open dark, bird-high, unbarred. The merbok looked at it. It shifted its feet on the perch, once, twice; it turned its head to the side, considering the gap with one eye and then the other, the way birds must consider everything, in halves; and it stayed exactly where it was, and after a while it tucked its head down and went back to sleep with its door standing open onto the whole night.

"See," he said softly.

"See," she said.

They meant entirely different things, and each heard the other's meaning perfectly, and that — though neither of them could have said so at the time — was the thing itself, the whole thing, arrived at fifteen and seventeen on a servants' stair: to disagree with someone all the way down and be understood all the way down, and find the second thing so much larger than the first that you would keep the argument going your whole life just to stay inside the understanding.

The scrape came from above them. Mei Foong looked up and Bee Nio was standing at the head of the back stair with a candle, in her nightdress, fourteen years old, her hair down her back.

How long she had been there, there was no telling. Long enough. The candle held perfectly steady — that was the detail Mei Foong kept, afterwards, when she turned the moment over: no wobble in the flame at all. Bee Nio looked at her brother sitting on the third step in the middle of the night; looked at the open door of the cage; looked at Mei Foong, on the second step, closer to both of them than any arrangement of that house permitted; and said nothing whatever. Her face did not accuse and did not smile. It simply took the whole picture in, deliberately, corner to corner, the way she took in a length of cloth she might want later — and then she turned, and the candlelight slid off the wall, and she was gone, and her door made no sound closing, which was somehow less comfortable than if it had slammed.

Boon Siew let out a breath and latched the little cage door shut. Neither of them said anything about it. Mei Foong picked up her basin and went to soak the beans, and her hands did the work while the rest of her stood on the stair still, hearing the argument over, and it was years before she fully understood what had been decided that night, and by which of the three of them.

---

Ah Yoke combed her hair that night as on every night, and that was when the story came.

It was their oldest ritual, older than Malacca: the wooden comb, the hundred strokes, Mei Foong sitting on the pallet's edge while behind her the old amah worked from crown to ends with the mechanical tenderness of hands that had done this ten thousand times — Ah Yoke, whose own hair had gone up into the sworn bun of the majie sisterhood decades ago and would never come down for anyone, combing out, night after night, this one head she had appointed herself to. Usually there was silence, or accounts of the day, delivered as failings. That night, twenty strokes in, without preface, Ah Yoke said:

"In Kwangtung, in a house bigger than this one, there were two sisters."

Mei Foong went very still. Stories from Ah Yoke came perhaps twice a year and were never only stories.

"They were close the way stairs are close," Ah Yoke said. The comb kept its slow time, stroke and stroke. "One step above, one step below, made from the same wood. They ground the rice-flour together every festival from the time they could stand. The elder had the obedient wrist; the younger had the clever one. You could tell their work apart by that — the elder's perfect because she was told, the younger's perfect because she couldn't bear otherwise." Stroke, and stroke. "The family made a marriage for the elder, and she stayed, and did what staying is. Kept the name. Kept the recipes. Kept the door. And her whole life was in that house, sixty years of it, and not one hour of it was hers, and she would have told you — she would tell you still — that she chose it."

"And the younger?" Mei Foong asked, when the pause had gone on one stroke too long.

"The younger loved where no one told her to," Ah Yoke said. "And the family said the word no, and she looked at the word, the way you would look at a door, deciding. And one morning in the fifth month, before the kitchen fires were lit, she took one bundle, and her mother's ring off the altar table — took back, she would have said, what was promised her — and she ran."

The comb stopped.

It stopped mid-stroke, at the crown, its teeth just resting against Mei Foong's scalp, and the whole small weight of the old woman's hand went still with it, and the room was so quiet that Mei Foong could hear the tide-sound of her own blood.

"And the one who ran—" Mei Foong prompted, barely aloud.

For a moment the hand on her head was not a comb-holding hand at all; it was just resting there, the way you rest a hand on a child in her sleep, or on something you are about to swear on.

"The oil is finished," Ah Yoke said. She drew the comb free, wound it in its cloth, and put it away in the case with the broken clasp. "Plait it yourself tonight. You're old enough."

She turned down the lamp and lay on her side with her face to the wall, and her breathing said asleep long before it was true, and she never finished the story — not that night, and not any night after, though Mei Foong asked twice more that year and was answered once with silence and once with a list of chores. Two sisters, a house bigger than this one, the obedient wrist and the clever one, the fifth month, the bundle, the ring, the word *no* looked at like a door. That was all of it there would be, for seven more years.

Mei Foong plaited her own hair in the dark, slowly, and while her hands worked she did with the story the only thing a girl who owned nothing could do with a thing given to her at night without explanation: she memorized it. Word by word, in Ah Yoke's exact order, with the stops in Ah Yoke's exact places — the way she memorized ledger columns, the way she had memorized the merbok's open door and the steady candle at the top of the stair — not because she understood what any of it was for, but because everything about the telling said that it was true, and everything about the stopping said that it was hers.
