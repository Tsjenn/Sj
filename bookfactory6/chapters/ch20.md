# The Exchange

Dr. Lim came back up the red road eight days later with a travel pass that called him a doctor on his rounds and, for the first time in all his visits, nobody in the house sick. He set his bag on the verandah table and did not open it, asked that Ah Yoke stay, and asked for the lamp though the light was only beginning to go; and Mei Foong understood that the consultation was to be for her whole life, and sat down across from him with her hands in her lap.

"I will give it to you the way I give a diagnosis," he said. "The worst first, and the whole of it, and no comfort until the end. Comfort that arrives early is a lie with its coat still on."

She nodded. Inside the house the cord creaked once against the beam and went still.

"Here is the worst. You would come into my house as the child's amah. Wages. A servant's room. Her washing in your hands, her meals, her fevers, her shoes. You would be within reach of her every day of her life — and mother to her never. Her first word will go to another woman while you are in the room. So will every word after it. I do not ask you to call that small. It is an amputation, and I am the surgeon telling you plainly: the limb, or the life."

He let that stand in the lamplight until it had been looked at from every side. Then he went on, in the same voice, the voice he used for charts.

"Now the mechanism. In February I delivered my own son and wrote his chart with my own hand, because records have been my discipline for twenty years. And then I did not carry the registration to the office. I put the form in my desk drawer and told myself: Monday. I have been telling myself Monday for five months. I believed it was the one dereliction of my working life, and I could not account for it, and it shamed me — until eight days ago, on this verandah, I understood what my hand had known before the rest of me. There is no entry. In the book the world keeps, my son was never born, and so he never died. The column stands open."

Mei Foong sat very still. Ah Yoke, on her stool by the door, did not move at all.

"The paperwork of an occupied town says what a household swears it says; I have watched three years of it. The paper will say: a daughter, born to my wife at the country house at Ayer Keroh in the eleventh month of last year, delivered by her own husband, registered late because the times are the times — half of Malaya is registered late. And from the hour the chop comes down she is as near untouchable as this world offers. A doctor's daughter. The Towkay's granddaughter. A name in the right column, a ration in her own right, a household no clerk profits by disturbing. When the counting men come, she will not be an entry that fails to balance. She will be the reason the page is turned."

"There is a paper in the district office," Mei Foong said. "With my name in it."

"There is." He did not look away. "I cannot make it not exist. Papers of this year go into drawers, and drawers into rooms, and the rooms belong to the war; it may burn, it may drown, it may sleep in the district book a hundred years. I can remove nothing. There is no version of this that removes it. There are only years in which nobody goes looking."

She took that and set it where she set everything that could not be helped, and asked the next question in order. "Bee Nio."

"It was hers before it was mine," he said. "We arrived at it in one evening, each waiting for the other to say it first, and in the end neither of us said it; we found it already said. She would not come tonight. She told me: a face can beg without leave, and yours must not. She will speak for herself when you have decided, and not one minute before."

"And in the house? Your mother-in-law buried the paper's story in February."

"She cannot be told the paper's story, no. She will be told a nearer lie: that the widow of the kitchen cannot keep a fatherless child safe in a counting year, and gives her daughter to a childless house to be its own, and comes with her as her amah; and that the paper was made to agree, the way papers this year are made to agree with whatever keeps a household off a list. It has the advantage of being nearly true, which is the only kind of lie that survives a family." He paused. "She will be glad, Mei Foong. I will not insult you by pretending her gladness will be a small thing to stand in a room with."

The lamp hissed. Out in the dark the rows of trees went on breathing.

"Last," he said, and stood, and took up his bag. "Understand the door. It locks behind her. A lie built to protect a child must last the length of the child; if we unsay it in five years or in twenty, we do not hand you back a daughter — we take from her a name, a family, a legitimacy, and hand her a scandal with a war's paperwork tangled round her feet. If you say yes, it is a yes for life. Hers, not yours. Hers is longer." He went down the steps into the smell of wet earth and stopped, one hand on the handlebar. "I will send no one for the answer. When you have it, send it down the road."

"You told me I would hate it," she said from the top step. "Do you?"

He considered it, exactly, the way he considered a chart.

"I am still counting," he said, and rode down into the dark.

---

The lamp was hooded, the house was quiet, the child slept in the sarong cradle with her fists above her head; and Mei Foong sat down at the table to argue with the dead.

She did the living first, because the living could be counted. Four doors. She had audited them a hundred times since June and she audited them again, item by item, a hostile clerk to her own hopes. Hide: the registration parties were a fortnight away at their old pace and nearer at their new one, and a baby was a document with a blank where a father must stand, and blanks were what clerks existed to fill. Run: the roads were checkpoints strung on string, the sea went one way and she had no papers for any of it, and her mother had tried running once, with fifty-one days' luck; the count did not improve. The truth: to name him was to hand the clerk who had pointed at the padang a widow and an heir to squeeze, and to open the older grave besides, the struck-off name, the oath — each lock still locked from the far side, exactly as it had been the night she audited it in the amahs' room, only now the house was occupied by counting men and the child was real and asleep three feet away. That left the doctor's door: everything itemized, everything priced, and the price her.

Then she let the ghosts in, one at a time, in order of age.

She began with her mother, because her mother had stood in this doorway before her. *You were erased for loving, and you answered erasure by sending me toward the blood that struck you off — near it, never told, a girl who eats. You chose eating for me. Look what I am offered for her: eating, standing, a name, a whole house — everything but me. You gave me away whole. I am asked to stay in the room and be given away in pieces, daily, for the rest of my life. Which of us drew the better bargain, mother?* — and she knew the answer even as she laid the accusation down. Her mother had died of not being offered this. Exiled, unanswered, eleven letters knocking at a shut door — if some doctor had come up the Kwangtung road and said *you may live at your daughter's elbow all her days, and watch her eat*, Guat Kim would have taken the bargain with both hands and called it heaven. From the far side of her own erasure her verdict came level as a measure: *girl, they are offering you the whole loaf of what I starved for. Take it, and do not look up from it.*

Her husband was harder, because her husband would have fought.

She could hear him — she had posted his letters, she knew his voice on paper and off it — *break it, refuse, run, the world that forbids you is burning, family is what you build.* And she gave him the stair answer, the old one, the one he had never beaten: *the bird eats every day.* He had lived by the open door — the printed letters, the refusal to sing small — and she had loved him for it and married him for it and would grieve it to her last morning; but he had died of the open door, and their daughter would not. *Family is what you build,* she said to him, in the ledger where he now kept his office. *Very well. Look at the building. Two mothers, a doctor, a grandmother's whole heart, walls of paper thicker than this war, and I build it out of the only timber I hold, which is myself. You built with that wood once. You do not get to grudge me the method, husband. The accounts are in my name.*

It was past the middle of the night when Ah Yoke came out to the table, carrying the red-thread bundle.

"You have been arguing," she said. "I could hear the quiet." She sat, and untied the knots — they gave slowly, remembering her fingers — and took out the child's slipper, the peony and the phoenix worked small as a prayer, the sole that had never once touched a floor, and set it between them in the ring of lamplight.

"Your mother made this for the child she gave away. I have carried it eleven years. Tonight it changes keepers, because tonight you become the second woman of that family to pay for a child with herself, and the second should hold the receipt of the first." She pushed it across the table an inch, no more, the way she pushed a settled account. "I will not advise you. I gave my advice thirteen years, one grain at a time, and it only ever said the one word: live. But I will testify, since I am the only witness left standing. I was asked once to raise a child near her blood and never tell. I said yes in a sickroom in Kwangtung, and I have kept it twenty-one years, and I will tell you exactly what it is, because nobody told me. It is work. Every day, at every meal, at every gate; it does not finish; the wage is watching her eat. I stood at your wedding one step back. I stood at her birth. I will stand at whatever comes, one step back and one to the left, to the end of my strength." She laid her worn hands flat on the table, palms down, the way she levelled a measure. "It can be done. I am proof. I am also the price. Look at me and choose with open eyes."

Mei Foong looked at her. She let herself do it fully, for the first time in nine years — the black-and-white, the servant's plait gone grey, the hands that had caught her daughter and her mother's daughter, the whole long unthanked arithmetic of a life spent one step back and one to the left — and Ah Yoke sat still under it and let herself be audited, hiding nothing, to the last line.

At dawn Mei Foong fed her daughter on the back step, in the grey between the pillared trees, as she had fed her every dawn of her life; and the child gripped her blouse with both fists and looked up at her from under the two fast strokes of the brow with her expression of settled ownership; and Mei Foong entered it all, and closed the morning, and carried nothing forward.

"Tell him yes," she said to Ah Yoke. "And tell him my terms. Three. Level, no seconds. One: I am never sent away. Her amah until she buries me or I bury her — no economy, no quarrel, no marriage, no peacetime ever dismisses me, and Bee Nio swears it to my face. Two: the name stays. They take the surname, the columns, the story, everything else. The name is Mei Lan. I named her and I paid for the name and it goes with her. Three: I keep the paper."

She said it with the sun not yet over the rubber, and she never in her life unsaid it.

---

She came down to Malacca on the estate lorry with a doctor's chit folded in her blouse — *Ho Mei Foong, widow, anaemia, under the care of Lim Cheng Hoe* — and her daughter asleep against her shoulder for the last eight miles she would ever carry her as her mother.

Bee Nio was waiting in the east room, beside the namwood cradle that had stood eight months in the morning light with a folded sarong in it. She had dressed plainly, and she did not reach for the child. She stood with her hands at her sides, and it could be seen what the standing cost her.

"I prepared five days of things to say," she said, "and I have thrown them all away except this. I will be given her first word. The word itself — the one that is yours — will be said to me, and I will answer to it, every day, in front of you, and I cannot refuse it even once, because refusing it once is the thread that unravels her. If you ever catch me treating that as small — if I am ever so much as graceful about it — then I have forgotten whose daughter I hold, and you have my leave to remind me by any means in this world." Her voice stayed level; her mother's training held; only her hands gave her away, and she let them. "I cannot give you back my brother. I can keep his daughter alive. Let me owe you what cannot be owed."

Nobody in that room took anything. Mei Foong crossed the floor and set her daughter into her cousin's arms herself — awake now, watchful under the fast dark brows — because if it was to be done at all it would be done as gifts are done, from the giver's own hands. Bee Nio received her the way she had once received a camphor box of slippers: as work another woman's whole self had gone into.

"It cannot be owed," Mei Foong said. "It can only be kept. Keep her."

That evening Dr. Lim asked, in the dispensary, about the Ayer Keroh certificate, and heard the third term, and said, "Burn it," once, as a doctor says a thing he is obliged to say. She had registered the birth in the district office in the twelfth month, under the widow story, walking in with the child bound to her; the clerk had heard the word *taken* and his brush had found other business, and the father's column had stayed empty — fear or mercy; that year the two wore each other's clothes. It was the only entry on this earth that said the child was hers.

"No," she said.

"A paper is a risk the length of its existence."

"A secret must have a living keeper," said Ah Yoke, from the doorway, "or it is only a thing that never happened. I taught her that. I stand by the lesson."

Dr. Lim polished his spectacles for a long time, which those who loved him knew was his way of clearing space to be equal to something. "Then keep it where no clerk goes," he said.

The vows were made before the Guan Yin altar in the hour before the house rose — the same altar, the same hour; the parallel was lost on no one and spoken of by no one. Four cups of tea, offered first to heaven and earth. Ah Yoke gave the measures, level, no seconds. One: in daylight the child is the daughter of the house of Lim, and no look, no syllable, in any year, before any person, says otherwise. Two: the truth has four keepers and takes no fifth. Three: when one keeper is left alive, the one decides. They drank. That was all. Four cups rinsed, dried, and back on the shelf before Third Aunt lit the first fire — the most binding contract Mei Foong witnessed in a lifetime of contracts, and the only one with no paper in it anywhere.

---

The Bibik was told on a Thursday, by Ah Yoke, over the sewing, and asked her three sharp questions, and received her answers; and on Friday the house of seven doors had every door open and Third Aunt was boiling eggs and grumbling about the price of dye.

"There has been no red egg in this house since before the war," the Bibik said, in the voice of decree that had been rationed for a year and was rationed no longer. "See to it. All of it. If heaven can remember this house, this house can remember its manners."

She held the child in the front hall in the good morning light, an old woman whose back had gone out of true straightening by the minute, and worked onto the small ankle a thin gold ring bought in another year for a first grandchild and never yet worn; and Mei Lan seized her finger with the two-handed hold and would not give it back, and the Bibik laughed — the sound crossing that hall for the first time since the lorry — and looked into the small cross face one beat too long.

"She has a look of the family," she said.

The room did not breathe.

"Children grow to the house that feeds them," the Bibik said, to the child, settling the matter as she settled everything. "Everyone knows it. You will be a Tan by the time you walk properly." She rocked her once, twice, with the ancient economy of a woman who had rocked a dynasty. "Heaven takes and heaven repays. I told them this house was short of blood. Heaven was listening. Heaven does not like to owe."

The Towkay came to the doorway and looked at the child the way he looked at contracts brought in by trusted men — briefly, taking the signature on faith — put one finger into the small fist for exactly a moment, and went back to the office. And when the child tired of gold and strangers and began to turn her head, hunting, the Bibik handed her across the room without a flicker.

"Take her, Foong. She wants her amah."

It was the first time the word was said in its new office. Mei Foong took her daughter — as she would take her from now on, at another's leave — and the child settled against her instantly, at home; and the Bibik watched the two of them with entire, unclouded satisfaction, restored, repaid, planning aloud the eggs and the ang ku and the visit to the temple; and of everything they had done and would ever do to her, Mei Foong thought, this joy was the cruelest and the kindest, and there was no ledger anywhere with columns for the difference.

---

On the night before registration day she took out the tin.

She unscrewed the fountain pen and sat with it a while and screwed it shut again. Some transactions a ledger cannot carry; the entry and the reason will not lie in the same line. The tin would go without a letter this year, and the next, and the one after — she did not know that yet; the silence knew it for her.

Then she took up the photograph. The one print, the hoarded dollar: the two of them before the studio man's vase of lilies, the borrowed kebaya, the beaded feet, his almost-smile, solemnity losing. She looked at it a long time by the bead of the lamp, and then she set the crease with her thumbnail along the line where their hands met, and folded herself back out of the frame. She did it slowly and evenly, the way she pressed a seam. Everything of the bride went into the dark behind the picture — the face, the kebaya, the morning — everything but the hand he held, which stayed. Let any clerk of any year to come find it and read what it said, which was all it would ever say: a groom, and a held hand, and a bride no one may name.

She laid it at the bottom of the tin, face down, and the letters over it, and pressed the lid home.

In the morning she walked up the street with a market basket. Two doors past the kopitiam smell the firm had a rental standing empty since the first months of the war, its tenant gone south ahead of the army and not come back; its key lived on the firm's board, and the firm's board answered to her. Inside, the house ran narrow and dark and long, room after room going away from the street; in the middle hall the altar recess stood swept and bare, the tenant's gods gone with the tenant, and flanking it, two small arched lamp niches with the soot-ghosts of old wicks in their throats — a dead oil lamp in the right, the left one empty. From under the greens in the basket she took the box: black lacquer, the size of two spread hands, Ah Yoke's once, given without a word. Inside it, folded flat at the bottom, lay the district paper — mother's name entered, father's column empty — and over the paper a square of silk, and on the silk the child's slipper, peony and phoenix, never walked in. She set the box level on the shelf of the left niche and squared it to the edge.

A hiding place, not a grave; she told herself so, and mostly believed it. *Paper is patient*, she had written once, in a braver hand, about a braver year. *It can wait.*

She locked the house, and bought kangkung on the way back, so that the basket should have had a reason.

---

Registration day was a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were Nagata's, and nothing survivable in that town was survivable without somebody bored having blessed it.

He went through the firm's book first, finger down, finger across, and found it true, and settled. Then Dr. Lim laid the declaration on the desk: a daughter, born at the country house at Ayer Keroh, the eleventh month of 1942; registration late, conditions being conditions; the father a doctor known to the administration; witnesses attached. Nagata did not ask how a doctor's daughter came to be eight months old before the registry heard her name. He was a man who checked columns for a living, and he checked nothing. He took up the stamp and brought it down twice without looking up.

"Family matters," he said. "The only kind left."

He had said it once before, stamping a girl out of the town; now he said it stamping a family into existence, in the same voice, the voice for heat and timetables; and Mei Foong, standing one step back and one to the left, could not tell — then or ever — whether it was a blessing, a receipt, or the one sentence of that year he entirely meant.

At the registry office a young clerk copied the declaration into the book at the counter, brush and chop, while the queue behind them shuffled its ration of patience. *There is no monster*, Ah Yoke had said once, of another book and another year. *There is a book, and a brush, and people who keep the book.* Mei Foong watched the brush go down the columns, unhurried, filling each one with what the paper before it swore. Mother: Tan Bee Nio. Father: Lim Cheng Hoe. Child — the surname first, as the form required — Lim Mei Lan. Nobody raised a voice; nobody touched the child; a column changed hands, that was all, and the ink dried while they waited for the copy. The whole of it, door to street, took one queue and eleven minutes at the counter, and was accomplished — the thing itself, the central transaction of all their lives — with less ceremony than the buying of a bicycle.

Outside, the street was about its ordinary business: the queues, the trishaws, the banana notes changing hands in fistfuls, the sentry at his corner with all day. Dr. Lim and Bee Nio walked ahead with the folded paper, at the parents' pace; Ah Yoke went behind them with the basket; and last, at the small distance that servants keep, Mei Foong carried the child.

Mei Lan had fallen asleep at the counter, mid-grievance, the way she did everything, completely; she slept on Mei Foong's shoulder with both fists closed in the cloth of her blouse, the two-handed hold, her breath going and coming against her mother's neck at the one distance no paper in the world could regulate. At the corner Mei Foong bowed with her, low, correct, held to the count of two, her back present — and for once, in the sun, in the open street, the whole of the rest of her present with it, holding everything she had bought and everything she had paid for it, which were the same thing, and asleep.

Then she straightened, and settled the small lawful weight of the doctor's daughter higher on her shoulder — documented, untouchable, fed — and walked on down Heeren Street behind the family, at the distance that servants keep, carrying her daughter home.
