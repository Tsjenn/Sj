# The House That Reads Letters

The lime came up the coast on a lorry on the first Tuesday of October, forty bags of it burnt from shell in a kiln that had been burning shell since before anybody's grandfather, and a drum of putty that had been slaking in somebody's pit for two years; and Hafiz took the lid off the drum in the five-foot way and made her put her hand in it.

It was cold. That was the first surprise — cold, and heavier than it looked, and silkier than anything that heavy had a right to be, closing over her wrist like cream left overnight, smelling of a beach at low tide and a wet blackboard. She stood in the doorway of her great-grandmother's shop with her arm in a drum of lime and understood that she was, for the first time in nine years of practice, entirely inside her own work.

"Two years in the pit," Hafiz said. "Three would be better. Ask your grandmother if she'll wait three."

"She'd ask why it costs more than cement."

"It costs less than cement. Cement costs later."

The wall proved him. Somebody in the seventies had rendered the shop front in a hard grey skim, and for thirty years the house had been trying to breathe through it. The old brick was soft and low-fired, made to take up damp in the wet months and give it back in the dry ones. Cement did not move, and was harder than the brick behind it, so when the wall wanted to shift it was the brick that gave; and the salt coming up out of the ground could not get out through the skim, and crystallised behind it, and ate the wall's face quietly into powder, leaving a tide-mark two feet up the front like the ghost of a flood that had never happened.

They took it off through the last wet weeks of October, and it came away in dinner-plates with the sound of a shell being cracked. Su-Lin did the recording herself, elevation by elevation, every patch of survival in blue and every square of loss in red, until she had a sheet that looked like a diagnosis and was one. Then the wall darkened two days, and on the third afternoon began to dry, patchily, from the top down: a hundred years of held water leaving a building at the speed buildings do things.

"There," said Hafiz, passing with a bucket. "That's it exhaling."

She wrote it in the site diary, crossed it out, and wrote it back in, because it was the only accurate description available.

The new plaster went on in three coats over the winter, worked with wooden floats, river sand graded through a kitchen sieve, and a bucket of the admixture that made the trust's young officer laugh aloud when he read the specification: gula melaka, dissolved and strained, a practice as old as the shell kilns. She worked two coats herself, badly at first, and her hands hurt for a week. The finished wall was not flat; it undulated very slightly under the light, the way old plaster does, and she caught herself running a palm along it whenever she passed, the way you touch the flank of an animal that is going to be all right.

She was, she noticed one morning in November, humming.

It was worth being honest about, so she was. For nine years she had been very good at work she did not love, and had assumed the flatness was the price of competence. This was the same skill in entirely different weather — measuring, recording, refusing to let a thing be covered before it was drawn — alive in her hands at last because the thing being measured was somebody she was trying to hear.

She kept a light diary that month, which was in nobody's works programme: three photographs a day from a taped mark on the airwell floor — ten, one, four — for four weeks, plotted on graph paper on the back of the door. The airwell was the house's lung and its one honest window, and it sent its light down the shaft in a column that swung off the back wall in the morning, across the hall at noon, and up the rear partition by four. So the deep middle of the house was never once in working light; and the light you could pass a needle in, that would let a woman of seventy see a bead the size of a mustard seed, lay in one band by the front window until half past four, and nowhere else.

The beading table would stand at the front window, then. She had suspected it for weeks on grounds of atmosphere and now had a month of photographs that said it on grounds of fact. Somebody had known this. Somebody had put four frames in that window in 1949 and worked round the sun for forty years and never written it down, because you do not write down what you can see.

The roof gave her the marks. The frame stayed open above the shop until December while a new purlin cured, and she went up most evenings. Up there, in the bat smell and the hot dark under the tiles, the house stopped being a shop and became what it was: a timber frame, jointed and pegged, dropped between two party walls built to hold it and nothing else.

The marks were on the undersides of the tie-beams, where nobody had looked since the raising: brushed on in something between red and brown. Strokes and hooks. A vertical; two verticals; a vertical crossed. She logged them, because logging what you do not understand is the whole discipline.

Teacher Yap looked at the photographs on a Thursday, over the counter, and made a sound of pure contempt.

"You have brought me a carpenter's arithmetic. This is not calligraphy, it is a man counting." He tapped the pictures with the red ballpoint. "One. Two. Six. Nine. The old shop numerals — any fish-seller's slate in this town had them when I was young. Quick, made for the hand, and nobody under sixty can read them now." He pushed his glasses up. "Why are you smiling?"

"Because they're in her ledgers."

She fetched the shop's day-book for 1953 to show him: Mei Foong's columns going down the page, dates and names and pairs and deposits, and there in the price column the same strokes and hooks, brushed by a woman sixty feet below and forty years after the man who numbered the roof. The frame had been cut and marked on the ground, every member lettered so that a crew could raise a house in a day without a drawing. Su-Lin sat on the joists that evening with the torch off. The carpenter had counted this house up out of a heap of timber; the beader had counted the town's brides through it in the same figures; and nobody had told either of them they were making a record.

---

The letters took the winter. She did them at the counter under the lamp, in Mei Foong's own order, which was by decade and by size and made sense once you stopped expecting chronology. Puan Aini sent down a supplier's list, and Su-Lin spent four hundred ringgit of her own money on unbuffered folders, tissue interleaving, and a document box the colour of oatmeal that cost more than a table. Each letter came out, was unfolded once, photographed, numbered on its folder and never on itself, transcribed, and went back.

Sixty-one of them. One from 1941, three from 1942, one from the January of 1943 with joy in it, then the two-year silence, then September 1945, and after that a slow file down the second half of the century — one a year, twice in some years, none in others, the gaps as legible as the writing. The last was 2004, in a hand that had begun at length to be an old woman's. Her own Chinese improved out of recognition in the particular way of a person learning a language from one hand: she could read Mei Foong now the way you read a friend's writing on an envelope across a room, and remained helpless with a newspaper. Teacher Yap found this hilarious and did nothing to correct it.

The museum got designed at the same table, in the gaps, on the backs of transcription sheets. She could have made a shrine; the trust would have approved a shrine, they take donations and photograph well. She could have made a period room with a mannequin at the frame, which was what the young officer kept sketching in the air. What she built came instead out of one line of Puan Aini's, delivered over a foam cradle in a cold room in a Dutch building and never since out of her head: *the record is the absence. That is not the archive failing. That is the archive telling you the truth.*

So the shop stayed a shop: four frames at the window, the trays under the glass as Sister Kam had sorted them in 1950, rust to rose, the wooden lasts back on their nails with the small ones at the end where the children's feet had been. A working atelier taking orders three days a week from anybody who came in with a foot. Nothing behind rope.

And in the ancestral hall, the wall.

She had numbered the bricks the night she opened it, eleven of them, course by course, so the niche could be closed exactly as it had been. They sat in their crate under the stairs all autumn, and she looked at the crate every day, and one evening in December she went to Hafiz's yard and told him she was not putting them back. He heard her out, and asked whether the trust would call it loss of fabric, and she read him the case standing up in the yard with the light going: that the blocking was not original but a wartime alteration; that it was itself the significant thing; that the meaning of this house lay in an eleven-brick decision taken by a woman under occupation and never revoked; and that closing it up to make the wall look nice would be, in conservation terms, an act of decoration.

"Decoration," Hafiz repeated, with relish. He was a man who could hold a grudge three weeks over a hairline crack, and had, and it had been earned, and it had finished. "You've been reading my letters to the trust."

"You've been reading my drawings."

"Put the bricks in the case with it. Numbered side out. Let them see the pencil."

So the niche stayed open, and got a sheet of glass in a slim brass frame that Hafiz's joiner made in four days; and behind the glass, on a cradle, the little arched dark eye of the wall held what it had been built to hold. The biscuit tin, English, roses and a hunting scene rubbed to ghosts, open, its lid propped beside it. The bundles inside under their red thread. The lacquered box, open too, the child's slipper on its silk. And flat at the front in its own cradle, one letter at a time — because paper cannot live under light, and because a house that shows you everything at once is not telling you anything. One letter a month, a card beside it in three languages, and on the twelfth month you began again. Four bricks along the bottom of the case had their carpenter's-pencil numbers turned to the glass.

The wall panel took her longer than the wall. She wrote nine versions and cut something out of every one, and what went up on a board the colour of old plaster was shorter than anything she had ever put her name to.

*This house belonged to Ho Mei Foong, brought to Malacca in 1934, aged twelve, as an amah's child, and at work in this street until 2004. She was a beadworker; between 1949 and the late 1980s the brides of this town were married in her work. In February 1942 she married Tan Boon Siew of this street privately, before an altar, with one witness. He was taken in the screenings of 1942 and did not come home. Where he died is not known; there is no record; nothing in this house pretends otherwise. She wrote to him for sixty-two years and never posted a letter. She had one daughter, raised by another family with Ho Mei Foong in the house as her amah, and not told. That was not an accident and it was not shameful: it was a decision taken to keep a child safe in a year when a child could be neither, and it was paid for daily, by the woman who took it, for the rest of her life. One letter is not shown here, at her daughter's request.*

Mei Lan had read all sixty-one over four visits, upstairs with the door shut, at the pace she did everything, which was faster than seemed decent and then abruptly not at all. She came down after the fourth holding the January 1943 letter in both hands like a hot dish, put it on the counter, and said, "This one is mine. Everything else you may hang in the street." Su-Lin said yes, and then wrote the withholding into the panel, because the alternative was to conceal a concealment, and this house had done enough of that.

The letter for the opening month picked itself, and cost her an evening with Teacher Yap over four characters at the top of it.

"You have written *the country got its name back*. Fine. Blunt. She is blunt. Then you have written *I am still waiting to be introduced*, and you must decide whether you meant it or whether you are being clever."

"It's what it says."

"It is what it says. The character is the one for presenting a person to a guest — a formal introduction, made by somebody with the standing to make it. It is a word for a room with people in it." He capped the pen. "She is in a doorway at six in the morning watching a flag go up over her own country, and she has chosen the word for being named aloud to strangers by a person entitled to name you. Nobody in her life was ever entitled. Write *introduced*, girl, and do not decorate it, and let whoever reads the card work for their supper."

So the card by the glass, from the first Saturday of March, read:

*Dear husband. The country got its name back tonight. I am still waiting to be introduced. There were fireworks the whole length of the street, and old Toh stood in the road and cried, and the school has run up a new flag, crooked at first and straight now, eleven points for eleven states, and not one of the eleven keeps a column on any paper anywhere for what I am. Our daughter is asleep on my shop floor with a flag in her hand. In three days they take her to the capital, which your father says is modern, and which will not know her, that being the point of it. I am sending nothing of yours with her. She has your brow and your handwriting and your bad habit of arriving at a table too fast, and nobody up there will know enough to say so; and she will grow up straight in a free country and be a nuisance to somebody, which is the whole of what I asked for. She says she will come back for the parade. They all say that. — Your wife, who kept the accounts through two flags and will keep them through this one.*

---

Mei Lan came down on the Friday coach, the third Friday of every month from November, and let herself be met at the terminal and complained about the driving.

She was not a sentimental visitor. She took the back room upstairs, brought her own kettle because Su-Lin's was a disgrace, and spent her first weekend reorganising the store cupboard on principles she declined to explain and which turned out, after a month of use, to be correct. She read the trust's grant conditions with a pencil, found two errors in the claim schedule, and rang the young officer about both on a Sunday, which Su-Lin discovered only later, from him, in a small voice. She was sixty-three, and had run a household in KL for forty years the way other people run a shipping line, and there was nowhere in this house that the competence could have come from except the woman in the folded photograph, who had squared envelopes to a counter edge for sixty years.

She did not go near the frames until December, and then it was Kwai Lan who moved her. The two of them had been coming in since October — the last of Mei Foong's apprentices, women in their seventies who had walked back into that shop the week the wall was opened and behaved ever since as though a fifteen-year interruption were a slightly poor joke — and Kwai Lan simply set a frame down in front of Mei Lan at the window table one Saturday, laid a needle beside it, and walked away.

The first session lasted twenty minutes. Su-Lin, up a ladder at the back of the hall, heard the silence, then a chair going back, then her grandmother saying pleasantly that she did not do this, and going to make tea nobody had asked for. Poh Choo, who was seventy and had not said forty words since October, unpicked the four beads Mei Lan had put in, laid the vamp flat, and left it exactly where it was on the table.

The second session was in January and lasted an hour. It was not enjoyable to watch. A row came out crooked, and was unpicked, and came out crooked again, and the shoulders went up around the ears in a way Su-Lin recognised so intimately from her own reflection that she had to go and be elsewhere.

"You are hauling them," Kwai Lan said, without looking up from her own frame. "Put it down and settle it. The bead is not running away from you."

The third session was in February, on a wet Saturday, and Su-Lin was on the ladder again, which is where she saw it. Her grandmother sat down at the frame without being asked, threaded the needle at the first attempt, settled her right elbow, and put her left hand flat on the black cloth to steady the ground — palm down, thumb tucked away underneath the fingers.

Kwai Lan put her tea down. She did it slowly, the way you put a glass down when you do not want to make a noise, and looked at that hand for a long moment, and then across the table at Poh Choo, who had also stopped. Neither of them spoke for the length of about four stitches.

"Nobody teaches that," Kwai Lan said at last, to the room.

Mei Lan did not look up. Her needle went in and came out, and the third bead of the row went down and stayed where it was put. "I was seven when I first sat at this window."

"I know when you sat. I was there. I was nineteen and you were a pest." Kwai Lan picked her tea back up. "I am not talking about the pattern. The pattern anybody can be taught. That hand is sifu's hand. She put it down like that all her life, and if you had asked her why she would have told you what she told me in 1950, which was *do not ask questions of your hands*." She drank. "Nobody taught her either."

By the end of that afternoon Mei Lan had the second row of a peony down; and the tail of the phoenix, which turns in six beads and which anybody who ever worked in that shop could do asleep, came off her needle in one movement at half past four with the light going gold in the front window. She looked at it for a second with an expression Su-Lin, on the ladder, decided not to interrupt, and then said that the light in this shop was appalling and somebody should have thought about it.

Three sessions. Fifteen years of it as a girl, forty-eight years of nothing, and then three sessions.

That night at the sink, drying the cups her grandmother washed, Su-Lin said: "Can I put it in the museum? Not you. The hand. The way you hold the cloth."

"Put whatever you like." Mei Lan turned the tap off. "It is not mine to be private about. Apparently it is hers."

---

They opened on a Saturday in March, at ten in the morning, with the shutters up the whole width of the front and half of Heeren Street standing in the five-foot way eating kuih off a tray that Hafiz's mother had produced from a car boot as if by conjuring. Raju's son brought the kopitiam kettle out and set two tables on the road and would not take money from anybody, which cost him, by his own later accounting, a fortune. Puan Aini came in a good baju and went round the case twice with her hands behind her back like a general inspecting a guard. The trust's young officer brought a photographer, stood a while in front of the open wall, and asked quietly whether he might take a copy of the panel text away with him, because that was how it ought to be done and nobody ever did it.

Su-Lin's mother came down on the early bus with a hairdo and a small hard handbag, and stood in front of the panel and read it, and then read it again, which took four minutes.

"You've put her name at the top," she said.

"It's her house."

"Yes," her mother said, and that was the whole speech; and it had cost a bus ticket and a hairdo and a public appearance at the scene of the family's oldest bad manners, and Su-Lin, learning the family's dialect late, understood that she had been given something. Her mother had brought the pen down in a spectacle case inside a plastic bag. It went into the case that morning beside the tin: dark green, mottled like river water under trees, gold nib still perfect, no name on it anywhere. The card Su-Lin lettered for it did not say *donated by*, and named nobody.

*Returned by the family.*

Uncle Raju cut the ribbon at ten, having decided some days earlier that there would be a ribbon and that he would cut it, and having borrowed gold scissors from the goldsmith on the grounds that his own were for chicken. He was eighty-one and he was magnificent. He held the two ends apart at arm's length and addressed the street for nine minutes without notes. He explained that the shop was reopening, which everyone could see. He explained that Sister Foong had sat in that window since before most of those present were born, which most of them knew. Then he explained at some length that the entire enterprise had been his own idea from the beginning; that when a KL girl in a nice car first came down this street knowing nothing, it was he, R. Raju, who had taken her in hand and told her what she had; that he had told her about the wall — "I did not tell her about the wall," he conceded, "but I told her everything else, and the wall came out of the everything else" — that he had personally supervised the roof, the plaster, the trust and the arrangement of the beads; and that he wished to say in closing that he remembered everything, ask anybody.

"He does," said Kwai Lan from the window table, without turning round. "He remembers everything. It is only that not all of it is his."

Raju cut the ribbon, pocketed the ribbon, and sat down in the best chair.

The two of them had been in since seven. They had arrived before the light, carrying their own cushions and their own scissors, and had rejected the new stools — too high, and the seats had a curve in them, and who had ever heard of a curve — in favour of the two that came out of the back store with sixty years of somebody's weight in them. Kwai Lan hung her scissors on the nail by the window where a nail had always been. Poh Choo squared her frame to the table edge and did not look up until eleven, when a woman from Muar came in off the road with a photograph of a kebaya and a daughter marrying in October, and asked whether anybody here still took orders.

Poh Choo took it. It was the first entry in the new book, written in the shop's numerals, and the deposit went into the drawer, and the woman's daughter put her bare foot on a sheet of paper on the shop floor to be drawn round, kneeling, the way it had been done in that room since 1949.

Su-Lin hung the photograph at noon, alone, because the street was outside with the coffee and because she had planned it that way. It had come back from the framer on Thursday: the wedding portrait, unfolded, both faces, mounted exactly as it was — the conservator had been immovable about the crease, which was not damage but evidence, the record of a decision taken with a thumbnail in a year nobody would ever know — so that the fold ran down between the two of them like a mended wall, and both of them, the man with his almost-smile and the girl failing at solemnity, stood on the same side of it at last.

It went above the altar shelf, on a hook Hafiz had put in on Wednesday and checked twice. On the shelf below, side by side on their indigo cloth, the two slippers: the left one that had waited sixty years in a tin under a window, and the right one that had spent forty-eight years in a KL cupboard in a bank's plastic bag, taken by a girl of fourteen on the night her family drove her away. A pair, worn, moulded to one woman's feet, black ground, peony and phoenix, rust and gold and jade.

She lit three sticks, because the shelf was an altar and not a display, and put them in the pot, and stood a minute with her hands together while out on the road Uncle Raju told somebody about the roof.

---

They were gone by six. The trays went home with Hafiz's mother, and Hafiz went with her, touching the door frame twice on the way out as he always did, saying he would be back Monday about the rear gutter. Kwai Lan and Poh Choo left at half past five with two orders in the book and their cushions under their arms. Uncle Raju was last, at ten to six, the red ribbon still folded in his shirt pocket, promising to come tomorrow and see how they were coping.

Su-Lin swept. The day had put beads on the floor — that was the thing nobody warned you about, the way a working shop sheds — forty or fifty of them along the skirting under the window, jade greens and one rust. She tipped them into the chipped saucer under the counter that was marked, in Mei Foong's hand, with the character for sweepings, and which had been full when she found it in June.

Then she put the lamp on, and took the tin's own paper out of the drawer, and the green pen, and filled it from the bottle, and sat down. She began in Chinese, because it was right, and got three characters in before she understood that she was writing them for herself and not for the recipient, and turned the sheet over. The woman she was writing to had used the English word *untidied* to a lawyer at the age of eighty-two, and would not be flattered by rusty penmanship.

*Dear Great-Grandmother — the accounts are in my name now.*

*The roof is sound. A man numbered the frame before he raised it and I have logged his numbers; they are your numbers, out of the price column. The cement is off the front and the wall has dried out and the lime is breathing. I found where your light is. I have put the frames in it, which you did without measuring anything, and I measured for a month to learn you were right.*

*The niche is open and it is staying open. Your tin is in it, and your letters, one at a time so the light does not take them; today the street read the one about the flag. I have written on the board that nobody knows where he died. I would rather say nothing than say a thing we do not know, which I learned partly from an archivist and mostly from you.*

*Your daughter is upstairs. She has her hands back — three afternoons, and the phoenix tail came off her needle like a bill being paid, and Kwai Lan put her tea down. Your slippers are a pair again. Your husband's face is over them and so is yours, the two of you on the same side of the crease at last.*

*Nobody in this house is called staff.*

*I have your pen. I am not giving it back and you are not going to make me. — Your great-granddaughter, who reads houses, and is learning to read your hand.*

She blotted it and folded it small, the way the others were folded, and took the reel of red thread from the drawer — the same reel, silk, three-quarters gone — and cut a length and tied it round the single sheet with the knot she had learned in October from a photograph and could now make without looking. Then she unlocked the case and set the letter in the open tin, on top of the last bundle, at the front where a hand would find it, and closed the glass and turned the key.

Then she did the round she had done every night since August. Back door barred. Kitchen light. The airwell: the cage on its hook, grey bamboo, its little door wired open in its figure-eight of copper, turning very slightly in the moving air of a house that could breathe again. Hall light. Shutters down and the pin through. The front door locked from the inside with the key on its worn leather tab, that a lawyer had slid across a desk to her ten months before.

She left one lamp on. It was the counter lamp, on its old flex, and she swung it round on its arm until it threw its warm circle up onto the wall above the altar shelf — on the two faces, on the crease between them, on the pair of beaded slippers below — and left the rest of the shop dark. Then she went out through the side door and pulled it to behind her.

Heeren Street was doing what it does at seven in the evening: shutters, motorbikes, somebody's television, the last of the kopitiam's charcoal. Su-Lin walked out to the middle of the road and turned and looked back at the one lit window in the terrace — the lamp that had burned over an abacus in an account room, and low and hooded over a birth in a country house at Ayer Keroh, and over sixty years of a counter in this street until the whole town set its watch by it — and anybody passing would have seen the light in the shophouse window and thought, as the street had thought for half a century, that somebody in there was still working.

Somebody was. She locked the last door and went to find her supper.
