# The House That Reads Letters

The lime came up the coast on a lorry on the first Tuesday of October, forty bags of it burnt from shell in a kiln that had been burning shell since before anybody's grandfather, and a drum of putty two years slaking in somebody's pit; and Hafiz took the lid off the drum in the five-foot way and made her put her hand in it.

It was cold, and heavier than it looked, and silkier than anything that heavy had a right to be, closing over her wrist like cream left overnight, smelling of a beach at low tide. She stood in the doorway of her great-grandmother's shop with her arm in a drum of lime and understood that she was, for the first time in nine years of practice, entirely inside her own work.

"Two years in the pit," Hafiz said. "Three would be better."

"It costs more than cement."

"It costs less than cement. Cement costs later."

The wall proved him. Somebody in the seventies had rendered the shop front in a hard grey skim, and the house had spent thirty years trying to breathe through it. The old brick was soft and low-fired, made to take up damp in the wet months and give it back in the dry ones; cement did not move, and was harder than the brick, so when the wall wanted to shift it was the brick that gave, and the salt out of the ground crystallised behind the skim and ate the wall's face into powder. They took it off through the last wet weeks of October, in dinner-plates, with the sound of a shell being cracked. Then the wall darkened two days, and on the third afternoon began to dry from the top down: a hundred years of held water leaving a building at the speed buildings do things.

"There," said Hafiz, passing with a bucket. "That's it exhaling."

The new plaster went on in three coats over the winter, worked with wooden floats, river sand through a kitchen sieve, and a bucket of the admixture that made the trust's young officer laugh when he read the specification: gula melaka, dissolved and strained, as old a practice as the shell kilns. She worked two coats herself, badly, and her hands hurt for a week. The finished wall was not flat; it undulated slightly under the light, the way old plaster does, and she caught herself running a palm along it whenever she passed, the way you touch the flank of an animal that is going to be all right.

She was, she noticed one morning in November, humming. For nine years she had been very good at work she did not love, and had assumed the flatness was the price of competence; and this was the same skill in entirely different weather, alive in her hands at last because the thing being measured was somebody she was trying to hear.

She kept a light diary that month: three photographs from a taped mark on the airwell floor — ten, one, four — for four weeks, plotted on graph paper on the back of the door. The airwell was the house's lung and its one honest window, and it sent its light down the shaft in a column that swung off the back wall in the morning, across the hall at noon, up the rear partition by four. The deep middle of the house was never once in working light; the light you could pass a needle in lay in one band by the front window until half past four and nowhere else. Somebody had known this. Somebody had put four frames there in 1949 and worked round the sun for forty years and never written it down, because you do not write down what you can see.

The roof gave her the marks. The frame stayed open until December, and up there in the bat smell and the hot dark the house stopped being a shop and became what it was, a timber frame jointed and pegged between two party walls built to hold it. The marks were on the undersides of the tie-beams, where nobody had looked since the raising. Strokes and hooks. A vertical; two verticals; a vertical crossed. She logged them, because logging what you do not understand is the whole discipline.

Teacher Yap looked at the photographs on a Thursday and made a sound of contempt.

"This is not calligraphy, it is a man counting. One. Two. Six. Nine — the old shop numerals. Any fish-seller's slate in this town had them when I was young, and nobody under sixty can read them now. Why are you smiling?"

"Because they're in her ledgers."

They were: the shop's day-book for 1953, Mei Foong's columns going down the page, and there in the price column the same strokes and hooks, brushed by a woman sixty feet below and forty years after the man who numbered the roof. The frame had been marked out on the ground, every member lettered so a crew could raise a house in a day without a drawing. The carpenter had counted this house up out of a heap of timber; the beader had counted the town's brides through it in the same figures; and nobody had told either of them they were keeping a record.

---

The letters took the winter. She did them at the counter under the lamp, in Mei Foong's own order: each one unfolded once, photographed, numbered on its folder and never on itself, transcribed, put back. Sixty-one of them — one from 1941, three from 1942, one from the January of 1943 with joy in it, then the two-year silence, then September 1945, and after that a slow file down the second half of the century, the gaps as legible as the writing.

The museum got designed at the same table, on the backs of transcription sheets. She could have made a shrine, or a period room with a mannequin at the frame, which was what the trust's young officer kept sketching in the air. What she built came out of one line of Puan Aini's, never since out of her head: *the record is the absence. That is the archive telling you the truth.*

So the shop stayed a shop: four frames at the window, the trays under the glass as Sister Kam had sorted them in 1950, rust to rose, the wooden lasts back on their nails. A working atelier, taking orders three days a week from anybody who came in with a foot. Nothing behind rope.

And in the ancestral hall, the wall.

She had numbered the bricks the night she opened it, eleven of them, so the niche could be closed exactly as it had been. They sat in their crate under the stairs all autumn, and one evening in December she went to Hafiz's yard and told him she was not putting them back. He asked whether the trust would call it loss of fabric, and she read him the case standing in the yard with the light going: that the blocking was not original but a wartime alteration, and was itself the significant thing; that the meaning of this house lay in an eleven-brick decision taken under occupation and never revoked; and that closing it up to make the wall look nice would be, in conservation terms, an act of decoration.

"Decoration," Hafiz repeated, with relish. "You've been reading my letters to the trust."

"You've been reading my drawings."

"Put the bricks in the case with it. Numbered side out. Let them see the pencil."

So the niche stayed open behind a sheet of glass in a slim brass frame, and the little arched dark eye of the wall held what it had been built to hold. The biscuit tin, English, roses and a hunting scene rubbed to ghosts, open, its lid propped beside it. The bundles inside under their red thread. The lacquered box, open too, the child's slipper on its silk. And flat at the front in its own cradle, one letter at a time — because paper cannot live under light, and because a house that shows you everything at once is not telling you anything — with a card beside it in three languages, changed monthly. Four bricks along the bottom had their carpenter's-pencil numbers turned to the glass.

The wall panel took her longer than the wall. She wrote nine versions, cutting something from every one, and what went up on a board the colour of old plaster was shorter than anything she had ever put her name to.

*This house belonged to Ho Mei Foong, brought to Malacca in 1934, aged twelve, as an amah's child, and at work in this street until 2004. She was a beadworker; between 1949 and the late 1980s the brides of this town were married in her work. In February 1942 she married Tan Boon Siew of this street privately, before an altar, with one witness. He was taken in the screenings of 1942 and did not come home. Where he died is not known; there is no record; nothing here pretends otherwise. She wrote to him for sixty-two years and never posted a letter. She had one daughter, raised by another family with Ho Mei Foong in the house as her amah, and not told — a decision taken to keep a child safe in a year when a child could not be, and paid for daily, by the woman who took it, for the rest of her life. One letter is not shown, at her daughter's request.*

Mei Lan had read all sixty-one over four visits, upstairs with the door shut. She came down after the fourth holding the January 1943 letter in both hands like a hot dish and said, "This one is mine. Everything else you may hang in the street." Su-Lin said yes, and then wrote the withholding into the panel, because the alternative was to conceal a concealment, and this house had done enough of that.

The letter for the opening month picked itself, and cost her an evening with Teacher Yap over four characters at the top of it.

"You have written *I am still waiting to be introduced*. Decide whether you meant it or whether you are being clever."

"It's what it says."

"It is what it says. The character is the one for presenting a person to a guest — an introduction made by somebody with the standing to make it." He capped the pen. "She is in a doorway at six in the morning watching a flag go up over her own country, and she has chosen the word for being named aloud by a person entitled to name you. Nobody in her life was ever entitled. Write *introduced*, girl, and do not decorate it."

So the card by the glass, from the first Saturday of March, read:

*Dear husband. The country got its name back tonight. I am still waiting to be introduced. There were fireworks the whole length of the street, and old Toh stood in the road and cried, and the school has run up a new flag, crooked at first and straight now, eleven points for eleven states, and not one of the eleven keeps a column on any paper anywhere for what I am. Our daughter is asleep on my shop floor with a flag in her hand. In three days they take her to the capital, which your father says is modern, and which will not know her, that being the point of it. I am sending nothing of yours with her. She has your brow and your handwriting and your bad habit of arriving at a table too fast, and nobody up there will know enough to say so; and she will grow up straight in a free country and be a nuisance to somebody, which is the whole of what I asked for. She says she will come back for the parade. They all say that. — Your wife, who kept the accounts through two flags and will keep them through this one.*

---

Mei Lan came down on the Friday coach, the third Friday of every month from November, and complained about the driving.

She was not a sentimental visitor. She took the back room upstairs, brought her own kettle because Su-Lin's was a disgrace, and reorganised the store cupboard on principles she declined to explain and which turned out, after a month, to be correct. She read the trust's grant conditions with a pencil, found two errors in the claim schedule, and rang the young officer about both on a Sunday. She was sixty-three, and there was nowhere that competence could have come from except the woman in the folded photograph, who had squared envelopes to a counter edge for sixty years.

She did not go near the frames until December, and then it was Kwai Lan who moved her. The two of them had been coming in since October — the last of Mei Foong's apprentices, women in their seventies who had walked back into that shop the week the wall was opened and behaved ever since as though a fifteen-year interruption were a poor joke — and Kwai Lan set a frame in front of Mei Lan one Saturday, laid a needle beside it, and walked away.

The first session lasted twenty minutes. Su-Lin, up a ladder at the back of the hall, heard the silence, then a chair going back, then her grandmother saying pleasantly that she did not do this, and going to make tea nobody had asked for. Poh Choo, who was seventy and had not said forty words since October, unpicked the four beads Mei Lan had put in and left the vamp where it was.

The second session was in January and lasted an hour, and was not enjoyable to watch. A row came out crooked, was unpicked, came out crooked again; the shoulders went up around the ears in a way Su-Lin recognised from her own reflection so exactly that she went and found work elsewhere.

"You are hauling them," Kwai Lan said, without looking up from her own frame. "Put it down and settle it. The bead is not running away from you."

The third session was in February, on a wet Saturday, and Su-Lin was on the ladder again, which is where she saw it. Her grandmother sat down without being asked, threaded the needle at the first attempt, settled her right elbow, and put her left hand flat on the black cloth to steady the ground — palm down, thumb tucked away underneath the fingers.

Kwai Lan put her tea down slowly, the way you put a glass down when you do not want to make a noise, and looked at that hand, and then across the table at Poh Choo, who had also stopped. Neither of them spoke for the length of about four stitches.

"Nobody teaches that," Kwai Lan said at last, to the room.

Mei Lan did not look up. Her needle went in and came out, and the third bead of the row went down and stayed where it was put. "I was seven when I first sat at this window."

"I know when you sat. I was there, I was nineteen and you were a pest." Kwai Lan picked her tea back up. "I am not talking about the pattern. The pattern anybody can be taught. That hand is sifu's hand. She put it down like that all her life, and if you had asked her why she would have said what she said to me in 1950, which was *do not ask questions of your hands*." She drank. "Nobody taught her either."

By the end of that afternoon Mei Lan had the second row of a peony down, and the tail of the phoenix, which turns in six beads and which anybody who ever worked in that shop could do asleep, came off her needle in one movement at half past four. She looked at it with an expression Su-Lin, on the ladder, decided not to interrupt, and then said that the light in this shop was appalling and somebody should have thought about it.

Three sessions. Eight years of it as a girl, forty-eight years of nothing, then three sessions.

That night at the sink, drying the cups her grandmother washed, Su-Lin said: "Can I put it in the museum? Not you. The hand. The way you hold the cloth."

"Put whatever you like." Mei Lan turned the tap off. "It is not mine to be private about. Apparently it is hers."

---

They opened on a Saturday in March, with the shutters up the whole width of the front and half of Heeren Street in the five-foot way eating kuih off a tray Hafiz's mother had produced from a car boot as if by conjuring. Raju's son set two tables out on the road and would not take money. Puan Aini went round the case twice, like a general inspecting a guard. The trust's young officer asked whether he might take a copy of the panel text away with him, because that was how it ought to be done and nobody ever did it.

Su-Lin's mother came down on the early bus with a hairdo and a small hard handbag, and stood in front of the panel and read it, and then read it again, which took four minutes.

"You've put her name at the top," she said.

"It's her house."

"Yes," her mother said, and that was the whole speech; and it had cost a bus ticket and a public appearance at the scene of the family's oldest bad manners, and Su-Lin understood that she had been given something. Her mother had brought the pen down in a spectacle case inside a plastic bag: dark green, mottled like river water under trees, gold nib still perfect, no name on it anywhere. It went in beside the tin, and the card she lettered for it named nobody.

*Returned by the family.*

Uncle Raju cut the ribbon at ten, having decided days earlier that there would be a ribbon and that he would cut it, and having borrowed gold scissors from the goldsmith because his own were for chicken. He was eighty-one and he was magnificent. He addressed the street for nine minutes without notes. He explained that the shop was reopening, which everyone could see. Then he explained that the enterprise had been his own idea from the beginning; that when a KL girl in a nice car first came down this street knowing nothing, it was he, R. Raju, who had taken her in hand and told her what she had; that he had told her about the wall — "I did not tell her about the wall," he conceded, "but I told her everything else, and the wall came out of the everything else" — and that he wished to say, finally, that he remembered everything.

"He does," said Kwai Lan from the window table, without turning round. "He remembers everything. It is only that not all of it is his."

Raju cut the ribbon, pocketed the ribbon, and sat down in the best chair.

The two of them had been in since seven, with their own cushions and their own scissors, and had rejected the new stools — too high, and the seats had a curve in them, and who had ever heard of a curve — for the two out of the back store with sixty years of somebody's weight in them. Kwai Lan hung her scissors on the nail where a nail had always been. Poh Choo squared her frame to the table edge and did not look up until eleven, when a woman from Muar came in off the road with a photograph of a kebaya and a daughter marrying in October, and asked whether anybody here still took orders.

Poh Choo took it: the first entry in the new book, in the shop's numerals, the deposit into the drawer, and the daughter's bare foot on a sheet of paper on the floor to be drawn round, kneeling, the way it had been done in that room since 1949.

Mei Lan stayed upstairs until eleven, then came down and took the third frame at the window, between the two of them, and let herself be introduced to nobody.

Su-Lin hung the photograph at noon, alone, because the street was outside with the coffee and because she had planned it that way. It had come back from the framer unfolded, both faces, mounted exactly as it was — the conservator had been immovable about the crease, which was not damage but evidence — so that the fold ran between them like a mended wall, and both of them, the man with his almost-smile and the girl failing at solemnity, stood on the same side of it at last.

It went above the altar shelf, on a hook Hafiz had put in and checked twice. On the shelf below, on their indigo cloth, the two slippers: the left one that had waited sixty years in a tin under a window, and the right one that had spent forty-eight years in a KL cupboard, taken by a girl of fourteen on the night her family drove her away. A pair, worn, moulded to one woman's feet, black ground, peony and phoenix, rust and gold and jade.

She lit three sticks, because the shelf was an altar and not a display, and stood a minute with her hands together while out on the road Uncle Raju told somebody about the roof.

---

They were gone by six. Hafiz went with his mother and the empty trays, touching the door frame twice on the way out as he always did. Kwai Lan and Poh Choo had left at half past five with two orders in the book and their cushions under their arms. Uncle Raju was last, the red ribbon still folded in his shirt pocket, promising to come tomorrow and see how they were coping.

Su-Lin swept. The day had put beads on the floor — that was the thing nobody warned you about — forty or fifty along the skirting under the window. She tipped them into the chipped saucer under the counter marked, in Mei Foong's hand, with the character for sweepings.

Then she put the lamp on, and took paper out of the drawer, and the green pen, and filled it, and sat down. She began in Chinese, and got three characters in before she understood that she was writing them for herself and not for the recipient, and turned the sheet over. The woman she was writing to had used the English word *untidied* to a lawyer at eighty-two, and would not be flattered by rusty penmanship.

*Dear Great-Grandmother — the accounts are in my name now.*

*The roof is sound. A man numbered the frame before he raised it, and his numbers are your numbers, out of the price column. The cement is off the front and the lime is breathing. I found where your light is: I have put the frames in it, which you did without measuring anything, and I measured for a month to learn you were right.*

*The niche is open and it is staying open. Your tin is in it, and your letters, one at a time so the light does not take them; today the street read the one about the flag. I have written on the board that nobody knows where he died. I would rather say nothing than say a thing we do not know, which I learned partly from an archivist and mostly from you.*

*Your daughter is upstairs. She has her hands back — three afternoons, and the phoenix tail came off her needle like a bill being paid. Your slippers are a pair again, and your husband's face is over them, and so is yours, the two of you on the same side of the crease at last.*

*Nobody in this house is called staff.*

*I have your pen. I am not giving it back. — Your great-granddaughter, who reads houses, and is learning to read your hand.*

She folded it small, the way the others were folded, and took the reel of red thread from the drawer — the same reel, silk, three-quarters gone — and tied the knot she had learned in October from a photograph and could now make without looking. Then she unlocked the case and set the letter in the open tin, on top of the last bundle, where a hand would find it, and closed the glass and turned the key.

Then she did the round she had done every night since August. Back door barred. Kitchen light. The airwell: the cage on its hook, grey bamboo, its little door wired open in its figure-eight of copper, turning slightly in the moving air of a house that could breathe again. Hall light. Shutters down and the pin through. The front door locked with the key on the worn leather tab a lawyer had slid across a desk to her ten months before.

She left one lamp on. It was the counter lamp, on its old flex, and she swung it round on its arm until it threw its warm circle up onto the wall above the altar shelf — on the two faces, on the crease between them, on the slippers below — and left the rest of the shop dark. Then she went out by the side door and pulled it to behind her.

Heeren Street was doing what it does at seven in the evening: shutters, motorbikes, somebody's television, the last of the kopitiam's charcoal. Su-Lin walked out to the middle of the road and looked back at the one lit window in the terrace — the lamp that had burned over an abacus in an account room, and low and hooded over a birth at Ayer Keroh, and over sixty years of a counter in this street until the town set its watch by it — and anybody passing would have seen it and thought, as the street had thought for half a century, that somebody in there was still working.

Somebody was. She locked the last door and went to find her supper.
