# The Wedding of Bee Nio

The borrowed cooks arrived three days before the lap chai, in the dark, announced by the squeal of handcart wheels and the voice of Third Aunt, who had been awake since four o'clock arranging the kitchen court into a country she intended to govern absolutely. By sunrise there were eleven women in a court built for five, four charcoal braziers where there were usually two, and a mountain of banana leaf and pandan on the long table that Mei Foong would remember for the rest of her life as the smell of that entire month: green, sweet, cut, bruised.

The house of seven doors was marrying its daughter.

Upstairs and streetward, in the rooms Mei Foong now entered only carrying things, the wedding was silk and lacquer and the Bibik's lists. Down here it was arithmetic. Three days of kueh before the first guest ever bit into one: kueh bangkit stamped and dried until they knocked on the tray like little bones; red-dyed angkoo pressed out of their moulds in ranks; the layered kueh lapis steamed colour by colour, each layer poured and set and poured again, a cake made the way a year is made. Third Aunt tasted everything, praised nothing, and rejected one tray in ten on principle, so the borrowed cooks would understand whose court this was; the rejected trays went to the children of the back lane, who took up positions by the drain from the first morning like sparrows who had read the almanac.

Mei Foong pounded, folded, stamped, steamed, carried. She was seventeen and she had stood at the edge of this house's ceremonies for six years, and she had learned that a wedding, from the kitchen side, is a siege in reverse: the house fills its own walls with provisions and then opens the gates on purpose.

On the second evening the lanterns went up.

The hired man came with his ladder and his coils of wire, and by dusk both air wells held strings of them, red and gold, hung crosswise between the upper railings, so that the shafts that all year carried down rain and grey light carried light upward instead. Mei Foong stood at the bottom of the kitchen court's air well with a basin against her hip and looked up at the burning squares swaying very slightly in air no one below could feel. The merbok cage hung on its pole in the corner, its bird head-tucked and unimpressed. Six years of looking up that shaft at a rectangle of weather. Now the house had put stars in it, because the house could do that, when it chose, for its own.

"The oil lamps are for the twelve days," said Ah Yoke, passing behind her with folded altar cloths, in the voice she used for facts that were also warnings. "Something must be kept burning in this house every night until the twelfth night. See it is not you."

---

The lap chai came at the hour the matchmaker had fixed as auspicious, which was also, the Towkay observed, the hour of maximum traffic on Heeren Street. Mei Foong saw it in pieces, which was how the kitchen saw everything: through the door of the back hall while carrying in the sweet tea, from the mouth of the passage with a tray of oranges wanted urgently and then not wanted.

It was an exchange of gifts between the two houses, and it was also, she understood at once, an exchange of claims. The Lim bearers came in procession from the street, tray after lacquered tray carried at chest height: roast pig glistening under red paper, bolts of cloth, brandy with foreign labels, oranges built into pyramids, gold in flat velvet boxes that were opened, angled to the light, and closed. The matchmaker walked beside the trays with her fan going like a rudder, announcing each item in the voice of a woman who collected a percentage of everything she named. The Bibik received it all seated, magnificently unsurprised. Then half of everything was sent back — that was the courtesy, the Tan trays returning to the Lims loaded with kueh from the court, with the pig's hind leg answering its foreleg, with a young rooster and hen in a basket, so that neither house should be seen simply to buy or simply to sell.

In the kitchen passage, Third Aunt watched her kueh leave in state on lacquer and said, "Six hundred pieces. They will say, the Tans did not stint," and wiped her face with her apron, and for one unguarded second looked like what she was: an artist watching her work go to the judges.

Mei Foong, going back for the next tray, nearly walked into the abacus of the whole affair — the Towkay and the matchmaker in the doorway of the front office, her fan folded now, business-shaped, and the Towkay saying, "...the Khoo family. Of Ipoh." It meant nothing to her yet: a name from a town she had never seen, and she carried her oranges past it the way she carried everything past everything, invisibly, and only later would she rule the line under that entry and see what it had cost.

---

She learned what it meant four days later, from the accounts, which was where this house kept its truths.

Boon Siew was at the long table when she brought the ledgers up, and he was not working. He had a photograph in front of him, face up, and he was looking at it from a slight distance, arms folded, the way he looked at a forward contract he did not intend to sign. The photograph was of a girl about his own age in a studio in Ipoh: pearls, a paper backdrop of a garden, a face that was pretty the way handwriting can be pretty, telling you nothing but the training.

"Khoo Siew Ean," he said, without being asked. "Only daughter of Khoo Teik Swee. Tin." He said the word tin the way his father said it, and the imitation was perfect and awful: a small reverent weight on it, an ingot dropped into a scale pan. "The dredges in the Kinta. Six of them. My father has done the sum out loud at me three times this week and each time the answer improves. Rubber and tin, Malacca and Ipoh, my ships — his word, my ships — carrying their ore." He looked up at her. "It is a merger, Ah Foong. I am the seal on the document."

Mei Foong set the ledgers down and squared them. In the margin of her mind an entry wrote itself: the matchmaker's fee, carried forward.

"The girl," she said. "What is she?"

"Nineteen. Convent-schooled. Collects gramophone records, the matchmaker says, as though reporting a harmless illness." He pushed the photograph an inch away with one finger. "I have nothing against her. That is the trap of it — there is nothing to be against. She is a column of perfectly correct figures, and here at last is arithmetic even my father's son cannot fault." He was quiet for a moment, and the house was loud below them, wedding-loud, hammering and deliveries. "I said, not yet. I said, Bee Nio first, one wedding at a time. I bought a season, maybe two. That is all refusing looks like in this house — buying time and calling it filial patience."

"And when the season is spent?"

"Then I will refuse," he said, evenly. "In some way that cannot be mistaken for patience."

She should have said nothing. Six years had taught her exactly what she was permitted to say in this house. She said: "Refusals have prices. You taught me that yourself, in the margins. Everything has a forward price, and men who will not calculate it are not brave, they are only slow."

"I have calculated it," Boon Siew said, and looked at her, and did not say any figure, and the look was the figure, and Mei Foong picked up yesterday's ledger and opened it between them like a screen, and checked columns she had already checked, while the wedding hammered on below.

---

They combed Bee Nio's hair up on the wedding morning in the hour before light, and Mei Foong was in the room, because Bee Nio had demanded it and brides that week were refused nothing.

The mistress of ceremonies had been hired from among the old bibiks of the town, a small dry authoritative woman who had married off, by her own accounting, one hundred and forty brides, and who moved through the rites the way Third Aunt moved through the kitchen: no wasted gesture, no appeal allowed. She had chosen the hour from the almanac; she had set the sam kai altar in the front hall — the homage to Heaven and Earth and the ancestors, the tall red candles that would burn through the twelve days, dragon and phoenix twined up their sides — and now, in the bridal chamber, by lamplight, she seated Bee Nio on the wooden rice-measure draped in red, facing the direction the almanac had approved, before a mirror that held one candle-flame and one sixteen-year-old face.

Mei Foong stood against the wall with the tray of combs and oil and watched her mistress, her tormentor, her almost-sister, being made into a wife. The old bibik combed the loosened hair three long strokes, crown to ends, and with each stroke she chanted the blessing in Baba Malay — first stroke to the end of the road together, second stroke for sons and daughters, third stroke for white hair reached side by side — and her voice was worn smooth like a doorsill, one hundred and forty brides deep. The hair went up. It went up and up, dressed over its frame, pinned with gold until Bee Nio's head carried a garden and her neck held it the way the house's pillars held the roof-tiles, and her face in the mirror got smaller and stiller as her crown grew, until the girl who had raged five days over a doctor's spectacles looked out from under the gold like a small garrison holding a very large fort.

Her eyes found Mei Foong's in the mirror.

"Everyone out," Bee Nio said. "One minute. Everyone except my girl."

The mistress of ceremonies opened her mouth, consulted something in Bee Nio's face against her one hundred and forty precedents, and shut it. The room emptied. Dawn was arriving in the air well, grey on grey; below them the kitchen court was already at full pitch, feeding the twelve days; and Tan Bee Nio, crowned, kneed her way around on the rice measure with a rustle like a tray of beads and took both of Mei Foong's hands, which no one had done in that house in six years.

On her feet were the slippers.

Mei Foong had given them up in their camphor box months before and had not seen them since — the year of her evenings, the peony and the phoenix worked bead by bead in rust and gold and jade and a blue that was almost night, ten thousand small decisions with two characters hidden inside the lining where no one would ever look. On Bee Nio's feet, in lamplight, they were no longer work. They burned quietly, like the lanterns, like something the house had always owned.

"I couldn't breathe," Bee Nio said, low and fast, the words she had been holding all morning with her chin up. "All month I couldn't breathe. Last night I lay there and thought, I will run, I will actually run, I will be the scandal of the Straits — and this morning they dressed me, and I put my feet into your year, and I stood up, and my feet knew how." Her grip was crushing; her crown never moved; she had already learned to weep without wrecking anything, straight down, one line on each powdered cheek. "You made my feet brave. That is what you did. Whatever they say the slippers are, that is what they are. So you will stay where I can see you today. That is an order. It is my last one."

There was a knock; the world resumed; the mistress of ceremonies repossessed the bride. But going down the back stair with the tray, Mei Foong found she was holding the banister, and stood a moment on the turn where a boy had once passed a banana through the rails, and let the thing settle into her where she kept the things that could not go into any ledger: her year, walking. Bee Nio would never need to know what was written in the lining. But she had felt it anyway. That was what perfect work did — it disappeared into someone else's story and worked there, secretly, like a name written in water that somehow never dried.

---

The bridegroom came at mid-morning, on foot down Heeren Street with his procession, in the long embroidered robes the rites demanded, and the door of the house of seven doors was shut against him, because that also was the rite.

Mei Foong saw it from the upstairs shutter she had been sent to close and did not close. The pantun singers stood on the five-foot way and sang up at the house — the old teasing verses in Malay, call and answer, the groom's party pleading in rhyme, praising the flower within, lamenting the cruelty of doors, while an uncle of the Tans answered verse for verse from inside, demanding to know the worth of the man, and the whole street gathered to score it, laughing at the old jokes in the old places. Dr. Lim Cheng Hoe stood at the centre of it all in his stiff gold-crusted splendour with the expression of a man both entirely ridiculous and entirely willing, which, Mei Foong was coming to understand, was his particular form of courage. When at last the door opened — the verses spent, the red packets passed, honour satisfied on both sides — he crossed the threshold of the house he was marrying into, and paused, and bowed, small and unscripted, to the door itself, as if to say: fairly sung, fairly opened. The street liked that. The street repeated it for a week.

What the street never saw was the kitchen court, an hour later, in the trough between the door-opening and the tea ceremony, when the second syrup pot came off the brazier wrong. It was one of the borrowed cooks' boys who took it, ten years old, hired for the week to mind fires — the hot gula came over the lip and across the back of his hand, and he did what children in kitchens do, which is not to scream, because a scream costs the job; he wrapped his hand in his shirt and stood in the corner by the water jars, going grey.

Third Aunt saw, hissed, sent for oil. Mei Foong was already at the jar dipping cool water when the light in the doorway changed, and the bridegroom of the house was standing in the kitchen court.

He should not have been there; a groom between rites belonged in the front hall being admired and fed; and afterwards Mei Foong could only suppose he had heard the particular pitch of a kitchen going quiet, the way a doctor hears. He came in among them in his gold, waved the flurry back down with one hand, and knelt — the robes pooling on the wet brick — and took the boy's hand out of the shirt and looked at it the way Mei Foong looked at figures.

"Good," he said to the boy. "Water, not oil. Whoever went for oil, drink it yourself, it is no use to him." He held the small hand steady in the basin Mei Foong brought, minutes long, talking all the while in a low running voice about nothing — about how he, personally, had burned himself on his own mother's syrup pot at this boy's age, and had cried, loudly, and been demoted from the kitchen forever, which was why he had been forced to become a doctor, there being no future for him in kueh. The boy, grey, hiccuped a laugh. From somewhere inside the gold robes came a doctor's flat tin; a dressing went on, neat as beadwork; and then Dr. Lim stood, shook his robes back into ceremony, pointed at the boy and said, "Wages of a full day. I was here, I witnessed the work," which was a sentence addressed to no one and aimed precisely at Third Aunt, and went back through the doorway into his wedding, and was being toasted in the front hall ninety seconds later.

He never told it. That was the part Mei Foong kept. All the twelve days of feasts, with every guest hungry for stories of the bridegroom, a kind act performed before eleven witnesses in a kitchen would have spent beautifully — and he never spent it. She checked, the way she checked everything: it never came back up the passage in any voice. He had done it and left it where it lay, like a coin sewn into a seam, and Mei Foong, who had been taught by an old woman that there were two kinds of giving, entered Dr. Lim Cheng Hoe in her private accounts under the second kind, and never afterwards moved the entry.

The tea ceremony filled the front hall through the long afternoon: the bride and groom kneeling to rank after rank of elders, offering tea with both hands, receiving in return the red packets and the gold, the Bibik upright as a candle taking her cup with her ringed hands while the ancestors watched from their altar through the smoke of the twelve-day candles. Then the viewing of the bridal chamber, the guests filing past the bed with its embroidered hangings and its heaped pillows, on which a small nephew had been rolled that morning, laughing, for luck and sons. Mei Foong glimpsed the room in passing, over shoulders: the mirror, the crown on its stand, and by the bed, side by side on a red cloth, the kasut manek, put off now for the viewing, catching the lamp.

The photographer from the Jonker Street studio came at five to make the formal portrait, arranging the family by rank on the front steps, and Mei Foong watched from the shadow of the passage and was in nobody's photograph, and did not know yet that she was memorizing the man, his black cloth, his voice saying hold still, hold still, against a morning two years off when he would matter more than anyone in Malacca.

---

The feasts ran their twelve days like a tide table: the relatives' day, the friends' day, the day for the Towkay's business associates when the Bentley stood washed at the door meaning nothing and impressing everyone, the lanterns lit every dusk, the sam kai candles burning down in the front hall, their dragons sinking slowly toward the brass. The kitchen court cooked until the smell of pandan lived in Mei Foong's hair. Twice more she saw Dr. Lim do a small thing and not spend it — a chair moved to put an old deaf uncle's good ear to the singers; the last and best of the crab claws going onto the plate of the matchmaker's silent husband, whom no one had addressed all evening — and each time she entered it, and each time the account grew stranger, because this was the man the fan had chosen, sight unseen, off a photograph and a fee. Even a blind net catches one true fish, said Third Aunt, when Mei Foong said something like this aloud over the washing. Do not tell the net.

On the twelfth night the house let out its breath. The last guests had eaten the last feast; the borrowed cooks were paid off and gone with their handcarts and their new stories; Bee Nio — Mrs. Lim Cheng Hoe, a person now who visited the house of seven doors, wearing her married hair — had ridden away to her husband's house through a street that turned to watch. In the kitchen court the crockery of three lending households stood in towers, and Mei Foong moved along the towers with a lamp and the tally book, counting borrowed plates for return, because a wedding, from the kitchen side, ends the way it begins: in inventory.

The lanterns in the air well were still lit. They would come down in the morning; tonight they burned on over the emptied house with the particular sadness of decorations that have outlived their feast, and their light came down the shaft and moved slightly on the wet bricks, and the merbok stirred once on its perch and made its small folded night-sound.

She heard him on the stair and knew the step, as she knew his hand in a ledger.

Boon Siew came into the court still in his good clothes from the last feast, collar loosened, and did not pretend, as he sometimes did, to have come for water. He stood across the long table from her, in the lantern light, with the towers of other people's plates between them, and she set down the tally book and waited, because his face was the face from the account room, the one with the figure in it.

"They drank to Ipoh tonight," he said. "You will have heard. A Khoo uncle was at the associates' table — passing through, they said, and nothing passes through Malacca in a wedding month by accident. My father stood up and talked about the joining of houses, in general, at length, in the direction of nobody, and everyone at that table could add." He turned his glass — he still had it, empty, and set it down among the plates like one more borrowed thing. "I sat there and smiled. That is what I could do. In public, in this family, in front of that man, there is nothing I can refuse that does not shame my sister's wedding, so I sat and smiled, and the smiling has been entered in everyone's books as agreement. I know how entries work. You taught me to read what the column does not say."

"And privately?" Mei Foong said. Her voice came out level, which cost what it cost.

He looked at her across the towers of plates, under the lanterns, and made no move around the table — and she understood that too, later: that he kept the table between them on purpose, because what he was going to say had to stand by itself, untouched, unhelped, or it was worth nothing.

"Privately, there is no Ipoh," he said. "There is no merger. I have done the accounts, Ah Foong — mine, not his. Two years I have done them, since a night on that stair, over that cage." He did not look at the cage; he looked at her. "Assets, liabilities, what a man owns, what owns him. My father counts what a marriage brings in. He has never once in his life counted what it costs, because it was never him who paid." He drew a breath, and said it plainly, the way true things went into a ledger, without ornament, in ink: "When I marry, it will be a marriage of my own accounting. Whatever it costs. However long the books stay open. That is the entry. I am telling the only person who checks my figures."

The air well held it, the way it held rain, straight down, a column with the two of them at the bottom, and Mei Foong stood very still in the smell of pandan with her whole life in her face, and did not lie about it, and did not move toward him either; and neither of them heard whatever it is that old women do not make a sound with, because in the mouth of the kitchen passage, with the altar cloths from the front hall folded over her arm, dark in her blacks against the dark, Ah Yoke was standing. How long she had been there, there was no telling. Long enough. She did not speak. She looked at the two of them across the borrowed crockery the way she looked at rice going into the bin — exactly, completely, counting — and then she turned back into the passage, and her felt slippers on the brick made the sound of nothing at all.

That night, on the pallet in the amahs' room, Mei Foong lay with her face to the wall and her breathing slow and false, and heard behind her the click of the case with the broken clasp.

She had heard that case a hundred times: the comb going in, the comb coming out. This was not the comb. Through her lashes, in the small light of the shrine lamp on the shelf, she watched Ah Yoke sit on the edge of her own pallet with the red-thread bundle in her two hands — the bundle from the bottom of the case, the one Mei Foong had seen buried there on their first night in this house six years ago and never once since. Ah Yoke did not open it. She held it on her knees the way you hold something at a graveside, and she looked at it for a long time — longer than prayers, longer than counting; the lamp fluttered and steadied, and somewhere streetward a last wedding lantern ticked against its wire in a wind that never reached the floor — and her thumb moved once across the knots, reading them, the way the blind read.

Then she put it back. Down at the bottom, under the winter things, where it lived; the case shut; the broken clasp was tied with its string; and Ah Yoke lay down with her face to the wall in the manner of a woman who has looked at a door for a long time and decided, one more time, not yet to open it.

Mei Foong kept her breathing slow, and memorized it all in Ah Yoke's exact order, with the stops in Ah Yoke's exact places — the bundle, the knots, the thumb, the not-yet — because everything about the holding said that it was true, and everything about the putting-back said that it was hers.
