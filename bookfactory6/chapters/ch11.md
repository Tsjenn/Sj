# The Betrothal

The matchmaker's fan came back to the house in the dry weeks after the seventh month, and this time it was open for Boon Siew. Mei Foong, carrying in the pink nyonyaware on its black tray, read the fan before she had set down a single cup: half-furled meant a possibility, resting on the knee meant negotiation, and fully spread, moving slowly, meant that somewhere the thing had already been decided and the afternoon was merely walking to where it stood.

The fan was fully spread.

The name was Khoo. Tin people, of Ipoh — the matchmaker laid the word *Ipoh* on the table the way the Towkay laid down a trump — old Khoo Teik Swee of the Kinta Valley, who had gone into the ground poor and come up out of it with six dredges, machines the size of temples that floated on ponds of their own digging and ate the earth day and night. There was a house, said the matchmaker, that people drove out on Sundays just to look at. There was one son, studying in Hong Kong. And there was a daughter, nineteen, convent-schooled, who played the piano and kept her father's household since her mother died, and whose photograph now came out of the matchmaker's folder and crossed the teak table face up.

Mei Foong, pouring, saw it upside down: a girl in a photographer's chair, hands folded the way they place your hands, looking not quite at the camera — past it, at something over the photographer's shoulder, with an expression Mei Foong could not name at the time and later, when she could, would remember she had seen at the very first minute. The expression of someone counting the exits politely.

"Khoo Siew Ean," said the matchmaker.

The Towkay's face had taken on the shine it took at the chalkboard when the Singapore line crackled good news. He said the sensible things first, because he always did — the distance, the different ways of northern families — and then he said the true thing, which was arithmetic. Rubber and tin. What the country stood on, both legs under one roof. The Khoos' tin went out through the same strait his rubber went out through; their money and his money were cousins already; it wanted only the formality. "A firm marries a firm," he said, "and the children do the paperwork," and laughed, and the matchmaker laughed, and the Bibik did not laugh but began asking the questions that mattered — the grandmother's people, the mother's grave, the aunts — which was her way of saying yes slowly.

Boon Siew stood through all of it near the foot of the stair, where the merbok's cage hung in its alcove, and said one thing only. Asked directly by his father, in front of the fan, whether he had any objection a father should hear, he looked at the photograph for a moment — at the girl watching her exits — and said, "None that you would count, Father."

Mei Foong, at the sideboard, kept her hands moving among the cups.

It was the truth, she thought that night, and it was spent like a lie. He had made his real refusal months ago, in the one place it was free — a lantern-lit air well, an audience of one — and it had bought what free things buy. Here, in the front hall, with the fan spread, *none that you would count* went into the room's ledger as consent, and was initialed, and carried forward. The house had a genius for this. It never forced anyone. It simply kept the books in such a way that silence compounded.

By evening the eight characters of his birth had been copied out fair for the temple, and Third Aunt was already talking about what northern people ate.

---

The lessons came for Mei Foong on the first Tuesday of every month, in a long buff envelope from a commercial institute in Singapore, and every envelope said the same three characters: Ho Mei Foong. She had paid for the course herself — eleven dollars for the year, counted out of the tobacco tin in her sewing box where the wedding money lived: the ang pow the Lims had pressed on the household staff at Bee Nio's wedding, and the quiet dollars from two Tranquerah brides whose mothers had seen Bee Nio walk and wanted their daughters walking in the same hands. Ah Yoke had watched her wrap the money for the postal order and said nothing, and then, at the last moment, added fifty cents from her own knotted handkerchief. "For the stamps," she said, in the voice she used for closing subjects.

*Book-keeping and Commercial Accounts, taught by post. Certificate upon examination.* The first lessons were beneath her, and she did them perfectly anyway, because the exercises came back from Singapore marked in red by a stranger who did not know she was anybody's amah girl, and there was a pleasure in that she did not examine too closely. By Lesson Four the course had begun handing her words for things her hands had done for years. Double entry, the lesson said, as though revealing a secret: every transaction lives twice, once as what was gained and once as what it cost, and the books do not balance until both are written. She sat with that one a long time, at the back-hall table, under the lamp.

For four years she had kept this family's books the way a swallow builds — exactly, and without a word for any of it. Now the words arrived by post, and at the end of them, if she passed, there would be a paper. She had seen the specimen certificate reproduced in the prospectus: scrollwork, a seal, and a line where they printed your name in full. Her mother's name had been written once in water and once on a slip that went to the fire, missing its first stone. Hers would be printed, whole, by a machine in Singapore, on a document no one could burn without her permission. Eleven dollars did not seem too much for that.

She studied at night because the days had filled with war, or with what Malacca that year called war. Wardens came down Heeren Street measuring doorways for sandbags and chalking marks that the rain took off by Friday. On the padang on Saturdays the Volunteers drilled, clerks and schoolmasters in khaki that had been cut for other men, and the town went to watch as it went to wayang, a show, with kacang for the children; when the siren was tested at midday the whole street checked its watches against it, and the coffee-shop tables agreed the note was flat. In June, France fell — France, which the High School atlases coloured a serious pink-adjacent mauve — and the front hall was grave for a week; and then rubber went up.

It kept going up. That was the year the chalkboard by the river never held a number long enough for the dust to settle on it. The wireless said the democracies were stockpiling, and the lighters went out to the roads so heavy that old boatmen sucked their teeth, and the money came in like the tide, and everyone said so, using those words, *like the tide*, and forgot what tides do. The Towkay's scoffing at the drills mellowed into sponsorship; he bought the Volunteers a lorry and had his photograph taken beside it. The town's mood came up the street some evenings like the smell of frying: prices climbing, danger rehearsed at noon and applauded, everything selling — how could a place be in peril when it had never been so rich?

Mei Foong, alone with the ledgers, was perhaps the only person on the street whom the boom made quietly cold. She could see it laid out in the ruled columns as plainly as a sum done wrong at the top and carried faithfully to the bottom: everything the firm had, going into one crop; the crop going to one buyer; the buyer across one ocean; the ocean held polite by a navy that was now, the wireless admitted in its mildest voice, rather busy elsewhere. It was MERBOK's rice letter written out in figures. A garden that grows only flowers. She checked the additions, which were perfect, and initialed the pages, and did not say to anyone that the books of a fortunate house can balance beautifully all the way to the edge of a cliff.

Bee Nio swept in most weeks from the Lim house on Tranquerah Road, six months married and wearing it like a new kebaya — the married woman's hair, the married woman's opinions — all of which she dropped at the kitchen door the way she had once dropped her school satchel. Of Ipoh she said what the whole town was saying, only faster: tin money, house like a bank, poor girl. She turned over Lesson Five while she talked, read half a page of it, and looked up with her eyes narrowed. "This is what you do already."

"Now there will be a paper saying so."

Bee Nio held the lesson a moment longer. Something moved behind her face — the arithmetic she was quickest at, which had nothing to do with numbers. "Good," she said, handing it back. "Papers are how this family believes things," and then, briskly, before the sentence could mean anything: "Cheng Hoe sends a joke. I refuse to repeat it. The patient supplies the stomach was the *peak*, Foong; it has been downhill for six months," and she stole a lesson-book pencil and left, trailing lime blossom and contentment, and Mei Foong sat listening to the house absorb her going, and smiled at the ledger like a fool.

---

The party left for Ipoh in October: the Towkay, the Bibik, the matchmaker travelling as staff officer, and Boon Siew. They went in the Bentley. It had never in six years gone farther than the New Year round of visits, polished like an heirloom tooth and driven by a hired syce at the pace of a funeral; now it was to make two days up the trunk road, crossing the rivers on chain ferries, because the Towkay had thought about arriving at a tin man's marble porch by train, in a hired car, and had gone out to the garage himself with a lamp.

The night before they left, Boon Siew came to the account drawer. He did not sit. He laid a folded paper on the ledger between them, squared to its corner, the way everything between them was squared.

"For market morning," he said. "Or for the stove. Your judgment."

She read it standing. It was MERBOK, and it was not like the others. The rice letters, the padi letters, even the answer that had made a planter foam — those had argued with men. This one argued with a mood; it argued with the entire warm, prosperous, applauding street outside the shutters. The boom is not a wall, it said; it is a lamp, and lamps are visible a long way at night. It said that a country that exported its safety along with its rubber should not be surprised to find, some morning, that both had been sold forward. It said the drills must stop being theatre and the Volunteers must stop being a Saturday, because whatever was coming would not come on a Saturday; and near the end it said the sentence she would find herself saying silently for the rest of her life, in queues and shelters and darker places than either of them could yet imagine: *The enemy is not yet at sea. The enemy is here already, and its name is complacency, and it holds a full glass.*

"This one knows too much about you," she said at last. "The tonnages. The godown figures. A man in the trade wrote this; the letter says so in every line. Someone will start counting the men in the trade."

"Yes," he said. He was looking at her steadily, and the lamp made its old margin around the two of them, wide enough for exactly this. "That is why the judgment is yours. You are the better judge of what a page costs."

He took the fountain pen from the drawer — she watched him do it, and said nothing, and the drawer looked wrong the moment it closed — "to write dutiful letters home," he said, "in a hand my father can show people," and he was gone up the stair past the sleeping bird, and in the morning the Bentley went north with the whole street watching, and the house exhaled and began, happily, without him, to plan his wedding.

---

She was not in Ipoh. What follows she assembled afterwards, entry by entry, from what he told her at the drawer in the weeks that came — he told it plainly, being no good at any other way — and she kept the account of it as she kept every account: nothing invented, nothing left out, and the totals carried honestly, even where they hurt.

They came down into the Kinta Valley on the second afternoon, and he saw the tin country for the first time: hills gnawed to the white bone, tailing ponds laid out below the road like fields of poured cloud, and then, far off, his first dredge — a grey wooden ark aground on its own pond, groaning as its bucket-chain climbed and fell. It ran all night. Everything there ran all night; that was the first thing the north taught you. Lying in the Khoos' guest wing he could hear it through the marble, a heartbeat under the valley, and had understood by morning that here the earth itself was on shift work.

The house was five years old and the size of a customs office, electric to the last cupboard, floored in stone from Italy, and nearly empty. Old Khoo Teik Swee received them in a hall that could have held the seven-doors household three times over: a heavy man gone loose in his clothes, hospitable in bursts, whose sentences sometimes stopped in the middle while his eyes went somewhere pleasanter — *unwell*, the aunts had said, in the voice that means a thing with a pipe in it. A brother-in-law did the actual negotiating. The dead wife was on the wall in a gilt frame, twice life size. And pouring tea beneath her mother's portrait, in a house full of servants and no family, was the girl from the photograph.

At dinner she was exactly correct, and Boon Siew, who had spent six years watching one girl be exactly correct in a front hall as a discipline of survival, recognized the workmanship and began, unwillingly, to pay attention.

On the second day the machinery granted them their interview: a walk through the garden, an aunt trailing at forty charitable paces, and then the room Siew Ean led him to as though concluding a tour, which held no piano at all. It held a gramophone in a cabinet of pale wood, and shelves of records — hundreds of them, each paper sleeve numbered in violet ink on the spine, catalogued in a ledger that lay open on the cabinet like a hymnal.

"The piano was my mother's," she said, following his look. "I let them keep saying I play it. It saves the aunts inventing something worse. These are mine." She wound the machine with the unhurried competence of a tally clerk and set the needle down, and a dance band from London, two years old and a world away, began to play into the enormous house. She let it play eight bars, watching not him but the record turning, and then she said, "One hundred and forty-one. My father owns six dredges and I own one hundred and forty-one records, and people say we are nothing alike. We are exactly alike. We are both mining. He brings up tin. I bring up this" — a small motion at the horn, the band, the crackle — "other people's evenings, in a house where it is never evening, only night shift."

He laughed before he knew he would, and she noted the laugh the way her father's clerks noted a yield, and put it somewhere.

They talked records, then ships, then — because he was stubborn about fairness even at his own scaffold — honestly. He did not perform for her. She had been performed for, she told him later, much later, by every son the fan had brought north, and had watched each one act the part of a man delighted; and here instead was a courteous, quiet person of twenty being scrupulously kind to her and absent in some room of himself with the door shut, and turning, when the pauses came, a fountain pen in his fingers — an old pen, worn gold, not a rich boy's pen — the way other men touched cigarettes.

She stopped the gramophone with her finger on the edge of the disc, so that the music slurred once and ceased, and in the silence of the great house with the dredge beating under it she said, "I will tell you what I have worked out, and you will tell me nothing, and that will do. Your heart is a locked room. I have stood in front of enough locked doors in this house to know one by the paint." She said it without drama, without hurt, in the tone of a woman confirming a delivery date. "I am not asking to live in it. I am asking not to be lied to. That is the whole of my price. Everything else — the aunts, the almanacs, the tin, the rubber — has been paid for already, by other people, in a currency I have never once been allowed to touch. But the lying they cannot do for you. That part would be yours, personally. Do not do it, and you and I will manage this thing like two honest clerks sent by our firms, and be kinder to each other than most of the marriages in this valley."

The pen had gone still in his hand. He said — and he reported his own words to Mei Foong evenly, sparing himself nothing — "You deserve better than what they are buying you."

"Obviously," said Khoo Siew Ean, and set the needle back down, and the band resumed mid-bar as though nothing whatever had happened. "So does the tin. It goes into the crucible anyway. Sit down, Mr. Tan; this next one you will like; it is the sound of people who think the world is going to last."

Downstairs, over three days, the elders did the work the fan had come to do. The eight characters were matched and found harmonious — the temple astrologer of Ipoh saw no obstacle, and would have been remarkable among astrologers had he seen one, with six dredges running. Terms were agreed with a decorum that did not quite hide its resemblance to the negotiations for a shipment of smoked sheet. And the almanac was consulted for the wedding itself and gave them, after the mourning terms of a Khoo grand-uncle and the schedules of two households were subtracted, the third month of the year of the Horse: spring, 1942. Her twenty-first year. Eighteen months away — and everyone in both halls said *eighteen months* comfortably, as though it were a station down a known line, a place you arrived at by simply continuing.

Boon Siew was present at the fixing, and silent. The machinery had never needed his voice. It had only ever needed his body in the room, and it had that, in a well-cut suit, one hand in his pocket around an old fountain pen.

---

The telegram reached Malacca on a Friday, brought up from the firm by the chief clerk and read aloud in the front hall to the assembled household: matters concluded — date fixed — third month, Horse year — home Thursday. The house went up around Mei Foong like a lamp being lit, aunts and amahs and Third Aunt all talking at once, eighteen months of lists beginning on the spot.

She had held his folded page for nine days, and had told herself that the judgment she was weighing was tactical — the tonnages, the traceable knowledge, the counting of men in the trade. Standing in the noise with the telegram being passed from hand to hand, she understood her own books at last, both entries: what she had truly been asking was whether the singing was worth anything now that the cage was ordered, and whether posting his voice could be told apart, in her own heart, from keeping some of him for herself. The lesson from Singapore had words for it. Every transaction lives twice: what is gained, and what it costs. She had never once sent his letters for her own sake, and she would not begin by withholding one for her own sake either.

On market morning she walked to the Kling Street counter with the household mail — the Bibik's temple letter, an order for velvet, three inquiries about wedding kueh — and posted MERBOK in the middle of them, where he had always gone, from the hand nobody saw.

---

They came home Thursday at dusk, the Bentley rolling up Heeren Street grey with two days of trunk road, and the household stood out at the doors to receive them in order of rank, Mei Foong at the edge of the arrangement where she stood in all the arrangements. The Towkay descended like a returning fleet. The Bibik, tired and shining, was borne indoors on a surf of aunts. Boon Siew got down last, and stood a moment in the street looking up at the house of seven doors as a man looks at a sum he has checked three times and must now sign.

The announcement was made that evening in the front hall, formally, to the household entire — the date, the almanac's blessing, the merits of the Khoo family enumerated — and rice wine went round in thimble cups, and Third Aunt cried, who cried at weddings not yet begun on principle. And at the end of it, in front of every gathered soul from the chief clerk to the newest kitchen girl, Boon Siew crossed the hall to where Mei Foong stood, carrying the firm's ledger that had travelled home in the motorcar with the accounts of the journey, and held it out to her with both hands, correct as a clerk before an auditor.

"Miss Ho," he said. "Three weeks of accounts have waited for you. Ipoh does not improve anyone's arithmetic."

A small wind of notice went through the servants at the *Miss* — Third Aunt's eyebrow filed it for the kitchen court — but it passed at once, because it looked like what it was, the son of the house being formal on a formal night. Mei Foong received the ledger with both hands and bent her head the correct half-inch, and said the accounts would be ready by Monday, and the hall moved on to its wine.

She opened the book that night at the long table, alone under the lamp, and found it at once, folded small and square at the page where the margins lived: newsprint.

The letters column, Saturday's paper, bought in Ipoh. MERBOK, Malacca, at the foot of it — below a new note from the editors, two lines in italic, that the views of correspondents were their own; the paper had never troubled to say so before, and the two nervous lines measured better than anything else how far the letter had gone. She read it through again in print, the lamp steady: the boom that was a lamp visible far out to sea; the safety sold forward; the drills that must stop being theatre. *The enemy is here already, and its name is complacency, and it holds a full glass.* Printed Saturday. The date had been fixed on the Thursday. The two facts had ridden home together, two days down the trunk road, folded one inside the other in an accounts book, to the only person on earth who could read both at once.

That was the message, and there were no words in it, which was how the truest entries in their ledger had always been made. The cage was ordered; the fitting was booked; the door stood open for eighteen more months, and the bird had looked at all of it and gone on singing — and of the whole world, she alone held both entries, the gain and the cost, and could never show the balance to a living soul.

She sat a long time. Then she did the accounts, because they were hers.

The journey's expenses were all in order, written in the travelling clerk's fist: the ferries, the hotels in Kuala Lumpur, the astrologer's red packet, and — she had known it would be there — the Bentley: petrol, oil, the hired syce's wages, a puncture mended at Tapah. She checked every figure and every figure stood. Then she dipped her pen — his pen was back in the drawer; she had looked first thing, the way you look at a healed place — and in the margin beside the Bentley's account, small, in ink, disguised as checking, she drew the bird.

*The merbok ate again,* she wrote beneath it, in characters a careless eye would take for tallying. And then, because the margins were hers now too, and someone in this house had to keep the true books: *and sang.*

She ruled the column off, clean through the wingtip, folded the clipping into Lesson Seven — *Profit and Loss* — where no one in this house would ever think to look, and closed the ledger on the year of the Horse, eighteen months away, coming up the line toward them all like a scheduled train.
