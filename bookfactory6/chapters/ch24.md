# Between Flags

The sentries stopped requiring the bow on a Wednesday in the middle of August, and Heeren Street understood before any voice told it: something that had made men bow was gone out from under the town in the night. Mei Foong crossed at the bridge with the marketing basket, and the boy soldier at the rail — seventeen, perhaps, the age the whole street had learned not to see — stood looking down at the river as though the river were leaving, and did not glance at her at all; and she walked past him unbowed for the first time in three years and seven months, and her back felt the strangeness of it the whole way home, like a garment taken off after so long that the skin no longer believes in weather.

The day before — a Tuesday — Nagata had come to the account room at his usual hour and had not opened the books. He had sat with his hat on his knees, which she had never once seen him do, and looked at the ledger where it lay squared between them, and said, "Leave the totals. There is no one left to lie to." Then he had gone away down the passage with the tread of a man carrying something he had never set down before, and she had thought about that tread all evening, and in the morning the sentries were not requiring the bow.

What had happened arrived the way everything true had arrived since the flags changed: rumor first, paper after. There had been a broadcast, the rumor said, the voice of the Emperor himself, high and far off and recorded, speaking a court language his own soldiers could barely follow; and the word the rumor finally settled on was not victory, and was not exactly surrender either; the word was *endure*. The town turned that word over for two days the way she held banknotes to the light. Then the Towkay walked into the front hall in the middle of the afternoon and put his hand on the walnut wireless he had turned to silence in February of 'forty-two with the gesture of a man closing the eyes of the dead, and opened its eyes again; and the set warmed, and hummed, and spoke English; and the English said that the war was over.

Nobody in the front hall cheered. The house had unlearned cheering the way it had unlearned firecrackers, and besides, the news was three years and seven months too late for the only ear it was wanted in. The kitchen court took it standing; Third Aunt sat down abruptly on the well-step with her apron to her eyes, and got up inside a minute and beat the dinner out of three eggs as though the eggs had occupied the town personally. It was the first great news of Mei Foong's life that could not be carried to the amahs' room, and she stood a moment at the foot of the back stair with it, undelivered, before she went up to the Lim house; and there, in the doorway, Mei Lan — in her third year now, sturdy, black-eyed, with a brow that at certain angles of lamplight stopped Mei Foong's breath in her body — looked from face to face of the grown people standing so still around the wireless and asked the question of the season: "Is it a festival?"

"Yes," said Bee Nio, after a moment. "The kind without cakes."

---

Then the town found out what it had never before had occasion to learn: that between one law and the next there is a gap, and the gap has no floor.

The soldiers drew back to their barracks and their compounds and waited, correct and armed and suddenly provisional, for whoever was coming to accept them. Out of the rubber and out of the hills came men the town had spent three years not mentioning, thin men with three stars on their caps, and notices appeared on the walls where the proclamations used to go, signed by committees nobody had elected and nobody argued with. The banana money died in an afternoon — not devalued: dead, refused at every stall at once, by common instinct, like meat gone off — and Third Aunt lit the stove with a ten-dollar note and said nothing historic; it was simply the driest paper in the house. In the household book the dollar column simply stopped, mid-page, and the rice column carried on alone, and Mei Foong ruled no line under the dead currency and felt no triumph at having kept the true book all along. Being right about ruin is the cheapest of all correct sums.

Accounts were settled that fortnight, and not all of them in rice. A godown broker who had grown sleek on requisitions was marched through the market with a placard on his chest and came home stripped to his singlet, alive, relieved of everything portable. A man was found in the river by the bridge one morning, and the street did not ask after him by name, having had three years of practice. The clerk of the padang did not run; perhaps there was nowhere north enough for what he owed. He shuttered his windows and stayed inside them, and the street began that week what it would keep up for years, which was to spit — old aunties, women who fed beggars off their own plates, spitting in the public road at a closed door. Nobody touched him. The street had settled on a longer sentence.

And Mei Foong, watching the gap open day by day, understood one account of her own had fallen due, and began to prepare for her creditor. She had been expecting him since the Tuesday of the hat.

---

The shophouse stood near the bridge end of the street, two doors up from the kopitiam, the firm's property since before the flood year: a cloth merchant's premises once, empty since 'forty-two, when the tenant had gone upcountry ahead of his debts and never written. The keys lived on the firm's board, and the firm's keys lived in Mei Foong's keeping, and in the week of the paper-burning she had chosen the house for exactly what it was — a shuttered nothing between a coffee shop and a dead tailor's, a house no list cared about — and had lodged Nagata's private ledger behind the loose brick of the lamp niche in its little ancestral hall, beside the lacquered box that had lain there, wrapped and silent, for two years already. The tenants' altar table still stood against that wall, too heavy to have been worth carting into ruin. There were two lamp niches, one each side, arched like small doorways for small gods; the soot of old wicks ghosted both; only one of them had a brick that came away in her hand.

Now, in the nights of the gap, she took the ledger out and did the work she had decided on the well-step, calmly, while Third Aunt wept and recovered.

She copied it. Entry by entry, in the lamplight of the empty shop, in the same small upright hand in which the original stood — for the original was in her hand too; he had dictated and she had entered, Tuesday upon Tuesday, and that had been his cunning once, to keep his confession in a Chinese girl's writing, and it was her cunning now. Nine leaves she did not copy. They were the pages with the street in them: who had bought passes and with what; whose daughter's bangles had paid for quinine; which widow's wedding gold had turned a requisition aside. She slit them out of the original with the beading razor and fed them to the stove in the shophouse court, one at a time, unhurried, reading none of them twice; whatever book went wherever now, those people had bought their lives once, at his prices, and would not pay twice. What remained, in either book, hanged nobody but the man who had dictated it — and the hand that had written it down.

Then she sewed the original into oilcloth and carried it to the vegetarian hall where Ah Yoke's sworn sisters kept their lamps, and gave it to the eldest with three sentences: whose hand had written it; that it was to sleep; and that if harm came to her by anything but age, it was to wake, and go and find whichever magistrate the new flag provided. The sisterhood took it the way it took everything, without questions, into the smell of sandalwood and starch. She walked home lighter by exactly the weight a hostage weighs.

---

He came on the sixth night of the gap, after the second curfew that no one was enforcing, and knocked at the shutter of the shophouse — not the mansion; he had been a methodical man for fifty years and was not going to stop in his last week in Malaya; he knew the firm's properties by heart and he had seen the seam of lamplight. When she opened the wicket he stood in the five-foot way in a collarless shirt and rubber-soled shoes, two days unshaven, a canvas rucksack on one shoulder, and a flat parcel under his arm; and the strangest thing about him, stranger than the missing suit, was the hurry in him. She had known him three years as a man who had never once been made to hurry. It came off him now like heat off a wall.

"Miss Ho." He took off his hat to her, which he had never done. "I have come for my book."

She stood back and let him into the hall, and did not light a second lamp. The one flame made its small circle on the altar table and left the walls alone. There was no tea. It was not a Tuesday.

She laid the ledger on the table between them. He did with it what she had known he would do, being of her own trade: he did not read it, he counted it, thumb against the leaves, twice.

"There were forty-one leaves," he said. "There are thirty-two."

"Nine are ash. They were the pages with the street in them." She kept her voice at the level of the flame. "Wherever this book goes now, and whoever reads it over your shoulder on the way — those people are not in it."

He turned the leaves then, one and another, and stopped, and she watched him understand the other thing, the larger thing, and his stillness change quality.

"This is not my book," Nagata said. "This is a copy."

"It is your book in every entry that is yours."

The lamp hissed. He set the ledger down with great gentleness, and stood with his hands loose at his sides, and was quiet in a way she had never heard from him in three years of Tuesdays; and the room did its own arithmetic around her, unbidden: an empty house, a shuttered street, a town with no law in it, a man with a rucksack and nothing left south of Siam to lose. For the first time since he had hung his hat on the firm's hook he was dangerous, and both of them stood inside the fact of it, and she thought — once, exactly, and put it away — of February, and Johor, and how tiring he had said the work was. She did not move. Stillness was the whole of her arsenal. It had not yet failed her.

"The original," she said, "is not in this house. It is where the sworn sisters keep what they are given, sewn shut, asleep. If I am hurt, it wakes and finds whoever governs next. If I am left in peace, it sleeps as long as I do." She let that stand its moment, and then gave him the rest, worst number first, the way she had been taught by a man who was not in the room. "And it is in my hand, Nagata-san. Every entry. The book that hangs you hangs me. You have audited my bonds for three years. Tell me if you have ever found one bad."

For a long breath he looked at her across the lamp. Then he put his hat on the altar table, and sat down on the tenant's abandoned stool like a man whose knees had been dismissed, and rubbed his face with both hands.

"All day, for three years, everyone lied to me," he said, to the floor. "My superiors downward, my inferiors upward, myself in both directions. One honest thing in Malacca." He laughed, or something adjacent to it. "I am being audited now, Miss Ho. Everywhere. On every road north there is a table and a man behind it, and this time it is my occupation in the folder." He straightened, and took the flat parcel from under his arm, and held it a moment on his knees. "So. I came to trade, not to beg. You knew that before you cut the pages, or you would not have cut them."

He put it on the table and pushed it into the light: a folder of stiff government board, tied with tape, its spine chopped in purple and inked with the year written the way the occupation had insisted all years be written — 2603.

"Every registration has its duplicate," he said. "Every amendment keeps a copy of what it amends. That is what a registry is: the office where nothing is ever only itself." He undid the tape with two fingers and folded the board back, and there it lay, three years of her nights made paper: the district file from the country, eleventh month of 2602, mother's name and no father's name; and pinned above it, riding it the way a stamp rides a letter, the amendment of 'forty-three, the doctor's household, the second registration, the child entered anew under the name she answered to now. "I stamped the doctor's paper without looking up. You were in the street outside; I remember the umbrella. That was the gift." He squared the file to the table's edge, precisely, terribly, his one tenderness. "Then I sent for the duplicate and read it for a morning. That was the insurance. I am a small man, Miss Ho. Small men hold what comes to hand." He stood, and stepped back from the table, leaving the folder in the light. "I find I do not want to be holding it when I am caught. Whatever else is entered against me — not a child. Take it. Give me my book, in your hand, minus your street, and we are settled at true prices."

She took the folder. She gave him the copy. Their two trades crossed above the lamp without touching, like the halves of a tally.

At the wicket door, with the night behind him, he stopped. "My daughter is fourteen now," he said. "If the ships are kind I will see her by the new year. I will tell her that her father was a clerk in the south." He settled the rucksack strap. "It is even true."

Then he bowed — not the exact calibrated depth of any letter of appointment, but low, unmetered, the depth a man gives where a debt cannot be entered in figures — and put on his hat, and went out into the gap between flags, and turned north; and whatever road took him, no entry in any book of this account follows him down it.

---

She read the file once through by the lamp, because she audited everything she burned. It was all there, as he had said, each paper the other's death: the true birth and the false one, the widow of the country registry and the doctor's daughter of the town, held together by a pin a clerk had pressed home in 'forty-three without knowing he was pinning a life. Then she carried it to the stove in the back court and fed it in leaf by leaf, unhurried, reading none of it twice, and stood in the smoke of the lie's whole paper trail while it went up over the roofs of Malacca, where records of that kind are kept.

And when the pan held only heat, she went back into the hall and took out the loose brick, and lifted down the lacquered box, and knelt with it in the lamp's circle, and opened it: the child's slipper, small as her palm, peony and phoenix in miniature; and beneath the silk, folded flat, the paper from the country registry — her own copy, the mother's copy, eleventh month, her name in the mother's column and the father's column empty, empty, checked three times the night it was written and empty ever since. Safety was one motion away. She had just burned its twin; the stove was still hot; and with this last leaf gone, Mei Lan was the doctor's daughter all the way down, and no road led back, and nothing on earth could ever unravel to hurt her.

She held it toward the heat of the court, and could not.

The ring could be dumb; rings are dumb by trade. The photograph was folded; heaven kept its register where no clerk could send for duplicates. Everything else that said the child was hers could die with her and would. This one leaf was the only thing in the world that said it in ink; and a person cannot burn the only proof that her life happened; she had learned that from a woman who carried a red-thread bundle across two seas and a war rather than travel light. She folded the paper back under the silk, beside the slipper, and fitted the lid, and set the box on the shelf of the niche, square to the edge; and then she went, the next evening, to the one pair of hands in Malacca that could help her close the wall properly.

Dr. Lim came at midnight with his sleeves rolled, and asked one question, mixing the mortar to the stiffness she showed him: "Am I closing it over anything I should see first?"

"You have seen it," she said. "You signed for its cover in 'forty-three."

He nodded, and took up the trowel. The departed tenant had left half a stack of pressed brick under the back stair and a bag of cement gone hard at one corner, ordered for a renovation the war had cancelled; good English brick, machine-faced, sharp at the arris, nothing like the soft orange bones of the old house, and there was no help for that; what mattered now was under the plaster, not behind it. The wall of 'forty-three stood true, course on course, wanting only its one dry brick; he buttered it and bedded it home and struck the joint flat, the way he closed a wound, and she stood at his shoulder with the lamp and thought: a repair says *something broke here*. This must say nothing at all. So they made it say nothing — bricks true, mortar hard, and over the face of it her plaster, floated out to the plane of the wall with a beader's patience, feathered to nothing at the edges; and when the whole wall was limewashed together no eye that had not held the brick would find the rectangle. It wanted to disappear, and it would very nearly manage it for sixty years, until other hands came who read walls for a living.

Dr. Lim washed his hands at the well afterwards, longer than the mortar required. "The British will come with forms," he said at last, drying each finger. "Births, deaths, claims. Every flag arrives believing paper starts over." He put on his spectacles. "It never starts over. It only changes hands. What we did tonight, we did for good."

"I know it," said Mei Foong; and the lamp niche's twin, on the altar's other side, kept its little arch open beside the blind one, like a house with one eye shut.

---

The British came back in the first week of September, to cheering.

The town that had learned everything at walking pace learned this at a run: lorries with round-faced boys in green throwing chocolate they did not have enough of, warships going down the strait with their signal lamps talking, children riding the running boards the way no child had ridden anything in four years. Flags nobody had seen since the blackout came up out of chests and floated over the five-foot ways, and the Bibik brought hers down from the camphor chest herself — folded under the winter quilts since 'forty-two, creased white along its lines like an old face — and hung it from the upper shutter with her own hands, correctly, and stepped back into the street to see it hang. Her face under it was not celebration. The house had guessed by then what she was doing, because with governments come lists, and with lists comes the end of the cruelest mercy there is; she was hanging out the old flag and the new grief together, and the street let her stand there alone as long as she wanted.

The relief rice reached the clinic that same week, sacks with red crosses stencilled on the gunny, and Dr. Lim, who had doctored a street for three years in eggs and gratitude and dwindling bottles, put his hand flat on the topmost sack the way he took a pulse, and wept in front of the whole queue without taking off his spectacles, and nobody in the queue looked away, because it was everyone's weeping and he had merely been elected.

And the associations opened their books. That was how it was done, in the end — no wire, no telegram, no officer at the door; the clan hall set out ink and a compiler, and the families of Malacca came, day after day, to give what they had, which was names. The Towkay put on the dark suit that hung loose on him now, and the Bibik put on grey, and Mei Foong walked behind them carrying the umbrella, and in the hall the compiler — an exact man, a schoolmaster before the war — asked only what was known, and wrote only what was answered, because exactness was the last tribute left in anyone's power. *Tan Boon Siew. Heeren Street. Taken, second month, 1942.* Nothing after the comma; the Towkay stood over the wet ink a moment, reading his son into the column among the sons of the town; and the Bibik, who for three years had refused a tablet because a tablet was for the dead and she would not have her boy dead by inference, said, in the voice she used for ordering the year's kueh: "Now the tablet. Sandalwood. The gilder on Java Lane, not the cheap one." Hope was permitted to die only in good order, formally, entered.

The tablet came home in the last week of September and took its place on the ancestral altar among four generations, small, dark, gold-charactered, new; and the house held its rite with the doors open, incense and offerings and the street looking respectfully in. Mei Lan was there in her festival clothes, nearly three, overawed for as long as a child is overawed; the smoke made her sneeze; and she put up her arms out of the middle of the solemnity and called — "Ma" — and Bee Nio bent and gathered her up and held her through the bowing, the child's cheek against her cheek, both faces turned toward the new tablet; and Mei Foong stood behind them in her place, two steps back, in the smoke of her husband's incense, and said nothing, and held the saying-nothing the way the altar held the flame.

---

The tending of the altar fell to her the way the feeding of the merbok had once fallen to whoever got there first — quietly, and then by custom, and then it was hers. Every morning before the house woke she dusted the tablets, refilled the oil, trimmed the wicks; the Bibik, watching once from the doorway, said only, "The girl was always careful," and let it stand; and so it came about that of all the household, only Mei Foong ever opened the offering box — the shallow black-and-red box that lived before the tablets and held the altar's small stores, spare wicks, matches, a knife for the wick-trimming, things nobody counted and nobody else touched.

She cleaned the new tablet exactly as she cleaned the others, the same cloth, the same number of strokes, not one beat longer, because the house that saw everything must see nothing; and on the third morning she brought the plate.

It had lain wrapped in the indigo cloth for three and a half years, moved from seam to case to floorboard, the glass twin of the folded print: Chan Brothers' plate, one dawn of the Year of the Horse fixed in silver, and on the glass everything stood reversed, light for dark, the lilies black, the morning shadowed — and whole. On the glass no one was folded away. On the glass the bride stood beside her husband with her face open to whoever should ever hold it to the light, his hand around her hand in front of the lens and the lilies, feet neat as type, married. No one would hold it to the light. That was the whole terms of it, and she had accepted the terms on a registration morning two years ago and did not reopen settled accounts. She wrapped the plate again, and lifted the wicks and the matches, and laid it flat in the bottom of the offering box, and set the altar's small stores back over it, and closed the lid; and now they stood together before his name, in glass, in the dark, in the one place in the house where only she would ever look.

The jade ring stayed where it had lived since the nun's stool: on its cord at her neck, inside the collar, against the skin, warm. Hands are seen.

That night, in the amahs' room, she took his pen from the drawer and unscrewed the cap and wrote the first letter in two years — the years when paper had been dangerous and truth on paper a rope — and the pen went as it had always gone, as if no time were owed.

*Dear husband. The flags have changed again. I am still your amah. Our daughter called another woman Mother today, and I said nothing, and the saying-nothing is the loudest thing I have ever done. Your name has gone up on a tablet in the front hall of your father's house, and I clean it, and nobody wonders why the amah cleans it so well — cleaning is what I am for. The rice has come back to the town. The truth has not. I have checked the accounts twice: it is not coming. I will keep them anyway. — Your wife, in the only office that never changed flags.*

She folded the sheet small and put it into the tin among the others and pressed the lid home, and the post office of the dead received it, losing nothing, answering nothing. Outside, Heeren Street lay rehearsing its peace: a wireless somewhere talking London through the shutters, the kopitiam's late lamp, the new flag stirring over doorways that had worn three flags in one girl's lifetime. The war was over; the street said so, the ships said so, the paper said so in three languages. Hers was not. Hers was the war of the shut mouth, and no broadcast ends that kind, and no flag flies for it; and she put out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark with her hand over the ring at her breastbone — not waiting; keeping — and then lay down, its whole army, to sleep before duty.
