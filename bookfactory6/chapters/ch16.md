# The Screening

The men of Heeren Street went out at first light in their cleanest shirts, carrying their papers, and the women stood in the doorways to see them go. There was no music, and every man walked at the same unhurried pace, and that was how you knew what kind of procession it was.

The proclamation had been on the walls for two days, pasted where the auction notices used to go: all Chinese males of the town between fifteen and fifty years to present themselves at the padang, by quarters, with their identity papers, for examination. The word the poster used was mild, a clerk's word, and nobody in Malacca was deceived by it, because for weeks the news had been coming up the road from the south the way true news came now, without paper, and none of it arrived as fact. It arrived as the way men's voices dropped when the island was mentioned. It arrived as a new habit the whole town had learned in a fortnight, of not asking after people by name.

The night before, Boon Siew had burned his drafts in Third Aunt's stove — four years of MERBOK's workings brought down from the firm's office in a bolster case, the crossed-out beginnings, the tonnage tables copied fair — feeding them to the fire one at a time, unhurried, reading none of them, while Mei Foong stood beside him and added the clipping she had kept folded into Lesson Seven, *Profit and Loss*, where no one in that house would ever have looked. Neither said why. Both of them could read. The fire took four years of singing in a quarter of an hour.

Paper burns. What a man knows does not. The tonnages were still in his head, and his hand, set to a page, would still make the same figures the same way, and there was no stove for that. Mei Foong understood this exactly, because she had said it herself, a year and a lifetime ago, at the account drawer: *someone will start counting the men in the trade*. It had been an argument, then. Now it walked beside her husband down the passage to the door in a clean shirt, and could not be taken from him, and could not be burned.

In the morning she opened the shallow left-hand drawer of the black desk, the drawer of nibs and blotting sand and red thread, and the fountain pen was gone. He had taken it the way he did everything that mattered, quickly, without asking, as if speed could get a thing past the sentries. She stood with her hand on the open drawer for the length of a breath, and closed it, and went to help with the tea that nobody drank.

At the door he stopped and told the Bibik he expected to be home by evening; the poster said examination, and an examined man was a stamped man, and a stamped man came home. He said it steadily, and the Bibik received it steadily, and both performances held. He did not look at Mei Foong, because he was a careful man now in the one matter where he had never been careful, and a look was a thing that could be seen. He looked instead down the length of the house — the passage, the first air well letting down its light, the turn where the kitchen court began — at the house entire. Then the Ee sons were calling from the five-foot way, and he went out among the neighbours, one clean shirt among many, and the street took him with the rest.

---

The padang had been roped the day before, and the queues formed by streets, so that a man waited among the faces he had waited among all his life — at the barber's, at the rice shop, at the temple on feast days — and this was either a kindness or a method, and was probably a method, and worked as both. The soldiers stood at the rope at intervals, not many of them, bored in the rising heat. At the far end, under a canvas fly, were the tables.

Boon Siew queued between the elder Ee son and old Heng the rice merchant, and did what he had come intending to do, which was to be of use in a line. When the sun cleared the shophouse roofs he took off his hat and put it on Heng, whose head was bare, and answered Heng's objection by saying he had been meaning to lose that hat for a year and could Heng please cooperate. He carried the stationer's water bottle back along the rope, a mouthful to each man of their quarter, the way rice had gone bead by bead through Ah Yoke's scoop in January. And he coached the tally clerk's nephew, a boy of sixteen with his fear standing in his eyes like water, in the only catechism he had to give: answer what is asked. Answer it short. Do not offer English, do not offer opinions, do not smile. If your voice will not hold, count the queue while you wait, the way you count a ledger — from the back, in tens.

"What are you going to answer, Mr. Tan?" the boy asked him — not asking that at all; asking, will it be all right.

"The truth, shortly," Boon Siew said. "It is the easiest thing to remember."

The queue went forward by inches all morning. Ahead, at the tables, the machinery could be watched and learned: papers passed across, a question, sometimes two, sometimes ten; and beside the tables, on a bench in the shade, sat three men with sacks over their heads, eye-slits cut in the cloth, and every man in the queue had already learned not to look at them, because there was no profit in knowing which of your neighbours' shapes you recognized. Now and then a hooded head would move, or not move, and a man would go left, to a soldier who stamped his shirt-front and his papers with a character in violet ink and waved him through the rope; or he would go right, toward the far corner of the padang, where there were more soldiers and, parked with its tailgate down, a lorry.

Nobody looked directly at the lorry either. It sat in the corner of every eye on the padang, all day, like the sun.

Dr. Lim reached the tables an hour before noon. Boon Siew was near enough by then to hear it done: name, age; occupation — *doctor*; the word passed sideways along the table; hands shown, palm and back, a doctor's hands; a question about the hospital, answered in three words. The stamp came down on his shirt over the heart and again on his papers, violet, and Dr. Lim was through the rope and outside it — and there he stopped, and stood, and did not go home. He planted himself at the rope with his stamped chest, as near the tables as a stamped man could stand, visibly a doctor, visibly useful, in case usefulness could be spent on someone else. It could not. It was the only power he had that day, and it was not transferable, and he stood there spending it anyway, hour after hour, in the sun.

---

By eight in the morning every door on Heeren Street stood open. Not ajar: open, the way the street opened for New Year, and for the opposite reason — so that word could travel, so that no woman was alone with the length of the day. News went from doorway to doorway like a bucket passed along a chain: *they are queued by quarters — the tables have started — they are asking occupations — the Ee cook's brother is stamped, he is coming home.* Each sentence was handed on whole, in a low voice, and the street stood the empty hours between them together, in its doorways, in sight of one another, which was the only shelter going.

In the house of seven doors, Third Aunt fought the day with the kettle. She boiled it at ten and made tea and set out the cups, and no one drank, and at noon she poured the pot away and boiled the kettle again, because boiling it was a task with edges. The cups stood full and cooling on the marble table like offerings. At midday she sent rice to the padang in a tiffin carrier with the gardener's eldest boy, and the boy came back an hour later with the carrier still full: no one was allowed near the rope. Third Aunt set it on the shelf unopened, and boiled the kettle.

Bee Nio stood in the front doorway from morning until dusk and would not sit. She was near her time, and the aunts brought her a chair, and it stood beside her empty all day like the cups, because sitting was a kind of verdict and she declined to return one. She stood with one hand flat where the child was and watched up the street toward the padang.

The Bibik waited in the ancestral hall. She lit the incense with her own hand and stood before four generations of tablets, and what she said to them was not heard by anyone; but the smoke stood up straight in the windless hall all day, and she kept it fed — a woman informing her ancestors of their obligations, keeping open the only door she had rank at.

Ah Yoke stood at the side door to the lane, where the rice queue had stood in January, and the lane was empty, and she stood there anyway with her hands folded into her sleeves, facing the direction of the padang, perfectly still, the way she stood before Guan Yin. Waiting was work. She had done it before. She did not speak all day.

Of all the women in the street, standing in all the doorways, only Mei Foong had no rank in her own waiting. The wives waited as wives, the mothers as mothers, and the street knew what was owed each and paid it — a hand on the arm, the first relay of any news. She was the amah's girl, waiting, it was understood, on the household's behalf; and she stood at the door with her marriage folded away inside her like the exercise book sewn into a quilt seam, an account no living soul but two could read; and she found she could do it, could stand there all day being nobody in particular, because she had eight years of training, and because he had looked down the passage that morning at the house entire, and she had been in the house, and so it had been at her.

In the middle of the afternoon, in the worst of the empty hours, the merbok began to sing in the air well — brightly, endlessly, the way it had sung through the raids, because it could do nothing else. Third Aunt put down the kettle, and went out, and threw a cloth over the cage. It was the first time in her life she had ever touched it.

---

Boon Siew came to the tables in the middle of the afternoon.

There was an officer who did not speak, and an interpreter who spoke for him, and at the end of the table, on a stool, with a wooden file box and a folder of his own, sat a civilian: a narrow-shouldered man of forty-odd in a shirt gone grey at the collar. All day he had sat there while the hooded men did the pointing, saying almost nothing. A season had picked him up from wherever the years had left him and set him down at this table, and the table had made him large, the way a stamp is large: small, and hard, and coming down on things.

Name. He gave it. Age. He gave it. Occupation.

"Clerk, in my father's firm. Rubber."

The interpreter wrote. It might have ended there; it was ending there, the papers already moving back across the table; and the narrow-shouldered man looked up from his folder, and their eyes crossed, the way strangers' eyes cross, for half a step.

This time it was not half a step.

Mei Foong had seen him once, from a five-foot way, in the flood year, holding his firm's shamed duplicate book to his chest, taking down in some inner ledger everything the world owed him. Boon Siew had never seen him at all. That was the difference between them, and it had been collecting interest for five years, and here, at a table on the padang, the account was presented. The clerk said a word to the interpreter. Then he opened his folder and laid out, squared to the corners, a small stack of newspaper cuttings, and Boon Siew, who could read a page upside down across a table, read the top one. The letters column. MERBOK, Malacca, at the foot.

The interpreter asked whether he wrote for the newspapers.

"I write invoices," Boon Siew said.

The clerk spoke again, low, at length this time, and he did not consult the cuttings while he spoke, because he did not need to; he had them the way Mei Foong had her mother's name, by heart, from carrying. The interpreter listened, and put the question the clerk had built for him — a tradesman's question, with a hook in it that only one pair of hands in Malacca could have baited: a figure, a tonnage, a godown figure from a letter three years old — whose figure was it, and how did a songbird come to know it?

There was a silence of a certain shape.

Boon Siew looked at the cuttings, squared on the table between the stamp and the second book, and Mei Foong was never told, and never needed telling, what went through him in that silence, because she kept his books and knew his method: worst number first, no cost hidden, then pay. A lie might buy an hour, and would be broken by dinner, because the man on the stool had the whole sum done already and wanted only the signature; and a broken lie spread. A broken lie reached the firm, the house, the hands that posted things.

"The letters are mine," Boon Siew said. "All of them. I wrote them alone, I posted them myself, and no one in my father's house has ever read one. My father thought MERBOK a fool, and said so at his own table. He was probably right."

The interpreter translated. The officer looked at Boon Siew for the first time, briefly, the way one looks at a figure before initialling it. The clerk sat back on his stool, and put his cuttings together, and squared them, and returned them to the folder, and his face did nothing at all, and that was the worst thing on the padang that day: not triumph; entry. A man posting a ledger that had finally balanced.

They did not stamp his shirt. The stamp stood in its pad an inch from his papers and was not picked up. They wrote his name in the second book. A soldier touched his arm and pointed.

That was all it took. A touch, and a point.

He went where the pointing said, across the corner of the padang toward the lorry, at the same unhurried pace at which the whole street had walked out that morning, and he did not look at the rope where the released men stood, because a man looking for a face gives the face away. At the tailgate he was made to give up nothing; there was nothing to give up; a clean shirt, papers that no longer mattered, and inside the shirt pocket, where the sun could not fade it, a fountain pen the padang could not see. He put his hand once on the tailgate hinge, and went up into the lorry among strangers from other streets, and stood, because there was nothing to sit on, and faced forward.

At the rope the released men of Heeren Street stood in a line with the violet stamps over their hearts, and did not call out, because it had been made very clear that there was no calling out, and Dr. Lim stood at the end of them, stamped and useless, his spectacles flashing blank in the low sun, doing the only thing that remained, which was to see it — to stand very straight and see all of it, so that the house should at least be told by someone who had not looked away.

The engine started. The lorry came around in a wide, slow, ordinary curve, the curve of any lorry in any yard, and went out of the padang past the rope, not fast, and was gone behind the shophouses. The dust it raised hung in the evening light for a while. Then that was gone too, and there was only the road, and the men standing in it.

---

Dr. Lim came up Heeren Street at dusk through the open doors, and the news came up the street ahead of him, door to door, so that by the time he reached the house of seven doors there was nothing left for him to carry but the manner of it. He stood in the doorway with the stamp over his heart and told them plainly, in order, leaving nothing out and adding nothing: the tables, the cuttings, the questions, the answer their son had chosen, the lorry. He had spent his working life saying hard things to seated families. He said this one standing, to a standing house.

The house minus one.

The Bibik went to the ancestral hall and was heard by no one. The Towkay stood in the front hall among the cold full teacups, and the sentence he arrived at, out of everything, was: "Schoolboy talk." It came out of him in a voice nobody had heard him use, and it was not a verdict on his son; it was the last five years arriving on him at once, every dinner at which he had said it, presented for payment in a single call. He went into his office and shut the door, and the lamp burned under it all night.

In the morning he began to spend.

He was a man who repaired in the only currency he trusted, and he set about the ransom of his son with the method he had brought to rubber: intermediaries, agents, margins. Mei Foong unpicked the quilt seam and brought the exercise book to the account room, and the Towkay, who had built the hoard against hunger, now bid it against silence, and silence is not a market. A contractor who supplied the garrison took gold in a folded cloth and returned in six days with word that the matter was military and closed to civilians. A broker who claimed a cousin among the interpreters took more, twice, and returned the second time without the cousin. A towkay from Singapore knew a channel; the channel took its fee in advance; the channel did not answer again. Mei Foong entered each outlay in the back of the exercise book, upside down and inward, in the small upright hand: *west room, third mattress, seam nearest wall: eight* became five, became two, became a line ruled through. She wrote it all, because if a thing is spent and not written it is lost, and something in her held to the law even now: nothing invented, nothing left out, the totals carried honestly, even where they hurt.

It was the last intermediary who was worth what he cost: an old Baba produce dealer with connections he did not boast of, who took the gold and was gone three weeks, and came back and set most of it down again on the table in its cloth, keeping only his expenses, which he itemized. There were no lists to be bought, he said. There was no office that sold releases, whatever was whispered in three towns; men who said otherwise were selling hope by the tael and would sell it as long as it was bought. The men taken from the padang had been taken beyond the reach of money. He was sorry. He had been paid to learn the truth, and that was the truth, and he would take nothing for it beyond his costs.

The Towkay sat still for a long time after the man had gone. *The house goes together, and the house comes back together,* he had said once, in January. He did not say it now. He counted what was left of the gold with his hands flat on the table, and his hands were an old man's, and Mei Foong entered the returned weight in the book without being asked, because the record at least could still be kept whole.

He had spent a fortune, and it had bought one thing, and the thing was true, which is more than most gold buys. He had bought the knowledge that there was nothing to buy.

Ah Yoke walked. One month off her deathbed, thin as a lath, she put on the black-and-white and went out each morning with joss and oil money knotted into her handkerchief, and walked to every temple in Malacca: to Cheng Hoon Teng first, where she had standing, and stood before Guan Yin with her feet together like a schoolgirl; then to the temple by the hill, and the temple by the river, and the small dim halls in side streets whose names the family did not know, because her theology had narrowed to a single point and any door at all might be the one with someone behind it. She was not bargaining. Mei Foong, who walked with her on the third morning and carried the joss, understood that she was doing what she did with the rice scoop: going down the whole queue, exactly, missing no one, so that whatever power kept the ledgers of heaven should find, when it looked, that on the mortal side every entry had been made.

Bee Nio came to sleep at the house of seven doors, and on the third night she crossed the dark room to Mei Foong's mat, near her time and slow, and lay down top-to-toe as she had at fifteen, and reached down and took hold of Mei Foong's ankle, and held it, and said nothing, and neither of them said why.

Mei Foong did not weep in front of the house. The house never saw her do anything at all, which the house put down to the trained steadiness of the amah's girl, and which was in fact the last service she could do him: he had kept her name off the padang with his answer, and she would keep her grief where it could not undo the keeping. On the evening the last intermediary left, she went alone into the account room and lit the lamp, and took the firm's ledger — the one he had put into her hands before the whole household, *Miss Ho, three weeks of accounts have waited for you* — and she closed it. It had lain open at the current page since the morning of the padang, because closing it had been a kind of verdict and she had declined to return one; now she closed the cover on the unfinished column, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room. She did not rule the column off. Ruling off was for accounts that were settled. She sat with her hands flat on the cover in the lamplight; and below her stilled hands, below the ledger, below everything, in a column of its own somewhere under the heart, something too new and too small to be entered anywhere had begun, that month, to keep its own count. She did not read that entry yet. She sat very straight with her hands on his ledger, and let the lamp burn, and the house around her made the sounds of a house at night, minus one.

---

The tally clerk came back at the end of the second month.

He came in the evening, to the side door, in a shirt that was not his own, so much thinner that Third Aunt, opening the door, said his name as a question. He had been stamped at last and released — some men were, without pattern or explanation, which was its own instruction to a town — and he had walked from the camp gate, and he had not gone first to his own house. He would not come in. He would not eat. He stood in the lane where the rice queue had stood, and he did not say what the holding camp had been like, and no one asked him twice, then or ever.

He took from inside the borrowed shirt a small parcel done up in a scrap of gunny and unwound it with cracked fingers, and held it out: the fountain pen, dark green, mottled like river water under trees, the gold of it unfaded.

It had come out hand to hand, he said. That was how things came out. A man going where he could keep nothing gave what he had to a man who might one day walk to a gate, and with it what weighed nothing at all: words, carried in the head, passed with the object like an address. He had carried his since the first week. He stood in the lane with his hands at his sides and recited it in the voice men use for other men's words, careful, level, looking at nothing:

"Tell Miss Ho the accounts are in her name now."

That was the whole of it. He bowed slightly, in the old way, to the household at large, and went down the lane to his own door, where his small daughter was.

Mei Foong carried the pen through the kitchen court, past the covered cage, through the second air well, into the account room, and lit the lamp. She opened the shallow left-hand drawer, and laid the pen on the folded square of blotting paper where it had lain for five years, squared to the corners, and stood looking at it.

She read the message entirely, both entries at once, the way she had read every page he ever laid in front of her. It was a love letter; it had his whole method in it, the worst number first and no cost hidden, and it said everything the padang had not let him look at her to say. And it was the other thing, and it said that too, plainly, so that she would not spend her years at a gate that was not going to open, buying hope by the tael. He had known she could carry both in one line. It was why the line could be so short.

The accounts were in her name now.

Nothing else ever came back.
