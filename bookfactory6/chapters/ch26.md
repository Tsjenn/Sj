# Everything Back But the Dead

The banana notes came out of the account-room drawer in bundles of a hundred, tied with the firm's own pink tape, and Mei Foong counted them twice, because the Towkay had asked for a figure and a figure was what he would be given. Tens with their bunch of pisang; hundreds with the ox and the plough; the thousands of the last year, printed in such a hurry that you could read the window through them. She entered the total at the foot of a clean page and ruled it off, and wrote nothing beneath the rule, because there was nothing to carry.

"How much," he said from the doorway.

She told him. It was a number out of a story, the kind of sum that had once bought godowns, and it was worth exactly the paper, and the paper was too thin for the stove and too smooth for anything else.

"Put them back," said the Towkay. "Lock it."

"They are not worth the drawer, sir."

"No." He stood a moment with his hand on the door frame, a big man gone loose in his clothes, sixty-three that month and not one of the years showing anywhere but in the hands. "One day a man will sit in that chair and tell me it was not so bad. I will open the drawer."

He went back down the passage, and Mei Foong turned the key on a fortune. Every house on Heeren Street kept such a drawer that year, and none of them could have said why in words a clerk would accept. It was not evidence against the men who had printed it. It was evidence against hope: proof, kept locked, that a thing a whole town had taken in its hands every morning for three and a half years could stop being anything at all between one breakfast and the next. Third Aunt's share lived under the lid of the rice bin. The coffee shop two doors from the firm's empty rental gummed a fan of hundreds under the counter glass and let customers rest their cups on the money.

The rest of the year came back the way the notes did not: gradually, and never quite. Rice stayed on cards and the queue stayed at the side door, and Mei Foong sat to the bin at first light with Ah Yoke's scoop and kept the measure level, and the queue still looked at her hand before it looked at her face. Prices ran ahead of wages like a dog ahead of a man. On the first of April the flag over the Stadthuys meant a new administration with a new name, and the town argued about it in the coffee shops with the ferocity of people who have discovered they are allowed to argue, and went on paying the same for fish.

And in the third week of the rains the carpenter came about the front-hall beam, put his knife into the underside of it and brought out a curl of wood dry as wadding. White ants had been at the house through all the years when no one had time to look up: the beam over the five-foot way was a shell around a nest, holding up the front of the house out of habit. Sound outside, the carpenter said. Eaten within. Not to be seen from the street until the day it comes down.

---

The claims office opened in a room at the town hall with three tables in it, and by the second week the queue for it went along the corridor and out into the sun, and every man in it carried a folder, and every folder was a work of the imagination.

That was the difficulty, and everyone in the queue understood it and nobody said it aloud. Papers had been burned all over Malaya, in courtyards and stoves and drums stirred with iron rods, and the men who burned them had stood on both sides of every counter. The firm's own wartime books said, in a beautiful hand, that four hundred and eleven bales of smoked sheet had been destroyed by damp. They had not been destroyed by damp. They had gone out of the godown at night, dry as biscuit, and the entry that said otherwise had been dictated by a man in a grey suit and written down by the girl now sitting at the account-room desk with the claim schedules in front of her.

Old Mr Devan came up from his office above the shop that sold paper offerings, in a black coat despite the heat. The board would hear claims for what had been taken by requisition and for what had been taken under colour of requisition. It would want dates, weights, marks, the names of officers where the claimant knew them, and it would test all of it against whatever other paper the town could produce. It would not be generous. It would be, in Mr Devan's word, judicial.

"Then we produce the books," said the Towkay.

"The books say damp," said Mr Devan.

The room was quiet. Through the window came the sound of the yard being swept, and further off a lorry that would not start.

"There is a second record," Mei Foong said.

She had known for three days that she would have to say it, and had spent the three days deciding on the sentence, and it came out flat and short and landed in the middle of the desk like a stone. She fetched the exercise book — the one that had ridden inland in a quilt seam and back again, its cover mottled with the tide-marks of four wet seasons — and set it on the blotter, open at the second year's columns, and stood away from it with her hands at her sides.

Every bale. The date it was condemned and the date it left; the weight the book had been told to say and the weight the scale had shown; the initials of the man who signed and the hour he signed at, because he had been a punctual thief. The quinine. The firm's two lorries, requisitioned in the third year, one with the name still on the door. The estate's output for eleven quarters, taken against printed receipts in a currency now locked in a drawer upstairs.

The Towkay read four pages standing up, then sat down and read the rest, and Mr Devan read over his shoulder; and Mei Foong stood where servants stand and watched her own handwriting being weighed by two men who between them had forty years of weighing.

"You kept this," the Towkay said at last. "In the same house as him."

"It was the true book, sir. The other one was for him."

"Where."

"In places," said Mei Foong.

He looked up at her, and something crossed his face that she did not try to read; and afterwards she thought that if a door had ever been going to open in that man before 1948, it stood ajar for about two seconds on that afternoon, and then the sweeping outside stopped and he shut it himself.

"It is in a servant's hand," he said to Mr Devan. "They will say we wrote it last week."

"They will say it," Mr Devan agreed. "So we will have her swear to it, and they will ask her questions, and we shall see what her book does under questioning." He turned a leaf with one finger, tenderly. "Towkay, I have read six hundred folders this month. This is the first document I have been handed that admits a loss on its own side. The board will not have seen one either."

So on a Tuesday in September Mei Foong put on the grey samfoo and went to the town hall behind the Towkay and Mr Devan, carrying the umbrella, and sat on a bench in a corridor for two hours and forty minutes among men who owned rubber and tin and shipping and who did not look at her once.

The board was three men at a long table: one in uniform, one writing, and a Chinese assessor from Singapore with a bad cough. They asked the Towkay eleven questions and he answered them in English, correctly, and each answer cost him, because every one required him to say what had been done to him by men he had bowed to daily in his own doorway. Then Mr Devan said that the firm's record for those years had been kept by a member of the household, present, who could speak to every entry.

She was sworn. The clerk asked her name and she gave it, and asked her position and she said, "I keep the accounts," and the clerk wrote something shorter; and she saw at the top of his page, upside down, the word *servant*, and left it there.

They questioned her for fifty minutes, and not gently; the assessor had been a broker before the war and knew which stone to turn. Why two books. Who ordered the false entries. Was she saying the firm had knowingly signed false declarations to the occupying power — yes. Under what compulsion. Where had the true book been kept, and how could the board be satisfied it had not been improved since. What were the marks on the bales of the second year.

She answered in the order asked, worst figure first, and where she did not know a thing she said so. Twice she corrected her own book aloud: an entry misdated by a day, and a total carried wrong in the month of the hunger, when her hands had shaken; and the assessor stopped writing and looked at her over his spectacles for the first time.

"You are telling this board that your own record is wrong in two places."

"In two places I have found, sir. If I say it is perfect you will believe none of it. It is wrong in two places and right in four hundred and nine."

The man in uniform put his hand over his mouth. The assessor coughed for a while, and made a note, and said, "Thank you, Miss Ho"; and that was the whole of her hour in the room where the money was.

The award came the following March, for rather less than half of what had been asked, in a currency that had lost ground while the file matured, and Mr Devan called it among the better results of that season. The Towkay put the letter in the drawer above the dead notes, and said nothing to anyone about how the claim had been made, or by whom, then or ever.

*Dear husband. Your father's firm was believed today, because I kept a true book in a false year. The clerk wrote 'servant' at the top of my evidence. Both things are correct and neither is the whole entry. Half the claim is allowed: half a lie is nothing, half the truth is money. — Your wife, sworn today, in public, under her own name.*

The firm restarted on gold that an old woman in a hurry had sewn into a mattress in December 1941.

There was not much of it. Most had crossed palms between Malacca and the camps in the year of the buying and had bought one true sentence and nothing else; the honester intermediaries had brought some of it back, and it had lain in the west room under three years of sleep. Now the Towkay unpicked the seams himself, with the door shut, and brought the taels down in a folded cloth and looked at them the way a man looks at the last of anything.

They went through the goldsmith off Java Lane in three lots so as not to move the price, and it became stock: rope, dammar, tinned goods for the estate lines, then rubber again, because the world wanted rubber the way it had in 1910. One lorry came back from the military disposals with three good tyres and a bed that had carried other cargo; the second never came back. The Bentley stood in the coach house on blocks under a sheet, its tyres perished to lace, and was mentioned by nobody. The Towkay was at his desk at half past five in the morning and at it again after dinner, and the street said it with approval: the old man had gone back to work.

He had gone back to work. He had not come back. Something had been taken out of him on a February morning in 1942 and was not returned by the return of the flags, and what filled the space was not grief exactly — grief moves — but a hardening around the places where things could still be counted. He counted the kerosene. He wanted the marketing money accounted for daily, to the cent, by Third Aunt, who had fed the household through the hunger on a genius no ledger could hold and who now stood at his desk each evening with her wet hands wrapped in her apron while he checked her arithmetic. When the whitewashers came he stood in the five-foot way and watched them the whole morning.

Mei Foong understood it, which did not make it lighter to live beside. A man who had believed everything had a price had found one thing that could not be bought, and one was enough to break the engine; so he held tight to what could still be priced and locked and issued by the hour, and the household adjusted around the smallness the way it adjusts around a bad step on a stair. Her own wage rose by a dollar in the second year.

---

What he could not lock up, he doted on.

Mei Lan turned four that November, and she was the one bright loud thing in a house of quiet adults, and her grandfather began spending money on her the way a man drinks after long abstinence. English boiled sweets in a tin with a castle on it. A doll from Singapore with eyes that closed. A gold chain, then a second, then a bangle that had to be taken off her at night because she would not sleep with it on.

He sent for her in the middle of the working morning. The clerks learned the sound of it — the office door, the child's feet on the tiles, the long silence after — and Mei Foong, carrying tea in, would find the Towkay of the firm with his ledger pushed aside and a four-year-old kneeling on the desk beside him, running the beads of his abacus with both hands, being told what the columns were for. He let her put her thumb in the wet ink of his chop and stamp the blotter, and he kept the blotter.

And then he would not let her out of the gate.

That came on within a season. The street was full of lorries now, he said; there were men in the town from the hills who had not gone back to work; there was a strike at the ice factory and meetings on the padang and a hundred idle boys with nothing to do but be idle in the road. So the child was not to be taken to the market, or to the temple in the seventh month, or down to the bridge to see the ships, and the gate was shut at six. He said it to Mei Foong and not to the child's mother, because it was Mei Foong who took her to those places.

She said, "Yes, sir," and stopped taking her.

Once — only once — she said the other thing, on an evening when he had sent the child in out of the courtyard rain and the child had cried and been given a sweet for crying. "She is very like her uncle at that age," said Mei Foong, in the safest words available to her, eyes on the tray she was clearing. "He used to sit on the step in the rain too. You let him."

The Towkay did not answer for so long that she began composing the shape of the rest of her life. Then he said: "I let him do everything he wanted, and the world took him off my doorstep in a lorry." He picked up his pen. "This one stays inside."

She carried the tray out; and on the back stair she thought, with a clarity that frightened her a little, that he did not love the child more than he had loved his son. He was holding her the way a man holds his hat in a wind.

---

Bee Nio and Dr Lim tried, that second year, for a child of their own. It was managed with the discretion of two people who knew exactly how much a house may be told, and Mei Foong knew because she carried the washing. It ended in the fourth month, at home, on a Sunday afternoon, quietly. Dr Lim came out to the landing afterwards and stood polishing his spectacles on his shirt for longer than the spectacles required, and said, "She will want the small basin, not the big one," and went back in. Mei Foong brought the small basin. Somebody downstairs was practising scales on the neighbour's piano, badly, over and over, and outside it was a fine clear evening with the swifts up; and that was all of it, and neither woman ever spoke of it to the other, because there was no sentence in either of their languages that would not have cost more than it carried.

After that, Bee Nio poured herself into Mei Lan like water into a jar.

It was not cruel and it was not sudden and it was not, Mei Foong told herself many times that year, malice; it was the way a person holds on with the arm that still works. But it had a shape, and the shape was decisions. In the order made in one night by four people and a ledger, the child's small government had been shared without anyone writing it down: Bee Nio was Mother, Mei Foong was the hands. Which meant that Mei Foong plaited the hair and knew the fevers, and that the middle-sized questions were conferred over briefly, in the doorway language the two women had spoken since 1943.

Now the questions came back answered.

The convent school in a year and a half — decided, the fee paid ahead, and Mei Foong heard of it from Third Aunt, who had it from the cook at the doctor's house. The child's hair thereafter cut short at the ear, because it was cleaner and the fashion. Frocks from a Singapore dressmaker's pattern, and leather shoes with a strap and a button. And in the third month of that year Bee Nio began taking Mei Lan on Sunday mornings to the church her husband's family had sat in for two generations, in a hat, in the family pew — a reasonable thing for a Straits-born doctor's wife to do; so that the seventh month went by without the child in the courtyard holding up her joss sticks in both fists, and nobody remarked on the absence except one person, who was carrying the offerings out to the gate at the time and did not break step.

For the child's birthday Mei Foong had beaded her a pair of slippers, worked at night for two months under the one lamp, on the last of the good black satin from before the war: the peony and the phoenix at half scale, so the pattern would sit right on a foot that small. Her mother's pattern, her own hands, the only inheritance she was permitted to pay out in daylight. She gave them wrapped in a rice-paper twist, in the front hall, with the household watching, as an amah gives a present to the daughter of the house.

Mei Lan put them on at once and walked up and down the tiles like a small empress and had to be prised out of them for the cake.

And Bee Nio picked them up afterwards and turned them in her hands and said, warmly, with real pleasure and no edge anywhere in it, "Foong, they are too good for her. She will have them scuffed through in a month." And she folded them into tissue paper, corner over corner over corner, and put them away in the camphor box for when the child was older.

Mei Foong watched the tissue being folded, and knew the folds, and knew the hands. She had unmade those exact folds herself one dawn in February 1942, over another pair of slippers, and had remade them so carefully that only the woman who made them the first time could ever have known — and that woman had opened the box a year later, and looked at the tissue, and said nothing at all, and put the slippers into her hands and closed her fingers over them. That had been the vow. This was the same hands, folding the same folds over her daughter's shoes, saying nothing, and meaning nothing by it at all.

"Yes, Mrs Lim," said Mei Foong. "That is best."

*Dear husband. Your sister has decided the school, the hair, the shoes and God. She does not know she is deciding. She is holding on, and I am what her hand closes on. I gave her back today a thing she once gave me, and did not say so. I keep a separate account of what it costs to say nothing. It does not balance. — Your wife.*

---

The word came into the newspapers that year the way water comes under a door.

There were unions now, registered, with offices and printed cards, and on the estates the tappers who had eaten tapioca for three years while the trees were tapped for somebody else had begun to meet under the smoke-house roof and to send their conditions up in writing: wages, tasks, the rate for low-grade, the price of the estate shop's rice. The estate inland at Ayer Keroh sent its paper down at the end of the dry season, and Mei Foong read it out to the Towkay in the account room, because it came in three languages and only one of them was his.

He heard it through with his hands flat on the desk.

"What do they think this is," he said, "a shop?" And then, without waiting: "Write to the manager. The rate stands."

There were strikes that year at the ice factory, and at a sawmill up the coast, and on estates in three states, and the newspapers carried them beside the shipping and the rice control and the arguments about the constitution, and used a word for the powers a government might take if matters went further. Nobody said it yet with a capital letter. It sat in the columns, unemphatic, technical, the way a word sits for a year or two before it becomes the name of a decade: emergency. Mei Foong read it three times in three months and thought nothing whatever of it, and that was reasonable, because in 1947 there was nothing there to think.

What she thought about instead was the Bibik, who had begun to sit down between tasks.

---

It came on the way the white ants had come on: soundly, from inside, unseen until a knife went in. The Bibik had aged through the occupation with the ferocity of a woman who intends to outlast it; and, having outlasted it, and having buried her hope of her son in good order, she began putting things down. First the marketing, which went to Third Aunt with a speech about standards. Then the stairs, twice a day instead of six times. Then, in the wet season, three weeks in her room with a cough that Dr Lim listened to for a long time, and afterwards described to Mei Foong on the landing in the flat voice he used for the truth: not this year, not next year, but this is the road now.

She kept the kitchen. That was hers to the end and everyone knew it. She sat on the tall stool with a cushion under her and made the kueh she had always made, in season, correctly, and directed the pounding of the spices from six feet away by ear alone, and had her hands in the dough at New Year; and it was in the kitchen that it happened.

They were making the ninth-month cakes — or rather the Bibik was making them, and Mei Lan was being allowed to press the moulds, and Mei Foong was doing the work of four people at the far end of the table, oiling, filling, turning out. The old woman's hands had gone thin and spotted and beautifully certain. She was talking, in her running kitchen commentary, about the gula melaka and the man who sold it and his father who had sold it better, and she wanted the small brass mould, the fish, the one with the notched tail.

"Kim," she said, without looking up. "The fish one. Behind you."

The kitchen court went on with its noises. A girl scoured. The tap ran. Third Aunt, at the stove, did not turn round and did not stop stirring, and the stirring became, in one second, the most careful stirring done in Malacca that year. Mei Lan pressed her mould and looked up at nobody, being four, and hearing only that somebody had been asked for something.

Mei Foong stood with the oil in her hand. Behind her, on its nail, the brass fish with the notched tail hung where it had hung for as long as she had lived in that house. She had heard the name from one mouth before, in the amahs' room, out of a fever, and had answered to it then to steady a dying woman's hand, and had thought that would be the only time in her life. Her mother's name. The name taken off the beams of this house before she was born, cut out of the family book, unspoken at this table for twenty-six years by decree of the woman now sitting three feet away with a wooden spoon.

She took the mould off the nail and put it into the Bibik's hand.

"Here," she said. "The fish."

"Mm," said the Bibik, and set it in the dough, and pressed, and turned it out; and that was all, and the ninth-month cakes were finished by noon.

Nobody corrected her. Not that day, nor the next month when it happened again over the ironing, nor in the year that followed, when it came oftener and by turns — *girl* one hour, *Foong* the next, and then, with no warning, *Kim*, in the voice a woman uses for someone she is used to. The house did what houses do, which was to say nothing and pass the mould; because to correct her would have been to say the name aloud in the front rooms, and nobody under that roof was going to be the one who did that.

So the old woman went on calling her by her dead sister's name, and the household went on hearing it and not hearing it, and Mei Foong answered. The first time out of reflex. The second out of pity. After that for a reason she would not have written in any ledger, and did write, once, in the shortest letter she ever put into the tin.

*Dear husband. Your mother has begun calling me by my mother's name. Nobody corrects her. I answer to it. It is the only true name I have been called in this house in thirteen years, and it is a mistake, and I take it. — Your wife, answering to Kim.*
