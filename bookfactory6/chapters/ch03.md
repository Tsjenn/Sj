# The Order of the House

The house woke from the back, like a fire catching. At half past four the cook, Third Aunt, came down in the dark and knocked the first charcoal into the first clay stove, and by five the kitchen court was breathing: three stoves lit, rice washed, the well pulley complaining, smoke drifting up the air shaft past the sleeping rooms to tell the household that the day had been started for them.

Mei Foong's post in those first months was the fans. She knelt at the stoves with a palm-leaf fan in each hand and kept the coals at the colour Third Aunt wanted — not red, not grey, but a breathing orange that Third Aunt could apparently hear falter from across the court. It was work a child could do and be seen doing, which Ah Yoke said was the point. *You are new twice over,* she said, in the Cantonese that was their private country. *New to the house and new to the town. Be useful where they can watch you being useful.*

So Mei Foong fanned, and watched the machine.

Because that was what the house was, she came to understand — not a family with servants, the way she had imagined rich houses in the village, but a machine with a family riding on top of it, and the machine ran from the kitchen court up. The court was a rectangle of worn granite open to the sky, stoves along one wall, the well in the corner, the mortar like a stone drum by the stairs, and lines of order invisible to guests but as real as the drains. The cook ruled the fire and everything that touched it. The wash-amah, Ah Moy, broad and silent, ruled the water: the well, the tubs, the Monday boiling of whites in kerosene tins, the tapioca starch for the Towkay's collars, the blue rinse, the smoothing irons with their little charcoal hearts, the court strung with wet flags where every garment hung in the order of its owner's rank. Old Auntie Poh, the seamstress, some manner of poor cousin to the Bibik and therefore half family, half staff, and wholly touchy about which, ruled cloth, and ate at a small table alone, which she called dignity and Third Aunt called airs. Below the women came Mutu the syce, who kept the motorcar the Towkay could not drive, and the garden boy; and below everyone, for now, came Mei Foong, who scraped pans, grated coconut until her knuckles learned the grater's teeth, plucked fowls, pounded, fetched, fanned.

Ah Yoke fitted into the order the way a keel fits a boat — underneath, load-bearing, not to be argued with. She was the Bibik's own amah, which set her above the kitchen; a majie besides, sworn to her black-and-white, beholden to nobody's husband, and the other women granted her the wary respect owed to a woman who had chosen her life on purpose. She never raised her voice, and never sat down before nine at night. When she said a floor was done, the Bibik herself would not stoop to check it.

The rules came to Mei Foong in layers, the way the smells did. Bathe last, from the cool end of the water. Eat when the family had eaten, and eat everything. Walk behind. The front staircase, never; the back stairs belonged to the amahs, and the seventh door, the little one to the Jonker side lane, was the servants' door, and something in Mei Foong preferred it — a door her own size, in a house of doors built to make people feel small.

And she learned the house's day, wound like the Towkay's pocket watch around seven in the morning.

At six the bird went out. This was Boon Siew's task by custom and his pleasure by nature: the merbok cage carried from the back veranda and hung on its long hook in the first air well, uncovered, so that when the Towkay came down the dove was already practising its small bubbling coos into the wet morning air. At half past six the kopi was brewed, thick as river water, through a flannel sock Third Aunt guarded like a relic. At a quarter to seven the eggs went in — two eggs, three minutes and a half, timed by Third Aunt's recitation under her breath, which Mei Foong eventually understood was a prayer being used as a measuring instrument. Toast over the coals; kaya out of its jar. At seven exactly, Tan Hock Lye came down the front stairs with his newspaper, sat at the marble-topped table in the first air well, cracked his eggs into a saucer, added dark soy and white pepper in quantities that had not varied since before Boon Siew was born, and read the rubber and tin prices while the house held its breath around him.

Nobody spoke to the Towkay before his second cup. Mei Foong learned this the way she learned everything that first year, by watching someone else fail: a visiting cousin asked him about lorries at four minutes past seven, and the silence that came back frosted the whole air well. By the second cup he was a man again, and would shout amiably for Boon Siew to explain what his school fees were buying, and the machine, having delivered him his morning, moved on to its other work.

What the machine made, mostly, was order. Clean floors, filled lamps, starched shirts, polished brass, meals arriving like tides. But twice a month, and daily before festivals, the court turned to its true art, and the Bibik came down to the kitchen, and they made kueh.

---

Mei Foong had believed, in Guangdong, that she knew what a cake was. The kitchen court corrected her.

Kueh was not cooking, as the Bibik practised it; it was a form of rule. Neo Guat Eng came down on kueh mornings with her sleeves pinned and her rings left upstairs, and the court reorganized itself around her the way iron filings reorganize around a magnet. She measured nothing. She corrected everything. She passed down the line of work like an inspector of troops — the coconut being grated, the first squeeze of santan kept strictly apart from the second, the pandan leaves pounded and wrung through muslin until the juice ran a green so deep it looked like something the jungle had bled — and she would stop, and dip one finger, and the court would wait.

"More," she would say. Or, "Enough." Or, worst, nothing at all, which meant the thing was to be begun again, the shame left as an exercise.

Mei Foong, at the bottom of the hierarchy, got the bottom of the work, and found — this was the first strange gift Malacca gave her — that she loved it. The gula melaka came in dark cylinders wrapped in dried palm leaf, and shaving it thin filled the court with a smell like smoke and honey courting each other. The rice was soaked and pounded and sieved silk-fine, the flour rising in a pale haze that settled on her eyelashes. Everything was exact and nothing was written down. The Bibik carried the recipes where nobody could inherit them by accident, and dealt them out in decrees: this much by the weight of the hand, this much until it smells ready, fold it the way I fold it or marry into some other house and ruin their kueh instead.

At the New Year the hierarchy of aunts arrived, and the court taught Mei Foong more about the family in three days than the front hall had in six months. Kueh season ranked every woman who entered it. The Bibik alone judged the pandan and the batter for the kueh lapis, whose nine layers were steamed one on another with a patience that was itself the point — *anyone can be rich,* the Bibik said, laying the ninth layer, *few can be careful.* Married aunts of standing got the lapis and the pineapple tarts. Lesser cousins rolled and crimped. Unmarried girls pounded and grated and strained, and Bee Nio pinched the tart edges beautifully for eleven minutes at a time before drifting off to report gossip. At the very bottom, Mei Foong scraped pans, kept the stoves at their breathing orange, and listened.

The listening was its own feast, because the court, at kueh season, spoke every language Malacca had.

She had arrived from Guangdong with one tongue and a hunger, and she discovered in the Tan house that words could be collected like dropped coins if you kept your eyes down and your ears open. There were two Malaccas in that house, and they did not speak the same language even when they used the same words. Front-hall Malacca was the Towkay's telephone English, loud and square as furniture; the trade Hokkien of towkays calling to one another about restriction and prices; the Bibik's Baba Malay turned formal and ornamental for guests, lace on iron. Kitchen-court Malacca was the Cantonese of Ah Yoke and Third Aunt, the market Malay Ah Moy bargained in, Mutu's Tamil out by the motorcar, and the cries that came over the back wall in their appointed order: the tok-tok of the noodle hawker's bamboo clappers at noon, the kacang putih man singing his roasted grams, the ice-ball seller's bell when school let out, the bread boy's one word — *roti* — flung like a stone at dusk.

Mei Foong sorted them all the way she would sort beads later: by weight, by colour, by where they belonged. Baba Malay came fastest, because the house ran on it. By the New Year she could follow the Bibik's decrees at decree speed; by the middle of 1935 she could answer in it, with the little turns the household used — and the first time she did, the Bibik's head came around from the stove. She looked at Mei Foong for a moment — one beat too long, the same beat too long as at the gate the year before — and said only, "The girl has an ear," and went back to her sugar.

*An ear.* Mei Foong put the two words away carefully, the way she put away everything the Bibik let fall, and did not know she would keep them all her life.

It was Auntie Poh's eyes that gave her the beads.

The seamstress had ruled cloth in that house for thirty years, and her hemming was still beyond reproach, but fine work had begun to swim on her, held further and further away as if distance would discipline it. Among her duties was the mending of the household's kasut manek — the beaded slippers that the Bibik and the aunts wore on any day worth dressing for, their fronts worked in glass seed beads finer than padi grains. The Bibik's second-best pair had burst a thread; a spray of beads had left a peony's edge and gone rattling into the box like escaping stars.

Mei Foong was sent up to thread needles for her, and for an hour she knelt and threaded and watched Auntie Poh's repair go wrong.

She could not have said, then, how she knew. It was the same knowing that would itch at her, in later years, over a queue of figures with one wrong sum in it. The old woman was matching colour faithfully, bead against bead, but she was pulling each stitch home too hard, so the mended patch drew tight while the old work lay easy, and the petal's edge, which in the original stepped down bead by bead like a stair seen from an angle, had gone blunt. From two feet away it would pass. The Bibik did not look at things from two feet away.

"You are breathing at me," Auntie Poh said.

"The old beads sit loose," Mei Foong said, in her new Baba Malay, before fear could catch the sentence. "Each one has a little room. The new ones have none."

Auntie Poh chose the ending that cost her least: she dropped the slipper in the girl's lap, said, "Show me, then, since Guangdong makes empresses," and folded her arms to watch the presumption fail.

Mei Foong picked out the tight patch bead by bead. Then she rethreaded, and let each stitch settle instead of hauling it, and counted the stair-step of the petal's edge off the undamaged petal opposite — three, two, two, one — and worked it. The court noises came up through the window; the afternoon moved; she noticed neither. Something in her had gone quiet in a way it had not gone quiet since Guangdong, and when she finally sat back the light had changed and her neck ached and the peony was whole. Not almost whole. Whole, so that even she could not find the border where the old hand ended and hers began; and for one moment, alone with it, she felt a thing she would have learned to call pride, if pride had been permitted her.

Auntie Poh held the slipper close, then at arm's length, then close.

"Hm," she said, and that was all any human being got out of her on the subject for the rest of her life.

But the slippers went back upstairs, and three days later the Bibik came down to the court with them and stood everyone still.

"Who mended this?"

There are questions that fall like a stick falls, and the court went flat under it. Auntie Poh's chin came up, half claim, half denial, her whole dignity in the balance. Mei Foong put down her fan and stood.

"I did, Bibik. Auntie Poh's needles. She let me try."

The Bibik looked at her — the third time Mei Foong had received that particular look, longer than a look needs to be — and this time it went down to her hands and stayed there, and something crossed the older woman's face and was gone before it could be read, like a fish turning under dark water.

"Then from now the mending comes to you," the Bibik said, "and Auntie Poh's eyes are wanted for the hems, where they are worth more." A decree: everyone's face saved, nothing explained, no appeal. She set the slippers down and left, and Third Aunt said something about empresses that made even Ah Moy laugh.

That night Ah Yoke came up to their room and found Mei Foong under the one lamp with the mending basket the Bibik had sent that same evening, without a word: needles, hanks of silk, folded papers of glass beads sorted by colour — rust and gold and jade and a blue like the bottom of the evening. The girl had the beads out on a cloth and was doing nothing with them at all. Only looking, moving them with one finger, the way you would touch coins from a country you intended someday to visit.

Ah Yoke stood in the doorway a long moment with a face like a held breath.

"Sleep," was all she said.

---

Bee Nio claimed her in the spring, in front of witnesses.

The occasion was aunts — a delegation from Trengkera come to sit the afternoon and eat the Bibik's kueh in the second air well. Mei Foong was carrying in the tray when one of them, a woman with a jaw built for appraisal, looked her over and asked the Bibik where the house had found the girl, in the tone used for furniture.

"That's my girl," said Bee Nio, from the doorway, before her mother could answer.

She came in and stood by Mei Foong — twelve that year, and already able to occupy a room the way incense does — and told the aunts, with sweeping inaccuracy, that Foong was hers, that Mak had given her to her, that she could mend anything, and that she was not for lending. The aunts laughed and were charmed, which was the performance's purpose. Mei Foong stood holding the tray while she was itemized, feeling her face heat, understanding exactly what she was in that room: a possession being praised. And when, asked a direct question by the appraising aunt, she began to answer it, Bee Nio's hand found her wrist below the table edge and pinched, once, precisely: *not out of turn.*

That was the daytime Bee Nio.

The nighttime Bee Nio came to the amahs' side of the house after the lamps were lowered, squeezed herself onto the end of Mei Foong's board bed with cold feet and half a sugee cake stolen from the covered stand, gave the stolen half entire to Mei Foong, and talked. She talked about the convent school she would start at, and whether the nuns took your soul or only your Saturdays; about the aunt with the jaw, whom she hated and imitated until Mei Foong had to press her face into the pillow. And then, one night, in the smallest voice the house ever got out of her, she asked whether Guangdong had ghosts, and whether Mei Foong's mother had become one, and whether it hurt to think of her.

"Yes," Mei Foong said, to all three.

Bee Nio held her hand under the thin blanket. "When I am married and rich," she said, "you'll come with me, and we'll have a house with no aunts in it," which in Bee Nio's language was a poem about love, and Mei Foong, who was learning all the languages of that house, translated it correctly and squeezed the hand that had pinched her wrist that afternoon.

She lay awake after Bee Nio crept back. Nobody's sister here, and everybody's girl — the Bibik's mender, Ah Yoke's charge, Bee Nio's *mine*. She turned the word over the way she turned new words of Baba Malay, looking for its grammar. It did not mean what sister meant. It did not not-mean it either. She fell asleep before she found the border, and never in her life quite found it.

The bicycle was Boon Siew's doing, and it was the Jubilee that made it possible.

That May the whole town went briefly mad in honour of a king across the sea: paper arches over the streets, flags on the trishaws, schoolboys drilled for marching, processions, every veranda full and every back lane empty. The provision shop's delivery bicycle stood inside their own side door those weeks — the Towkay had bought its shopboy's afternoons while Mutu ferried the family about — and Boon Siew, fifteen, looked at the machine leaning unwatched by the seventh door, then at his sister and Mei Foong shelling nuts, and made one of his findings, which arrived the way his father's decrees did but pointed the opposite way.

"You should both know how to ride," he said. "There's no one in the lane. Come on."

"Girls don't," said Bee Nio, already standing up.

The rules the lesson broke were not few. The bicycle was the shop's; the lane was public; a daughter of the house did not straddle a delivery bicycle like a shopboy, and an amah's girl touched it only to hold it steady for her betters. Boon Siew waved all of it away with his maddening, unanswerable serenity — *Bee Nio will learn, so Foong learns too; fair is fair* — and stationed the garden boy at the lane's mouth as sentry, paid in Jubilee kacang putih.

Bee Nio went first, by right of rank. She shrieked, ran over her own brother's foot, achieved thirty triumphant feet, and dismounted into the wall with her arms up like an acrobat, demanding applause, which she got.

Then it was Mei Foong's turn.

She had watched the machine while Bee Nio fought it, the way she watched everything — where the weight lived, what the wrists did, how the thing wanted to fall. So she was better than she should have been, immediately: past the first wobble and out into the middle of the lane, standing on the pedals, the wind of her own small making on her face, the paper arch swinging nearer, and for three or four seconds she was nothing at all in the order of any house — not a servant, not a charge, not anyone's girl, just speed. Then the front wheel found the drain gully.

The lane turned over. She sat down hard on warm stone with the bicycle on top of her, its bell giving one bright absurd ding, its front wheel spinning above her like something applauding, the sky beyond the wheel very blue, both palms stinging, and Bee Nio's face arriving over her, white with fright.

And Mei Foong laughed.

It came up out of her before she knew it was coming, rusty, hiccupping, entirely beyond her control — the spinning wheel, the ding, the outrage of the ground — and she heard it as if it were a stranger's, because it was: she had not heard herself laugh since Guangdong, since a courtyard and a voice she did not let herself remember at noon; and here it was, alive after all, sitting in a Malacca drain in a stolen afternoon. Bee Nio, reassured, began to laugh too; then Boon Siew, hauling the bicycle off her, laughing his quiet laugh through his scolding — *brake, then fall, not everything at once* — and at the mouth of the lane the garden boy, sentry duty forgotten, laughed loudest of all.

Boon Siew set her on her feet and looked at her skinned palms with the practical frown he turned on the merbok's clipped wing.

"Again?" he said.

"Again," said Mei Foong.

She fell twice more and then stopped falling, and rode the lane end to end four times; and that evening Ah Yoke, taking her stinging hands in a grip that was information in itself, said only that some kinds of stupidity, done quietly, were the only holiday servants got — as close to a blessing as the black-and-white ever issued. Bee Nio swore all present to secrecy with terrible oaths, and told it as her own adventure to three separate cousins by August. *My brother taught us. My girl rode best of anyone.* Both halves said with equal pride; and Mei Foong, hearing of it, gave up once more on finding the border of that *mine*, and noticed that she minded it less than she had in spring.

By December she had been in the house a year and a half, and the machine had made its room for her: the fans passed to smaller hands, and Mei Foong had the mending basket, and a place at the mortar.

The solstice came, when every household in the street made the little rice-flour balls of reunion, and the court filled with steam and aunts. Mei Foong was set to pounding the soaked rice, and she liked the mortar the way she liked all work that answered her: the stone drum between her feet, the pestle's weight doing the labour if you let it, the wet rice going down from grain to grit to a flour like held moonlight. She had her own way with it, as everyone has their own way with a pestle: at the top of each stroke her wrist turned, a small outward flick that shook the head clean, so the rhythm was not thud, thud, but thud-and-turn, thud-and-turn, tidy as a loom. She did not know she did it. She would not have known her own walk from behind, either.

The Bibik was crossing the court with a bowl of the first-batch flour, pausing as she always paused to inspect everything on the way. Mei Foong, eyes down, felt rather than saw her stop at the edge of the steam; felt the pause lengthen past inspection; and had just lifted her face to ask whether the flour was wanted finer when the bowl went out of the Bibik's hands.

It broke on the granite like a shot. Flour leapt up in a soft startled ghost and settled on the wet stones, on Mei Foong's feet, in the new silence. The whole court turned.

The Bibik was not looking at the bowl. She was looking at Mei Foong — at Mei Foong's hands on the pestle, with an expression the girl had no word for in any of her languages, as if the steam between them had shown the older woman something and then taken it away again.

It lasted a breath. Then it was gone, shut like a door in a wall you afterwards cannot find.

"Clumsy," the Bibik said, to nobody, of nobody, in the voice she used for closing subjects. "Old hands in the cold. Sweep it." And she turned and looked across the court to where Ah Yoke stood at the far stove with a ladle stopped in the air.

Mei Foong, kneeling for the broom, caught it between them: one look, sent and received. It was not long. It was the whole length of the court, and it carried something the way the hawkers' cries carried through walls — she could tell that much, she who had spent eighteen months learning how this house spoke: a sentence went from the Bibik to Ah Yoke and a sentence came back, in a language that used no sound at all, and both faces closed over it at the same instant, like water over two stones.

Nothing was said. The Bibik went on to the stoves and quarrelled mildly with Third Aunt about the syrup, as usual. Ah Yoke's ladle finished its journey. The aunts resumed. The court, which digested everything, digested this.

Mei Foong swept the flour and the blue-and-white pieces into her pan, picking the shards out of the mess the way you save beads from a burst thread, and told herself what a sensible girl in her position was obliged to tell herself: a bowl was a bowl; old hands drop things; not every look was a word. She almost believed it. But she had an ear — the Bibik herself had said so — and under the steam and the pounding that resumed around her, she understood that she had just heard the house speak its oldest language, the one nobody was going to teach her: the one made all of silence, in which something, tonight, had been said about her.

She carried the shards out, washed her hands, came back to the mortar, and took up the pestle. Thud-and-turn, thud-and-turn. It was the only way her hands knew.
