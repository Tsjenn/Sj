# The Letters

Teacher Yap corrected her homework with a red ballpoint that had, by his own account, outlived four thousand students, and he brought it down now on Su-Lin's notebook and drew a small exact circle around one pencilled word. "Wrong," he said. "Again."

"It's in the dictionary, Teacher."

"The dictionary gives you words. It does not give you the writer." He tapped the circle. "You have put *waiting*. The character is *keeping*. A watchman keeps. A shopkeeper keeps. A widow keeps. Your writer is not sitting at a window. She is on duty." He recapped the pen, which was how he ended arguments. "Read it again with the right word and you will see the whole sentence stand up."

Six weeks had passed since the weekend that was supposed to be a weekend, and Su-Lin's life had rearranged itself around a biscuit tin the way traffic rearranges itself around roadworks: officially temporary, visibly permanent. She drove down from KL on Thursday nights now and back up before dawn on Mondays, past the exit sign for Ayer Keroh, a name that meant nothing to her but a slip road; she had negotiated a remote arrangement with the firm that the firm called flexible and her mother called throwing away a career, and which consisted in practice of taking the partners' calls standing in the airwell, where the Nokia found its one reliable bar, and sending markups from the fax machine at the photo shop by the bridge. She had missed the Puchong presentation. She had heard, through Jason, the word *sabbatical* used about her in a meeting, in the tone of a diagnosis. She minded this less than she could afford to, and she knew what that meant, and she went back to the letters.

Teacher Yap had arrived the way everything arrived on this street. In her third week, defeated by a character with nineteen strokes, she had asked Uncle Raju — rhetorically, she thought — whether anyone alive could still read an old hand like Miss Ho's. On Tuesday morning at seven there was a knock: an old man in pressed trousers and hotel slippers, holding an umbrella against the sun, who looked at her with the flat institutional patience of a man who had taught form after form of the young and the hopeless.

"You are the one who cannot read," he said. "Raju told me. Show me the letters."

He had taught at the Chinese school for forty years, and he refused payment with real indignation, and accepted instead a pot of tea each visit and the standing right to be appalled by her stroke order. The one rule was hers, and when she explained it he had looked at her for a moment and then nodded once, as at a correct proof: *she* did the translation, alone, at night, character by character, with the dictionary and the stroke-count index and her Standard Five Chinese groaning under the load. He corrected. He did not go ahead of her. She could not have said cleanly why it mattered — except that the letters had cost someone sixty years, and it seemed indecent to strip-mine them in an afternoon.

"Slow is correct," was all Teacher Yap said. "She wrote at the speed of a person who meant it. Read at the speed it cost."

When she told Uncle Raju what he had delivered to her door, he took his cup in both hands, entirely satisfied with himself.

"The street provides," he said.

---

The evening the letters turned over and showed their true face, Teacher Yap did an unusual thing: he picked up the envelopes.

He had never touched them before — his jurisdiction was her notebook; the originals lay in their rank down the counter glass, oldest at the left, and he treated the rank the way one treats another teacher's classroom. But that night, waiting for the tea to draw, he lifted the topmost envelope, tilted it to the lamp, and read the two brushed characters that were its entire address.

"Boon Siew," he said. "A man's name."

Su-Lin put the teapot down.

"They are all addressed the same," Teacher Yap said, walking two fingers gently down the rank. "The same two characters. Fifty envelopes, one address, no street, no town, no stamp." He looked up. "Who is Boon Siew?"

"Tan Boon Siew." Her own voice sounded odd to her, careful, like feet on a scaffold. "The son of the family my — of the family up the street. He was taken in the screening. In 1942. He didn't come back."

She fetched the register she had made the first weekend — of course she had made a register; dates were numerals and numerals were legible, and sorting was the one part of an archive an illiterate could do. One letter from 1941, alone before everything. Three from 1942. One from the start of 1943 — and then a silence, nothing more from that year, nothing from 1944, the worst years holding their breath — and then 1945, and from there one letter a year, sometimes two, marching in that small upright hand down the decades: 1946, 1948, 1950, through the sixties, the seventies, past the year Su-Lin was born, into the nineties.

Teacher Yap set the last envelope down and squared it to the counter edge, and for a while the shop was so quiet she could hear the beads settle in their trays.

"I taught composition for forty years," he said finally. "Diaries, essays, love letters confiscated at recess. A diary is not addressed. A letter is sent." He put one finger, very lightly, on the envelope's two characters. "These were written to a dead man for fifty years, and folded, and put in a tin. Girl. There is no name for this form."

There was, Su-Lin thought. She had been holding it for six weeks without seeing it. The tin was a postbox. Every letter had been delivered the moment the lid went on. The dead have a very reliable postal service: nothing is lost, nothing is returned, and nothing, ever, is answered.

She had finished translating the first 1942 letter two nights before, and it stood in her notebook in her blunt site pencil under the ghost of that disciplined hand, with her bracketed doubts still in it, because she had not had the nerve to resolve them:

*Dear husband. Sixty-one days. The neighbour brought your pen home, and the message with it, carried in his memory, because paper is searched and memory is not. The accounts are in my name now — that was the message, all of it. I write to say: received. I have opened a new ledger. I will enter every day into it until you come back to [audit / examine] me, and if you do not come back I will enter every day into it anyway. A ledger does not close because the owner is away. — Your wife, who may not sign herself so anywhere but here.*

In the margin her own handwriting, the architect's handwriting, had added: *61 days from the padang = May 1942. Check.* As if it were a survey. As if checking were a thing you could hide in.

*Your wife.* The street said there had been no wedding — the street that was eight feet wide, that would have eaten for a week, that remembered the menu of everything. And the tin said *received*. Su-Lin went upstairs that night and took out the folded photograph and looked for a long time at the crease, at the narrow wedge of hem and two beaded feet surviving at the bottom edge, and once again she did not unfold it. The fold was not hers. But she stood there long enough for the candle-smell of the room to change, and when she finally slept she dreamed in columns, credits on one side, debits on the other, and every entry in a hand not her own.

The second 1942 letter took her most of a week. She finished it alone on a Wednesday after Teacher Yap had gone, the night market packing up across the way, the last loudspeaker love song dying somewhere near the bridge.

*Dear husband. One hundred days. Your mother burned gold paper for you in the front hall with all the doors open, so that the street could see the family's grief. It was correct. I stood behind her chair. An amah does not burn paper for the son of the house. At night I burned mine in the kitchen court, in the stove, with the rice pot on top so that the smoke would have a reason. The smoke does not know rank. It went up the same. Where you are, note it in the accounts: two fires came from this house. One of them was mine.*

Su-Lin sat on the beader's stool with her hands flat on the counter until they stopped wanting to shake, and understood that she was no longer reading history. She was reading grief at sixty years' distance, and the distance did nothing, nothing at all; it came through the paper like heat through a wall.

---

"Uncle. How was he taken? Boon Siew. Does the street know?"

She asked it at the corner table on a slow grey morning, the kopitiam empty but for two trishaw men and the smell of kaya. Uncle Raju did not answer at once, which from him was itself an answer, like a barometer falling.

"The street knows," he said. "The street was standing in it." He turned his cup its half-turn. "There was a clerk. Malacca man, worked in offices before the war, and once upon a time he lost a court case to the Tans — their books were too clean, you understand; somebody in that house kept figures like a razor, and his side bled for it. In 'forty-two he found new employment. At the padang, with the screeners. Pointing."

"What was his name?"

"No." He said it without heat, the way he corrected her coffee order. "Until 1950 you could not say that man's name on this street without somebody spitting — old aunties, girl, women who fed beggars off their own plates, spitting in the public road like sailors. Then he moved to Singapore, and Malacca forgot him on purpose." He looked out at the street, eight feet wide, leaning. "Forgetting was the last thing we could do to him, so we did it thoroughly. I will not dig him up for you. Write your family's names in your notebook. Leave the weeds in the ground."

---

The archive room of the museum in the old Dutch building smelled of silverfish powder and air-conditioning fighting a losing action against the strait. The archivist, Puan Aini, was a small brisk woman who handled paper the way Hafiz handled roof timber, and when Su-Lin explained — a family member, taken in 1942, in the screenings — she did not ask any of the questions Su-Lin had braced for. She said, "Sit, please," and put on cotton gloves, and began bringing things.

She brought the registration cards of the occupation, brown as tea, each with its photograph the size of a stamp and its purple chop. She brought a resident's pass, palm-sized, creased soft as cloth from being carried against a body for three years — "the chop meant a man could walk home," Puan Aini said, and set it down, and let it lie there being what it was. She brought photographs of the padang from before the war, cricket and bunting, and none from the weeks Su-Lin asked about, and the absence sat on the table with the rest. And she brought a volume bound in dark cloth, printed after the war by the clan associations: a remembrance book, names in columns, compiled from what families came forward to give.

"How many?" Su-Lin asked. "In Malacca. In those weeks."

Puan Aini straightened the volume on its foam cradle before she answered.

"If I gave you a number, I would be choosing between other people's numbers, and they disagree, and none of them was counted at the time by anyone who meant the count to survive. The men who kept lists burned the lists. What we hold is names — where a family gave a name." She opened the book. "Most families gave a name and nothing else, because a name was all they ever had."

The columns went on for pages. Su-Lin read them slowly, the way she now read everything, and the reading was its own kind of rite; some entries carried a trade, a street, an age; a few, terribly, carried more — *returned to the family*, one said, and the words were worse than any she had translated. And then, in the middle of a column, in type that made it no different from its neighbours:

*Tan Boon Siew. Heeren Street. Taken, second month, 1942.*

Nothing after the comma. No place. No date of death. No word *died* at all, because the compilers had been exact men, and exactness was the only tribute left in their power: they wrote what was known, and what was known was *taken*.

"There's nothing else?" Su-Lin heard herself ask. "No record of — where. What happened to them, after the lorries."

"For some, there are accounts. Testimony, from after the war. For most —" Puan Aini closed her gloved hands together, a small liturgical gesture, "— the record is the absence, Miss Tan. That is not the archive failing. That is the archive telling you the truth. Many were never accounted for. Anyone who tells you otherwise is decorating."

She let Su-Lin sit with the book for an hour. Nobody decorated anything. On the way out, Su-Lin stopped in the doorway of the gallery where the school groups went, and watched a bored twelve-year-old lean his forehead against a case, and thought about a nineteen-year-old arguing rice yields in a kopitiam, and had to stand in the five-foot way for a while before she could see the street properly.

---

Her grandmother called that evening, unprompted, for the first time in forty years of Su-Lin knowing her.

She was under the airwell when the Nokia buzzed, because that was where the bar was, and the screen said AH MA, and the rain-smell was coming down the shaft the way it had the first day. She pressed the green key and her grandmother's voice arrived level as a shelf, no preamble, the television silent behind it.

"Su-Lin. You are still in Malacca."

"I'm still in Malacca, Ah Ma."

"Your mother tells me the developer has increased the offer. That the agent says the window is the window." A pause, exactly the width of a breath. "I am calling to tell you my view, since apparently it needs saying twice. Whatever is in that house, it is not ours. Foong was staff. Sell it."

Each word set down like a tile, spaced, faced, true. Su-Lin stood in the airwell and listened to the perfect evenness of it, and the evenness was the thing — she had spent twelve years around structures, and she knew what it meant when a member under load showed no deflection at all: it meant the load was going somewhere you couldn't see.

"Ah Ma," she said carefully, "you haven't asked me what's in the house."

Silence. Not an empty silence; a held one.

"Three calls," Su-Lin said. "You've never once asked what I found."

"I have no interest in a dead servant's cupboards." Level. Level. "Sell it, Su-Lin. Some houses are better as money."

The line ended the way her grandmother's calls always ended, cleanly, the receiver going down on the subject like a lid on a tin. Su-Lin stood looking at the phone's little green screen until the backlight died. Her grandmother had not asked, and people did not skip questions out of indifference — indifference asks idly, the way her mother asked about everything. People skipped questions they were afraid had answers. *I have never owned a beaded slipper*, said flatly, to a question nobody had asked, six weeks ago. Under the evenness, distinct as a watermark held to the light, Su-Lin heard it now for what it was, and it changed the shape of everything: her grandmother was afraid.

---

The trust's approval for the roof came through mid-month, and the house filled with the honest noise of work: scaffolding up the back, tarpaulins, two of Hafiz's men walking the tiles with the flat-footed roll of cats, the beetle-eaten purlin coming down in the airwell looking, in daylight, exactly as tired as it had a right to. Hafiz ran the site the way she would have — protection sheets first, everything photographed before touched — and he talked to the house more than to anyone on the crew, which she found restful. On the second morning he arrived carrying a tiffin of kuih from his mother, sent specifically, he said, on hearing that Sister Foong's shop was staying a shop.

"She sends a message also." He looked faintly pained, a man delivering his mother verbatim. "She says: her wedding slippers from this house still fit, and if the shop beads again, her granddaughters will be measured here and nowhere else. She has decided this. I'm only the postman."

It was the first time in weeks that Su-Lin laughed, and the sound surprised her in her own house.

It was the hall that brought them to the wall. The crew had sheeted the ancestral hall to protect the altar from roof dust, and Hafiz came down one afternoon, moving the way he did when the building had said something to him, and stood in front of the patch beside the altar — the tea-tray rectangle she had drawn on graph paper the first weekend with one question mark beside it. The roof work's vibration had opened a hairline at the patch's corner, and a flake of plaster the size of a fifty-sen coin had let go. He crouched, raked his torch across it, and was quiet for a long time.

"Come and look at your wall."

She crouched. In the small wound where the flake had fallen, brick showed — and even to her eye it was wrong. She had seen the house's original bricks in the roof space and where the stair wall had shed its skin: thin, hand-struck, low-fired things, orange as roof tiles, generous soft lime between them. This brick was denser, straighter, its edge machine-sharp, and the mortar around it was a hard grey line.

"That's not the house's brick," Hafiz said. "Not Dutch-time, not the renovations either side — I know those. Pressed brick, that face, that arris, and that mortar mix — " he touched it with one finger, the way Raju had not touched the photograph " — this is nineteen-forties. Could be a few years after. Not before." He sat back on his heels and looked at the patch, the whole tea-tray of it, minutely proud of the wall's plane. "Someone closed this during the war, or just after. And they closed it well. That's not a repair, Su-Lin. Repairs don't care if you see them. This wanted to disappear."

The torch made its warm circle on sixty-year-old plaster, and neither of them said anything for a while.

"We open it properly," Hafiz said at last. "I'll write it into the works — the trust will approve it as investigation of non-original fabric, that's routine, two weeks, maybe three, for the letter. Conservator present, photographs at every course, everything recorded. Whatever went in there, someone meant it to wait." He stood, and gave the patch a last look, almost courteous. "It can wait three more weeks. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Su-Lin.

That afternoon, on her way back from the fax shop, she stopped at the hardware store where the proprietor was still discussing fighting fish, and bought a cold chisel and a club hammer, and told herself they were for general purposes, and did not examine the telling.

---

*Tonight I write what I cannot say aloud in this house,* the first letter said, the one from 1941, the one that started everything; and it ended — she had finished it in her second week, and copied the ending into the front of her notebook like a motto, or a warning — *Paper is patient. It can wait for a braver year.*

At ten past midnight, Su-Lin stopped being patient.

She had gone to bed properly, that was the worst of it — brushed her teeth in the one-cup kitchen, lain down on the mattress in the room above the shop, listened to the street tick and drip, and had the argument with herself in complete, well-constructed sentences. The letter of approval would come. The conservator would come. Everything would be photographed at every course. She had shaken hands on it with a man whose entire working life was the belief that you don't open a wall until you can read the letter it's in, and she had made that belief her own. And against all of that stood one fact with the mass of a planet: a woman had written to a dead man for fifty years, two doors from where his family burned joss for him, in a house where a niche had been bricked shut during the war, and everything that woman had ever hidden had been addressed. The tin was addressed. The fold in the photograph was addressed — *For Mei Lan, when she asks.* The wall was the same hand. She knew it the way she knew load paths, below argument. The wall was addressed, and she could not lie in the dark one more night without knowing to whom.

She worked like a professional, which was the shape her shame took. Dust sheet doubled at the skirting, taped. The altar shrouded. The little digital camera from her site bag: the patch photographed square-on and raking, with the tape measure in frame. She pencilled a grid on the plaster, numbered it, scored the perimeter with a blade, and set the cold chisel at the corner where the flake had fallen, and hit it.

The plaster came away in plates, and behind it the grey mortar was hard but old, and let go along its joints under the chisel like a thing that had, in the end, only ever been asked to hold until someone came. She was careful. She was ashamed. She was unstoppable. All three were true at once and none of them modified the others, and she noted this about herself from a small distance, the way she noted the time on site photographs. Each brick she freed she numbered in carpenter's pencil and set on the dust sheet in its course order, so that the wall could be rebuilt exactly — as if putting it back were the point, as if any of this could be put back. The house made its noises around her. Twice she stopped, listening, sweat cold on her back, certain of footsteps; it was rain in the gutters both times, and she went on.

The eleventh brick came out into darkness that did not stop at brick.

Behind the patch the old niche stood whole: the little arched head its twin still wore on the altar's other side, the lamp shelf, the soot ghost of wicks that had burned there when the wall still had both its eyes. The cavity breathed out at her — camphor, ash, sixty years of held air — and on the shelf, square to the edge the way everything that woman ever touched was square to the edge, sat a lacquered box.

She lifted it out with both hands. Black lacquer gone matte as slate, the size of two spread hands, weightier than it looked, the way dense work always is. She sat back on her heels on the dust sheet, in the torch's circle, in the sheeted hall with the altar shrouded beside her like a witness with its eyes covered, and worked the fitted lid loose.

Inside, on a folded square of silk faded to the colour of an old ceiling, lay a slipper.

A child's slipper. A single beaded slipper for a foot shorter than her palm, the silk of it gone tender, the beadwork intact — and the pattern reached her before the size did, because she had been living inside that pattern for six weeks: the open peony, here no bigger than a five-sen coin; the phoenix tail making its same turn in six beads where the counter's slipper spent forty. The whole grammar of it, miniature. The thumbnail of the adult page. Someone had beaded the Malacca pattern for a foot that had barely learned to stand.

Beneath the silk lay paper, folded flat, and she knew before her hand moved that she would not be able to stop her hand.

It was brittle, foxed at the folds, ruled in columns, brushed in black with one purple chop gone brown at the edge — a registration paper, a record, the bones of the form familiar to her now from a museum table and cotton gloves. She could not read most of it. She did not need to. She had learned her numerals the first week, and the date column gave itself up under the torch: the eleventh month, 1942. And she had traced one name so many hundred times in six weeks of nights that her own pencil knew it — there, in the column where the museum's forms had carried the mother's name: *Ho Mei Foong.* And below it, in the child's column, two characters she had known how to write since she was six years old, because she had written them on red packets every New Year of her life.

*Mei Lan.*

Where the father's name should have stood, the column was empty. She checked it the way she checked everything, three times, with the torch close. Empty.

The torch stood on the floorboards, its beam lying long across the hall, lighting the little slipper from the side the way rain-light had lit the house the first day. The street outside had gone to its deepest hour; somewhere back towards the bridge a dog spoke once and thought better of it. Su-Lin put the paper down on the silk, very carefully, dead level, as if it could spill.

Then she sat down on the floor of the dark hall.
