# The Family Meeting

Her mother had put out sixteen chairs for twelve people, and by ten on the Sunday the four spare ones stood against the wall beneath the wedding photographs of three living generations, empty all morning, like a jury that was never called. The dining table had its extra leaf in, there were paper napkins folded into the water glasses, which meant the caterer, and a fruit platter under cling film on the sideboard, which meant the caterer had been told to come early, because the family would want to eat before it argued.

Su-Lin arrived at twenty past nine with a cardboard box on her hip, and the maid took it at the door and staggered slightly. The folders had come off the photocopier by the bridge on Friday night: twelve spiral-bound sets, tabbed with the coloured flags she used on tender submissions, every document captioned with what it was and who had examined it. Under her arm, rolled, were two A2 prints in ink on tracing paper: the family as told, and the family as built.

The house filled the way that house always filled. Uncle Beng came early, so the room would be arranged before it arrived, and put the developer's letter face up on the coffee table where the light caught the letterhead. Auntie Gek Choo brought a tin of Muar peanuts nobody would open. The Muar cousins came at ten in a hired car, the eldest being Uncle Chin Guan, eighty-one, who had to be got up the front step by two people and then would not sit down. Vanessa, nineteen, sat on the fourth stair with one earphone in and one out, which was the family's whole position on the young.

Nobody had told them what the meeting was for, so they had assumed, the assumption lying face up on the coffee table with a KL company's crest on it: a *final* offer, and improved; probate cleared; the girl had had her six romantic months in the damp; sentiment was all very well, but the roof of a hundred-and-forty-year-old shophouse was not.

"You are very organized," her mother said, at her shoulder, in the voice for a table badly set. "Whatever you have decided, you could have telephoned it."

"I didn't call the meeting, Ma."

Her mother's face did what it did when a fact arrived out of order. Then the gate rattled, a taxi door slammed, and the whole room stood up without being asked, because Mei Lan was coming up the path with her handbag over her forearm and, squared against her ribs, an English biscuit tin printed with roses and a hunting scene rubbed to ghosts.

---

She would not sit at the head of the table. She sat where she always sat, on the long side, facing the window, set the tin down on the cloth in front of her, and looked at the room over the top of her glasses until it had finished sitting down.

"Eat first," she said. "Or you will not eat."

They ate first: twenty minutes of yee mee and steamed fish, every pair of eyes going round the tin like a fly at a window, and Mei Lan asking Uncle Chin Guan about his knees. Then the plates went out, the folders came round, and Mei Lan put on her spectacles.

"Nobody is selling anything this morning. I asked Su-Lin to bring the papers; she will take you through them and you will let her finish. Then I will say what I want done, and Beng can say what it costs, because he will say it anyway."

"Ma," said Su-Lin's mother, low and fast, "we agreed — at your age, this kind of—"

"I am sixty-two," said Mei Lan, "and I have been dead of shock for a fortnight and have not noticed. Angela. Sit back."

Su-Lin stood up at the end of the table and did what she was good at. This family could not be talked into a thing and could not be wept into one; the only objects it had ever respected were a set of drawings and a bill of quantities. So she gave them a submission.

She began with the house, because they thought they were there about the house: the deed of 1948, the transfer clean, the name on it. Then the tin — fifty-one letters, dated, in one hand, addressed to two characters and never posted. Then the wedding photograph in its sleeve, both faces up, the crease running between them like a mended wall. Then the birth record, and Dr Sim's report on it in the conservator's own careful negatives: that she could not certify that a document told the truth; that this one was consistent in every material respect with late 1942; that nothing about it was new, and nothing about it was imitating anything. Then Teacher Yap's translations, with the originals beside them.

She did not editorialize; she had rehearsed not editorializing in the car. She read three letters aloud and let them do their own work.

*Dear husband. You have a daughter. She was born on the tenth of November, in rain, and weighed five catties on the scale they use here for rubber, and she has your brow — do not be vain about it. She is called Mei Lan.*

Nobody moved at the date. She had expected the date to be the moment; it was not. The room took it the way a beam takes a load it has been carrying all along and is only now being told about.

The moment came four minutes later, on the last of the three, from 1945.

*Our daughter called another woman Mother today, and I said nothing, and the saying-nothing is the loudest thing I have ever done.*

Auntie Gek Choo put her hand over her mouth. At the fourth stair, the earphone came out.

---

Then it began, and it went on for two and a half hours, and Su-Lin learned that a family is not one thing reacting. It is nine or ten things reacting at different speeds in the same room.

Uncle Beng got there first, because Uncle Beng always did, and he got there by the only road he had.

"Nineteen sixty-one," he said.

"Beng," said Mei Lan.

"No, no. Take out the folder. Tab six." He had brought his own copy of the will as well, a photocopy of a photocopy, furred at the folds and plainly out on his desk for some days. "Old man's last will. Everything in it is ordinary except the education money, and everybody in this room has had some of that. Read the words."

Su-Lin read them. She had read them eleven times already, at two in the morning, at the shop counter.

*And I direct my trustees to set aside the sum hereinafter specified for the education of the descendants of my son.*

"Descendants of my son," said Uncle Beng. "The son died in the war, no issue; everybody knew this. So in 'sixty-three the trustees resolved — tab seven — that it be read as the descendants of my son's *family*, otherwise the money sits there doing nothing for a class of person who does not exist. Forty years we have paid out on that. My schooling. Vanessa's fees this year. Su-Lin — your last two years and the year in Melbourne; don't look at me like that, your mother filled in the form."

"I know," said Su-Lin. "I checked in June."

"Then you have done the sum." He turned his glass a quarter turn on the cloth, which she had never noticed before that he did. "If that paper is what she says it is, the class of person exists, has existed since November of 'forty-two, and is sitting at this table asking Chin Guan about his knees. Which makes every ringgit that fund paid the rest of us a payment to the wrong people, and there is a lawyer in this town who would take that case on a Tuesday."

"Nobody is taking any case," said Su-Lin's mother.

"I have already had an opinion." He said it without triumph, which was the frightening part. "And it cuts both ways. The estate was distributed in 'sixty-two on the footing that the son had no issue. If he had a widow, if he had a child—"

"Mr Devan's letter is tab nine," Su-Lin said. "His conclusion is that a claim of this age is an expensive way to purchase a headache."

"Lawyers who are eighty do not want work. That is not the same as no work existing."

And then Mei Lan pushed her plate an inch away from her and said, in the level, brisk, entirely ordinary voice she used on hospital receptionists and contractors and forty years of committees:

"Beng. Get me a sheet of paper."

The room went quiet. Uncle Beng took a sheet from Su-Lin's box himself and set it in front of his sister; his wife handed down a pen; and Mei Lan wrote for four minutes with her head bent, in the small clear convent hand Su-Lin had seen on birthday cards her whole life, and read it back without looking up.

"I, Lim Mei Lan, born the tenth of November nineteen forty-two, having this year been informed of the circumstances of my birth, make no claim against the estate of Tan Hock Lye, against the settlement of nineteen sixty-one, against the trustees, or against any person who has received money from either. I want nothing repaid, reversed or accounted for. Anything of mine in it, I give now to whoever has already spent it." She put the pen down. "Chin Guan will witness it; he is the oldest thing in the room. Beng, take a copy to your lawyer and let him bill you for reading it."

"Ah Ma," said Su-Lin, "you should take advice before you sign anything."

"I have taken advice. I took it at Bukit Cina eleven days ago, from a woman who has been dead a year." Her chin came up, and for a moment, across a table of catered fish, Su-Lin saw a small straight-backed figure at the edge of a group photograph. "There is money in this family that was written for my father's children and went to everybody's children instead. It reached the right people by accident; and the man who wrote that clause put it in for people he had been told did not exist, which is the nearest that old man ever came to saying he was sorry." She capped the pen and handed it back to Auntie Gek Choo. "I am not here to audit the dead. I am sixty-two, and the one thing I still want is not money."

Uncle Chin Guan witnessed it, his name coming out in the enormous careful characters of a man taught to write in a village school in the thirties. Then he sat back and looked at the wedding photograph in its sleeve.

"Sister Foong. The one who counts. When we came down from Muar at New Year, my mother went to the account room for the year's money, and it was always the amah at the desk with the abacus and the old man watching her do it. My mother said, that one counts better than the son-in-law." He shook his head slowly. "In Muar we knew the shop also. My sister was married in her shoes."

---

Su-Lin's mother said nothing for a long time after that, which in her mother was a form of weather.

She had said plenty earlier: *impossible*; the thing about the optics; twice in the first half hour, that a family could not simply be told a thing like this on a Sunday. Standing at the end of the table, Su-Lin learned that an objection is a shape, and if you keep laying facts under it the shape either stands up or it does not. Her mother's objections did not fall down. They were outlasted — one fewer every ten minutes, nothing dramatic about it; the process by which a room that has been arguing about a roof comes, in two hours, to be arguing about a gutter.

It was the album that finished it. Su-Lin had not planned it, and afterwards she thought that was the only reason it worked.

It was Vanessa, who had come off the stairs an hour before and was sitting on the floor at the corner of the table, photographing the wedding portrait with her mobile phone. She looked up into a gap in the room.

"Wait. Auntie. If great-great-grandma is her" — pointing at the photograph — "then how come there's, like, no pictures of her at anything? We have pictures of *everyone*."

One great too many, Su-Lin thought; she had made the same mistake herself in May, promoting the dead a generation, out of some instinct that a war must stand further away from you than it does. Nobody corrected the girl. It was interesting how nobody corrected the girl.

"There is one," Su-Lin said. "Nineteen forty-four, on the front steps. Tab two."

"No, but at *ours*," said Vanessa. "Like, our stuff."

And Su-Lin's mother went out and came back with two albums, the maroon padded ones with the sticky pages gone brown at the corners, and opened them on the table; and the family put its heads together over its own photographs as it had never done over its own past.

She was in nine of them.

A wedding in Petaling Jaya in 1969: second table from the back, in grey, handbag in her lap. Two funerals. A house-warming, at the edge of the group, not quite in the line. And a red-egg party in 1971, a hotel function room with a paper banner, and at the end of the second row a small straight-backed old woman in grey, looking not at the camera but slightly down, at the bundle in the arms of the young woman two places along.

"That's you," said Vanessa to Su-Lin, with her finger on the bundle.

Su-Lin's mother sat down and put on her reading glasses and took them off again.

"She came to your full moon," she said. "She gave a red packet and would not sit at the family table, so I put her at the back with your father's office people, and I was — " Her hand moved on the plastic page and stopped. "I was *relieved*. That she would not make a fuss about where she sat. That was my whole thought about her, Su-Lin. That she was easy to seat."

"Ma."

"Every one of these, somebody invited her and somebody seated her at the back, and she came anyway, thirty years, and left before the food so nobody need work out what to do with her." She looked up, her eyes red and her voice entirely unchanged. "What am I supposed to do with that now? Tell me the correct thing and I will do it. She is dead. I cannot go and seat her differently."

Nobody had an answer. It was Mei Lan who said, after a while, "You can stop doing it to the living," and her daughter said, "Don't start, Ma," and blew her nose; and that was as near to a reconciliation as that room had produced in its history, and Su-Lin understood that it would have to do, and that it was quite a lot.

Vanessa, still on the floor, said: "Can I say something?"

"No," said her father.

"So great-great-grandma was, like, the best beader in the state, everyone in Malacca called her master, she ran a business for fifty years and owned the shop — and *we* went around telling people she was our servant?" She looked up at the ring of adults with the clean bewilderment of somebody who has come in at the end of a film. "Sorry, but that's the scandal, right? That's not even a secret. That's just us being embarrassing."

There was a silence of a particular thickness.

"Aiyo, this girl," said Auntie Gek Choo, and laughed — the first laugh in three hours, high and shocked and grateful — and then Uncle Chin Guan was laughing, and then most of the room, in the helpless way of people who have held one expression too long; and Su-Lin put her hand over her eyes and laughed at her own drawings until she had to sit down.

---

She made the proposal at half past one, the second sheet unrolled and weighted at the corners with water glasses.

Not sold. Not to the developer, not to anybody, not now, and — she said it plainly — not ever, if she could arrange matters so that it was not hers alone to sell.

The shophouse would go into a restoration agreement with the heritage trust, which had eight months of Hafiz's reports on it already and had allowed her the roof grant in April on condition the works followed the conservation programme and not her temper. The ground floor would go back to what it had been: the counter, the trays, the lasts on the wall. A working atelier — two beaders at the window table, three places for apprentices, real orders from real brides, because a craft kept in a glass case is a stuffed bird. The middle hall and the airwell open to visitors four days a week, thirty at a time and no more; and not a museum of a family. A museum of a street's hands, the lasts with the brides' names inked on them, 1949 to 1991.

"And what does it earn?" said Uncle Beng.

"In a good year, nothing. In a bad year it loses about eleven thousand." She had the sheet for that too. "The atelier pays the beaders and about a third of the running costs. The grant covers the fabric works. The rest is me: two days a week with the firm from October, which halves my income, which I have costed."

"And when you get bored?" said her mother. "Or married, or ill, or promoted?"

"That is the good question," said Su-Lin. "It is why the trust agreement matters more than my intentions. Intentions are not a structure."

Then she unrolled the last sheet, which was neither a plan nor a family tree, and put it flat on the table, and the room leaned in.

It was a lineage. Thirty-one names in her draughtsman's lettering, running down from 1949 like a wiring diagram: Sister Kam, who sorted; Kwai Lan; Poh Choo; four girls in 1953; the ones who married out, the ones who went to the factories in the seventies, the ones who taught their own nieces in Klebang and Muar and Batu Berendam. She had built it out of Sister Kam's tobacco tins, the wage ledgers, the numbers pencilled on lasts, and eleven afternoons of Uncle Raju's memory.

"Two are alive and still working," she said. "Kwai Lan is seventy-five, off the Klebang road; her hands are gone for fine work, but she can do the counting-out. Poh Choo is seventy-one and beads three hours a day in Muar because she says it is that or television. They came to the shop last month and stood in the doorway, and neither would come in until I asked twice. They take the window table, paid properly and paid weekly. Poh Choo has already told me my grandmother's stitching is loose."

"She said what?"

"Loose."

"She was always a liar," said Mei Lan, with enormous satisfaction, and wrote something in the little diary she kept in her handbag.

Uncle Beng looked at the thirty-one names for a while, doing whatever it was he did.

"Eleven thousand in a bad year," he said at last. "For how many years?"

"Until it works or I do."

"That is not a number." He took out his own pen and pulled a napkin over. "A building like that wants a sinking fund, not a good mood. Roof, wiring, termites — and insurance on a place with the public walking through it is not insurance on a house. I have done this for hotels." He wrote, and turned the napkin round. "That, per year, before you pay yourself anything. I will find you the insurance, and once a year I will look at your accounts and tell you where you are lying to yourself."

"Beng," said Su-Lin's mother, "she doesn't want—"

"She wants," said Uncle Beng. "You are all sitting here very moved. I am the one asking who pays for the roof in nineteen years." He capped the pen and looked, for the first time all morning, straight at his sister. "You will not audit the dead. Fine. Somebody has to audit the living."

---

She drove down on the Tuesday, and the rain came in over the sea about four, so they gave up on the airwell and took the drawings to the kopitiam.

Raju's son brought two coffees without being asked — kopi-o for Hafiz, the same for her now, a small conversion nobody had remarked on — and Uncle Raju watched from the good chair for exactly as long as it took to establish that he had watched, then went back to his crossword.

They spread the ground-floor plan across two marble tables, weighted with the sugar tin at one end and Hafiz's phone at the other. He read it the way he read everything, slowly, with one finger, out to the edges, then read it again.

"You've given the visitors the middle hall."

"They see the altar shelf from the doorway. They don't come past the line."

"Mm." His finger stopped on the airwell. "Light the cage. Not a spotlight — something low and warm on the wall, so the shadow of it goes up." He turned the sheet towards himself. "And this is eighteen months, not the six you have written here. Lime plaster does not care about grant conditions, and the timber man in Klebang works to his own calendar and no other."

"Eighteen months," said Su-Lin.

"With me on it. If you want me on it."

"I want you on it."

"Then put it in an appointment letter with the payment terms in it, and I will say yes to the letter." He sat back with both hands round the cup, and outside the rain came down the five-foot way in a sheet and a trishaw man swore at it comprehensively in three languages. "After that we can have the other conversation."

"Which conversation is that?"

"The unhurried one," said Hafiz, and drank his coffee, and asked her whether she had thought about where the fire exit was going to go.

She had. They argued about it for forty minutes, and about the ramp, and about whether a lime render could hold the ghost of the old signboard without faking it — a question for a conservator, he said; a question for a client, she said. The rain stopped at half past five the way it does there, all at once, and when she looked up the marble table had four cups on it instead of two, and neither of them could have said when the second round arrived.

---

Her mother came down on a Saturday at the end of the month.

She came by bus, which was unlike her, and stood in the doorway a while before coming in, which was not. Su-Lin watched her look at the counter, the trays sorted rust to rose, the lasts going up the wall in rows, small to smaller. She did not say the house was damp. She did not price anything out loud. She went the length of the shop and stopped at the middle hall, at the line where the visitors would stop, and looked in at the altar shelf: the two beaded slippers standing together on their square of faded silk, left and right, a pair for the first time in sixty-two years, and beside them the two tablets Mei Lan had ordered from the carver in Jalan Tokong.

Then she came back to the counter, took out of her handbag something wrapped in a plastic bag from a KL bank, put it on the glass and pushed it across with one finger.

It was a fountain pen. Dark green, mottled like river water under trees, its gold worn and unfaded, the cap tight with age.

"Ah Ma gave me this the year I sat my exams," her mother said. "She said, this is your grandfather's lucky pen, he won it for English, use it and don't lose it. I sat every paper of my life with it. I asked her once which grandfather, and she said, finish your revision." She kept her finger on it a moment longer. "It has been in a drawer under my passports for thirty-eight years, Su-Lin, and I never once wondered where a dead man's prize had been for twenty years before it got to me."

Su-Lin picked it up. It was heavier than it looked, the way dense work always is, worn smooth in one place along the barrel where a thumb had ridden it for decades. She turned it to the light: two English words, the name of a school in Malacca, a year.

Nineteen thirty-eight.

Her grandmother had handed it down the line with the story sanded off it and one word left standing by accident. *Grandfather.* He was.

"She wrote all of them with this," Su-Lin said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Fifty-one letters. Sixty years of a ledger nobody audited. It is in one of them — a neighbour brought it back to her in 'forty-two, after."

Her mother stood on the customer's side of the glass, in a shop with a woman's own name over the door, and looked at the pen in her daughter's hands.

"Then it does not belong under my passports," she said. "Put it where it goes."

Su-Lin took the ledger off the shelf, squared it at the end of the counter, and set the pen beside it on its folded square of blotting paper, exactly as an old woman had set it down every night of her working life; and her mother watched her do it, and said nothing at all about it, and after a moment reached out and moved it a quarter of an inch, until it was straight.
