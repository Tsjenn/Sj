# The Family Meeting

Her mother had put out sixteen chairs for twelve people, and by ten o'clock on the Sunday the four spare ones stood along the wall beneath the wedding photographs of three living generations, empty all morning, like a jury that was never called. The dining table had its extra leaf in. There were paper napkins folded into the water glasses, which meant the caterer, and there was a fruit platter under cling film on the sideboard, which meant the caterer had been told to come early because the family would want to eat before it argued.

Su-Lin arrived at twenty past nine with a cardboard box on her hip and had it taken from her at the door by the maid, who staggered slightly and said, "Wah, miss, books ah?"

"Folders," said Su-Lin. "Twelve. Put them on the sideboard."

They had come off the photocopier at the shop by the bridge on Friday night: twelve spiral-bound sets, tabbed with the coloured flags she used on tender submissions, every document printed on the good paper with a caption saying what it was, where it came from, and who had examined it. Under her arm, rolled, were two A2 prints, drawn out properly at last in ink on tracing paper: the family as told, and the family as built.

The house filled the way that house always filled. Uncle Beng came early, so that the room would be arranged before it arrived, and put the developer's letter on the coffee table face up, where the light fell on the letterhead. Auntie Gek Choo brought a tin of Muar peanuts nobody would open. The Muar cousins came at ten in a hired car, the eldest being Uncle Chin Guan, eighty-one, who had to be got up the front step by two people and then would not sit down. Vanessa, nineteen, in her first year of college, sat on the fourth stair with one earphone in and one out, which was the family's whole position on the young.

Nobody had told them what the meeting was for. They had assumed, because it was that kind of family and the assumption was lying face up on the coffee table with a KL company's crest on it: a *final* offer, and improved; the probate cleared; the girl had had her six romantic months in the damp; sentiment was all very well but the roof of a hundred-and-forty-year-old shophouse was not sentiment.

"You are very organized," her mother said, at her shoulder, in the voice for a table badly set. "Whatever you have decided, you could have telephoned it."

"I didn't call the meeting, Ma."

Her mother's face did the thing it did when a fact arrived out of order. Then the gate rattled, and the taxi door slammed, and the whole room stood up without being asked, because Mei Lan had come up the path with her handbag over her forearm and, under her other arm, squared against her ribs, an English biscuit tin printed with roses and a hunting scene rubbed to ghosts.

---

She would not sit at the head of the table. She sat where she always sat, on the long side, facing the window, and set the tin down in front of her on the cloth, and put her spectacles case beside it, and looked at the room over the top of her glasses until it had finished sitting down.

"Eat first," she said. "Or you will not eat."

They ate first. It was the most uncomfortable meal Su-Lin had attended in her life, twenty minutes of yee mee and steamed fish and a set of eyes going round and round the tin like a fly at a window, and Mei Lan asking Uncle Chin Guan about his knees.

Then the plates went out and the folders came round, and Mei Lan took her spectacles out of the case, and said:

"Nobody is selling anything this morning. I asked Su-Lin to bring the papers and she will take you through them, and you will let her finish. When she has finished I will tell you what I want done, and then Beng can say what it costs, because he will say it anyway."

"Ma," said Su-Lin's mother, low and fast, leaning in, "we agreed — at your age, this kind of—"

"I am sixty-two," said Mei Lan, "and I have been dead of shock for a fortnight now and have not noticed. Angela. Sit back."

Su-Lin stood up at the end of the table with her heart going like a site meeting and did what she was good at. The family could not be talked into this and could not be wept into it, and the only things they had ever respected were a set of drawings and a bill of quantities. So she gave them a submission.

She began with the house, because they thought they were there about the house: the deed of 1948, the transfer clean, the name on it. Then the tin — fifty-one letters, dated, in one hand, addressed to two characters and never posted. Then the wedding photograph in its sleeve, both faces up, the crease running between them like a mended wall. Then the birth record, and Dr Sim's report on it, read out in the conservator's own careful negatives: that she could not certify that a document told the truth; that this one was consistent in every material respect with late 1942; that nothing about it was new and nothing about it was imitating anything. Then Puan Aini's note comparing the registry chop against three of the archive's own. Then Teacher Yap's translations, each with the original beside it, initialled by an old man who had made her write her corrections out in full.

She did not editorialize. She had rehearsed not editorializing in the car. She read three letters aloud and let them do their own work.

*Dear husband. You have a daughter. She was born on the tenth of November, in rain, and weighed five catties on the scale they use here for rubber, and she has your brow — do not be vain about it. She is called Mei Lan.*

Nobody moved when she read the date. Su-Lin had expected the date to be the moment; it was not. The room took the date the way a beam takes a load it has been carrying all along and was only now being told about.

The moment came four minutes later, on the last of the three, the one from 1945.

*Our daughter called another woman Mother today, and I said nothing, and the saying-nothing is the loudest thing I have ever done.*

Auntie Gek Choo put her hand over her mouth. At the fourth stair, the earphone came out.

---

Then it began, and it went on for two and a half hours, and Su-Lin, standing at the end of her own drawings, learned that a family is not one thing that reacts. It is nine or ten things reacting at different speeds in the same room.

Uncle Beng got there first, because Uncle Beng always got there first, and he got there by the only road he had.

"Nineteen sixty-one," he said.

"Beng," said Mei Lan.

"No, no. Take out the folder. Tab six." He had brought his own copy of the will as well, a photocopy of a photocopy, furred at the folds and plainly out on his desk for some days. "Old man's last will. Everything in it is ordinary except the education money. Everybody in this room has had some of that and half of you don't know it. Read the words."

Su-Lin read them. She had read them eleven times already, at two in the morning, at the shop counter.

*And I direct my trustees to set aside the sum hereinafter specified for the education of the descendants of my son.*

"Descendants of my son," said Uncle Beng. "The son died in the war, no issue; everybody knew this. So in 'sixty-three the trustees resolved — tab seven — that it should be read as the descendants of my son's *family*, otherwise the money sits there doing nothing for a class of person who does not exist. Forty years we have paid out on that. My schooling. Vanessa's fees this year. Su-Lin — your last two years and the year in Melbourne, don't look at me like that, your mother filled in the form."

"I know," said Su-Lin. "I checked in June."

"Then you have already done the sum." He turned his glass a quarter turn on the cloth, which she had never noticed before that he did. "If that paper is what she says it is, the class of person exists, and has existed since November of 'forty-two, and is sitting at this table asking Chin Guan about his knees. Which makes every ringgit that fund paid the rest of us a payment to the wrong people, for forty years, and there is a lawyer in this town who would take that case on a Tuesday."

"Nobody is taking any case," said Su-Lin's mother.

"I have already had an opinion." He said it without triumph, which was the frightening part; Uncle Beng frightened her most when he was accurate. "And it cuts both ways. The estate was distributed in 'sixty-two on the footing that the son had no issue. If he had a widow, if he had a child—"

"Mr Devan's letter is tab nine," Su-Lin said. "He gives it a page and a half. His conclusion is that a claim of this age would be an expensive way to purchase a headache."

"Lawyers who are eighty do not want work. This is not the same as no work existing."

And then Mei Lan pushed her plate an inch away from her and said, in the level, brisk, entirely ordinary voice she used on hospital receptionists and contractors and forty years of committees:

"Beng. Get me a sheet of paper."

The room went quiet. Uncle Beng took a sheet from Su-Lin's box himself and set it in front of his sister; his wife handed down a pen; and Mei Lan wrote for four minutes with her head bent, in the small clear convent hand Su-Lin had seen on birthday cards her whole life, and read it back without looking up.

"I, Lim Mei Lan, born the tenth of November nineteen forty-two, having been informed this year of the circumstances of my birth, state that I make no claim against the estate of Tan Hock Lye, against the settlement of nineteen sixty-one, against the trustees, or against any person who has received money from either. I have no wish to have any payment repaid, reversed or accounted for. Anything of mine in it, I give away now, to whoever has already spent it." She put the pen down. "Chin Guan, you will witness it, you are the oldest thing in the room. Beng, take a copy to your lawyer and let him bill you for reading it."

"Ah Ma," said Su-Lin, "you should take advice before you sign anything."

"I have taken advice. I took it at Bukit Cina eleven days ago, from a woman who has been dead a year." Her chin came up, and for a moment, across a table of catered fish, Su-Lin saw a small straight-backed figure at the edge of a group photograph. "There is money in this family that was written for my father's children and went to everybody's children instead. I have thought about it every night since I found out, and I will tell you what I think. It reached the right people by accident; and the man who wrote that clause put it in for people he had been told did not exist, which is the nearest that old man ever came in his life to saying he was sorry." She capped the pen and handed it back to Auntie Gek Choo. "I am not here to audit the dead. I am sixty-two, and I have one thing left that I want, and it is not money."

Uncle Chin Guan witnessed it with his hand shaking and his name coming out in the enormous careful characters of a man who had learned to write in a village school in the thirties. Then he sat back and said, to nobody:

"Sister Foong. The one who counts." Everyone turned. He was looking at the wedding photograph in its sleeve. "When we came down from Muar for New Year, my mother went to the account room for the year's money, and it was always the amah at the desk with the abacus and the old man watching her do it. My mother said, that one counts better than the son-in-law. Every year she said it." He shook his head slowly. "In Muar we knew the shop also. My sister was married in her shoes."

---

Su-Lin's mother said nothing for a long time after that, which in her mother was a form of weather.

She had said plenty earlier. She had said *impossible*; she had said the thing about the optics; she had said twice, in the first half hour, that a family could not simply be told a thing like this on a Sunday. And Su-Lin learned, standing at the end of the table, that an objection is a shape, and if you keep laying facts under it the shape either stands up or it does not. Her mother's objections did not fall down. They were outlasted. Every ten minutes there was one fewer of them, and nothing about it was dramatic; it was the process by which a room that has been arguing about a roof comes, in two hours, to be arguing about a gutter.

It was the album that finished it, and Su-Lin had not planned it, and afterwards she thought that was probably the only reason it worked.

It was Vanessa, who had come off the stairs an hour before and was sitting on the floor at the corner of the table, photographing the wedding portrait with her mobile phone, which Su-Lin allowed. She looked up into a gap in the room.

"Wait. Auntie. If great-great-grandma is her" — pointing at the photograph — "then how come there's, like, no pictures of her at anything? We have pictures of *everyone*."

One great too many, Su-Lin thought; she had made the same mistake herself in May, promoting the dead a generation, out of some instinct that a war must stand further away from you than it does. Nobody corrected the girl. It was interesting how nobody corrected the girl.

"There is one," Su-Lin said. "Nineteen forty-four, on the front steps. I've printed it at tab two."

"No, but at *ours*," said Vanessa. "Like, our stuff."

And Su-Lin's mother went out and came back with two albums, the maroon padded ones with the sticky pages gone brown at the corners, and opened them on the table; and the family put its heads together over its own photographs as it had never once put its heads together over its own past.

She was in nine of them.

A wedding in Petaling Jaya in 1969: second table from the back, in grey, handbag in her lap. Two funerals. A house-warming, at the edge of the group, not quite in the line. And a red-egg party in 1971, a hotel function room with a paper banner, and at the end of the second row a small straight-backed old woman in grey, looking not at the camera but slightly down and to the left, at the bundle in the arms of the young woman two places along.

"That's you," said Vanessa to Su-Lin, with her finger on the bundle.

Su-Lin's mother sat down. She put on her reading glasses and took them off again.

"She came to your full moon," she said. "She gave a red packet and would not sit at the family table, so I put her at the back with your father's office people, and I was — " Her hand moved on the plastic page and stopped. "I was *relieved*. That she would not make a fuss about where she sat. That was my whole thought about her, Su-Lin. That she was easy to seat."

"Ma."

"Every one of these, somebody in this family invited her and somebody in this family seated her at the back, and she came anyway, thirty years, and left before the food so that nobody would have to work out what to do with her." She looked up, and her eyes were red and her voice had not changed at all. "What am I supposed to do with this now? Tell me the correct thing to do about it and I will do it. She is dead. I cannot go and seat her differently."

Nobody had an answer. It was Mei Lan who said, after a while, "You can stop doing it to the living," and her daughter said, "Don't start, Ma," and blew her nose; and that was as near to a reconciliation as that room had ever produced, and Su-Lin understood that it would have to do, and that it was in fact quite a lot.

Vanessa, still on the floor, said: "Can I say something?"

"No," said her father.

"So great-great-grandma was, like, the best beader in the state, and everyone in Malacca called her master, and she ran a business for fifty years and she owned the shop — and *we* went around telling people she was our servant?" She looked up at the ring of adults with the clean bewilderment of somebody who has come in at the end of a film. "Sorry, but that's the scandal, right? That's not even a secret. That's just us being embarrassing."

There was a silence of a particular thickness.

"Aiyo, this girl," said Auntie Gek Choo, and laughed — the first laugh in three hours, high and shocked and grateful — and then Uncle Chin Guan was laughing, and then most of the room, in the loose helpless way of people who have held one expression too long; and Su-Lin, who had not laughed in eleven days, put her hand over her eyes and laughed at her own drawings until she had to sit down.

---

She made the proposal at half past one, standing again, the second sheet unrolled and weighted at the corners with water glasses.

Not sold. Not to the developer, not to anybody, not now, and — she said it plainly, having learned in June that this family respected only sentences with edges — not ever, if she could arrange it so that it was not hers alone to sell.

The shophouse would go into a restoration agreement with the heritage trust, which had eight months of Hafiz's reports on it already and had allowed her the roof grant in April on condition the works followed the conservation programme and not her temper. The ground floor would go back to what it had been: the counter, the trays, the lasts on the wall. A working atelier — two beaders at the window table, three places for apprentices, real orders from real brides, because a craft kept in a glass case is a stuffed bird. The middle hall and the airwell open to visitors four days a week, small entry fee, thirty people at a time and no more; and not a museum of a family. A museum of a street's hands: the beadwork, the goldsmith's grille, the signwriter, Raju's coffee sock, the lasts with the brides' names inked on them, 1949 to 1991.

"And what does it earn?" said Uncle Beng.

"In a good year, nothing. In a bad year it loses about eleven thousand." She had the sheet for that too. "The atelier pays the beaders and about a third of the running costs. The grant covers the fabric works and comes with conditions I would want anyway. The rest is me. I go down to two days a week with the firm from October, which halves my income, which I have costed — and yes, that is a plan I would have laughed at in January."

"And when you get bored?" said her mother. "Or married, or ill, or promoted?"

"That's the good question," said Su-Lin. "It's why the trust agreement matters more than my intentions. Intentions are not a structure."

Then she unrolled the last sheet, which was neither a plan nor a family tree, and put it flat on the table, and the room leaned in.

It was a lineage. Thirty-one names in her draughtsman's lettering, running down from 1949 like a wiring diagram: Sister Kam, who sorted; Kwai Lan; Poh Choo; four girls in 1953; two in 1955; the ones who married out, the ones who went to the factories in the seventies, the ones who taught their own nieces in Klebang and Muar and Batu Berendam and one, in 1981, in Singapore. She had built it out of Sister Kam's tobacco tins, the wage ledgers, the numbers pencilled on lasts, and eleven afternoons of Uncle Raju's memory.

"Two are alive and still working," she said. "Kwai Lan is seventy-five, off the Klebang road; her hands are gone for fine work, but she can do the counting-out and she has opinions about everybody. Poh Choo is seventy-one and beads three hours a day in Muar because she says it is that or television. They came to the shop last month and stood in the doorway, and neither would come in until I asked twice. They take the window table, paid properly and paid weekly. Poh Choo has already told me my grandmother's stitching is loose."

"She said what?" said Mei Lan.

"Loose."

"She was always a liar," said Mei Lan, with enormous satisfaction, and wrote something in the little diary she kept in her handbag.

Uncle Beng sat looking at the thirty-one names for a while, doing whatever it was he did.

"Eleven thousand in a bad year," he said at last. "For how many years?"

"Until it works or I do."

"That is not a number." He took out his own pen and pulled a napkin over. "A building like that wants a sinking fund, not a good mood. Roof, wiring, termites, insurance — and insurance on a place with the public walking through it is not insurance on a house. I have done this for hotels." He wrote, and turned the napkin round. "That, per year, put aside before you pay yourself anything. I will find you the insurance. Not free — found, that is all — and I will look at your accounts once a year and tell you where you are lying to yourself."

"Beng," said Su-Lin's mother, "she doesn't want—"

"She wants," said Uncle Beng. "You are all sitting here very moved. I am the one asking who pays for the roof in nineteen years." He capped the pen and looked, for the first time all morning, directly at his sister. "You said you will not audit the dead. Fine. Somebody has to audit the living."

---

She drove down on the Tuesday and the rain came in over the sea about four, so they gave up on the airwell and took the drawings to the kopitiam.

Raju's son brought two coffees without being asked — kopi-o for Hafiz, the same for her now, a small conversion nobody had remarked on — and Uncle Raju watched from the good chair for exactly as long as it took to establish that he had watched, then went back to his crossword with the air of a man whose work in the world is done.

They spread the ground-floor plan across two marble tables, weighted with the sugar tin at one end and Hafiz's phone at the other. He read it the way he read everything, slowly, with one finger, out to the edges, and then read it again.

"You've given the visitors the middle hall."

"They can see the altar shelf from the doorway. They don't come past the line."

"Mm." His finger stopped on the airwell. "You'll want the cage lit. Not a spotlight — something low and warm, on the wall, so the shadow of it goes up." He turned the sheet a little towards himself. "And this is eighteen months, you know. Not the six you have written here. Lime plaster does not care about your grant conditions and the timber man in Klebang works to his own calendar and no other."

"Eighteen months," said Su-Lin.

"With me on it. If you want me on it."

"I want you on it."

"Then say it in an appointment letter and put the payment terms in, and I will say yes to the letter." He sat back and wrapped both hands around the cup, and outside the rain came down the five-foot way in a sheet and a trishaw man swore at it comprehensively in three languages. "After that we can have the other conversation."

"Which conversation is that?"

"The unhurried one," said Hafiz, and drank his coffee, and asked her whether she had thought about where the fire exit was going to go.

She had. They argued about it for forty minutes, and about the ramp, and about whether a lime render could be got to take the ghost of the old signboard without faking it, which he said was a question for a conservator and she said was a question for a client, and the rain stopped at half past five the way it does there, all at once; and when she looked up, the marble table had four cups on it instead of two, and neither of them could have said when the second round had arrived.

---

Her mother came down on a Saturday at the end of the month.

She came by bus, which was unlike her, and she stood in the shop doorway for a while before she came in, which was not. Su-Lin watched her look at the counter, the trays sorted rust to rose, the lasts going up the wall in rows, small to smaller. She did not say the house was damp. She did not price anything out loud. She went the whole length of the shop and stopped at the middle hall, at the line where the visitors would stop, and looked in at the altar shelf, at the two beaded slippers standing together on their square of faded silk, left and right, a pair for the first time in sixty-two years, and at the two tablets Mei Lan had ordered from the carver in Jalan Tokong, standing side by side where her mother had put them.

Then she came back to the counter and opened her handbag and took out something wrapped in a plastic bag from a KL bank, and put it down on the glass, and pushed it across with one finger.

It was a fountain pen. Dark green, mottled like river water under trees, its gold worn and unfaded, the cap tight with age.

"Ah Ma gave me this the year I sat my exams," her mother said. "She said, this is your grandfather's lucky pen, he won it for English, use it and don't lose it. I used it for every paper I ever sat. I asked her once which grandfather and she said, finish your revision." She kept her finger on it a moment longer. "I have had it thirty-eight years, Su-Lin. It has been in a drawer under my passports. I never once wondered where a dead man's prize went for twenty years before it got to me."

Su-Lin picked it up. It was heavier than it looked, the way dense work always is, and worn smooth in one place along the barrel where a thumb had ridden it for decades. She turned it to the light: two English words, then the name of a school in Malacca, then a year.

Nineteen thirty-eight.

"She wrote all of them with this," Su-Lin said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Fifty-one letters. Sixty years of a ledger nobody audited. It's in one of them — the neighbour brought it back to her in 'forty-two, after."

Her mother stood on the customer's side of the glass, in a shop with a woman's own name over the door, and looked at the pen in her daughter's hands.

"Then it does not belong under my passports," she said. "Put it where it goes."

Su-Lin took the ledger off the shelf and squared it at the end of the counter, and set the pen down beside it on its folded square of blotting paper, exactly as an old woman had set it down every night of her working life; and her mother watched her do it, and did not say anything about it at all, and after a moment reached out and moved it a quarter of an inch, until it was straight.
