# The Beaded Slippers

The right slipper came home from the cobbler on a Thursday in April, trial-mounted so the fit could be proved before the work went further, and Bee Nio had it out of its paper and onto her own foot before Mei Foong had fairly set the parcel down. One beaded slipper and one stockinged sole, on the cool tiles of the back hall, and the bride-to-be of the house of seven doors stood looking down at her own feet as though one of them had been returned to her from the future.

By then the slippers had their own calendar, and the household had learned to live inside it. An hour by daylight every day, the Bibik had decreed, and so every afternoon there was an hour in which Mei Foong belonged to no one but the tray: the frame with its stretched black cloth, the drawn pattern ghosting up through the weave, the beads in their wooden partitions — rust, gold, jade, and the blue that was almost night — each smaller than a grain of padi, each waiting to be lifted four at a time on a needle finer than the argument for using it. She had begun the right slipper's face in the dark of the year, and by April the peony had opened across the vamp, full-headed, its outer petals shading rust into gold the way a fire shades, and the phoenix had begun to climb out of the flowers with its tail still unwritten. Every new moon the Bibik came to the back hall after dinner, took the frame to the lamp without a word, and read the month's rows the way the Towkay read a broker's telegram. Twice she had touched a place where a bead sat proud and Mei Foong had taken out the row. Once — the February moon — she had looked a long time and said nothing at all, and put the frame down with a care that was its own remark.

The trial mounting was meant to be a fitting and nothing more. Bee Nio stood, wiggled her toes inside the year's work, looked down for a while at the peony on her own foot, and then — because she was sixteen, and because the gramophone songs from the amusement park records were in her the way hymns were in the convent girls — she danced.

It was a cinema step, two glides and a turn, the kind she practised behind the mosquito net with a hairbrush for a partner. On the smooth tiles, in one slipper, it half worked, which was more dangerous than not working at all. She glided, she turned, she sang three words of the chorus — and the beaded toe clipped the leg of the long table, a small click, no louder than an abacus bead, and a thread at the edge of the vamp gave, and five gold beads went their five separate ways across the floor with a sound like the first rain.

The law of the house was the Bibik's law: if a bead sits wrong, take out the row; and a sprung thread was worse than a wrong bead. Mei Foong was on her knees with her palm flat to the tiles before the last bead stopped rolling, and it was from there, from the floor, that she saw Bee Nio begin to cry.

Not prettily, which was how Bee Nio ordinarily cried, having studied it. This was the other kind, sudden and unbudgeted: standing on the tiles in one slipper with her hands hanging, weeping over five beads and an inch of thread as though something had been decided against her forever.

"I've spoilt them," she said. "I've spoilt them, I spoil everything, I can't even stand in them, how am I to walk—" and there it was, arrived out of the broken thread the way the true thing always arrived, through some small crooked door: how am I to walk. Not in the slippers. Out of the house of seven doors, into a house where she knew nobody, in a life already fitted to her before the fitting.

Mei Foong got up off the floor with five gold beads in her fist and did what there was to do, which she was good at. She sat Bee Nio down, eased the slipper off the way you would take an egg from under a difficult hen, and turned it in the light so the sprung edge could be seen for what it was — one row, one evening, nothing the pattern would remember. "It is a fitting," she said. "Things break at fittings so they will not break at weddings. Ask the seamstress. Ask anyone."

"Truly?"

"Truly," said Mei Foong, who had invented it that moment, and Bee Nio's face came back together and was even pleased, because a superstition that put your accidents to work was exactly the kind of religion she could love. Within the quarter hour she had decided the broken thread was lucky, had retold it as lucky, and had gone off to be lucky at somebody else, carrying her wet eyes lightly, the way she carried everything she owned.

The mending Mei Foong did that night, after the house had gone through its diminishment, by the one lamp, alone: an hour to take out, two to lay in again, gold following gold, each bead seated with the tiny defended click that meant it would not move again in this life. Nobody asked what an evening cost. There was no column for it; she had looked. That was what it was to be the hands of a house: your work went into other people's stories and disappeared there, and the perfection of the disappearing was the pride you were allowed.

She was sixteen going on seventeen, and she would never in her life stand in a bridal chamber weeping over nothing while another girl knelt on the floor sweeping up the cost of it. She had understood that with perfect clarity, kneeling there. It did not make her love Bee Nio less. That was the finding she took up to the pallet at midnight: you could see a thing all the way to the bottom and love on top of it anyway. It seemed to be how everything in that house was done.

By July the right slipper's face was finished and folded away in cloth, and the left was on the frame, and the phoenix's tail had come into its colours — jade barred with night-blue, each feather-eye a ring of gold around one rust bead, forty small watchful suns — and Boon Siew had taken to bringing the firm's papers to the long table in the evenings and working at the far end of it while she beaded.

There was nothing in it that any law of the house could name. The back hall was a public room; Ah Yoke or the second amah was always within earshot, sewing in the corner by arrangement so old nobody remembered arranging it; he was the son of the house at one end of a table and the amah's girl was at the other, and between them lay eight feet of teak and the entire architecture of Malacca. He drafted MERBOK's letters there in pencil, under the firm's correspondence, where a page could be slid beneath a page. That year the merbok had learned new songs: in May, that a colony which drilled volunteers on Sunday and sold its rice-land on Monday was rehearsing a play while dismantling the theatre; in August, answering a planter, that loyalty was a fine dinner but a poor larder. She posted them as she had always posted them, folded among the household mail between the Bibik's temple subscriptions and the order for lamp oil, from the counter on Kling Street, where the clerk had never once looked at her face. The fountain pen went on living in the account drawer, an unsettled account of its own; the ledger margins went on talking in their small code of birds and weather; and on the nights he brought his work to the long table they did not talk at all, mostly, and the silence was the margin kind, wide enough for two, and she would have been ashamed to admit how she came to arrange her week around it.

That night the house was down to its one lamp. He had finished his drafting and not left. He was watching her hands — she knew it the way you know rain is coming, by pressure, without looking up — and she went on lifting beads four at a time, laying the feather-eyes in their rings, because the work was the one place her hands had never once shaken.

"How do you keep the row straight," he said at last, quietly, "when you can only see one bead at a time?"

"You don't keep the row straight," she said. "You seat each bead straight. The row is only what that adds up to." She heard, too late, everything else the sentence was, and kept her eyes on the needle.

"My father should hear that. It is more use than anything the brokers taught me." He had come down the table while he said it — to see the feather-eyes, which could not be seen from eight feet — and he leaned to look at the phoenix with his hands behind his back, careful, correct, and then the needle drew its thread and one bead of the four decided otherwise, leapt the cloth, and rolled.

They reached together. It was nothing: a bead, a reflex, two hands arriving at the same small darkness at the edge of the lamplight — her fingers closed on the bead and his closed over where it had been, so that the side of his hand came to rest against the side of hers, warm, still, neither pressing nor lifted, and for the length of one breath the entire house of seven doors was that half inch of skin. Neither of them moved. Moving would have made it something; staying made it something; there was no third thing available. She thought, with the cold clear part of her mind that never once in her life shut up, that this was how it happened to people — not in gardens, not in songs, but over a runaway bead at the end of a day's accounts, between two people who had been careful for five years.

The pan came down in the kitchen doorway like the end of the world.

It was the big blackened rice pan, which did not slip from hands that had carried it fifty years, and it hit the tiles flat, a gong-crash that stood the whole dark house on its feet, and rolled its rim round in a long diminishing circle while Ah Yoke stood over it making no move at all to pick it up. Third Aunt's voice came from the kitchen court, sharp with sleep, asking what had died; an aunt called down the stairwell; Boon Siew was upright and away from the table with his papers in his hand, saying something easy about the hour, good night, the correct son of the house at the correct distance, gone up into the dark of the front rooms. Ah Yoke picked up the pan. She looked once at Mei Foong across the lamplit table, and the look had nothing in it whatever, which was how Mei Foong knew the pan had not slipped.

The scolding she was braced for did not come during the sweeping-up, nor on the stairs. It came later, in their room, the lamp down to a bead of flame — Ah Yoke on the pallet's edge with the wooden comb in her hand, not yet combing, turning it over as though it were the thing she was deciding about.

"Look at me," she said, and Mei Foong looked, and what was in the old amah's face was not anger. It would have been easier if it had been. "The son of the house is a door you cannot walk through. I know the price of that door." Her voice was low and hard and level, the voice she counted rice in, and her hands, Mei Foong saw, were not level at all. "You think because a door stands open it is yours to use. Doors stand open in this country all year, girl, and the houses still choose who walks in them. That one leads through the ancestral hall, past the tablets, past the Bibik, past every name on that altar — and the girl who tries it does not come out the other side. She does not come out anywhere. Do you hear me. I have—" and there the sentence stopped.

It stopped the way the comb had stopped, two years before, mid-stroke at the crown; the same silence, the same held hand; and in that silence Mei Foong heard, as clearly as if it had been finished, the story of two sisters in a house bigger than this one, the obedient wrist and the clever one, the bundle, the ring, the word no looked at like a door. I have seen it paid, the sentence was going to say, or I have paid it, and it said neither, and the not-saying filled the room to the ceiling.

"Sleep," Ah Yoke said, and put the comb away unused, and turned to the wall.

Mei Foong lay in the dark with her hand — the outside edge of it, the half inch — burning gently, like a coin held too long, and set the evening with the other things she was collecting: the name written in water, the story with no ending, and now the price of a door, quoted but never itemized. She did not promise herself anything about Boon Siew; she was too honest for promises she had no intention of keeping. She promised herself accuracy instead: to see it all the way to the bottom, whatever it was, and know what it cost before she paid, which was more than anyone else in that house could say of their weddings.

The war came by wireless on a Sunday evening in September, in the voice of an old Englishman reading from a paper six thousand miles away, and the walnut cabinet carried it through the house of seven doors the way the monsoon drains carried the first rain — suddenly, in every room at once.

Mei Foong heard it from the doorway with the supper tray in her hands: Germany, and Poland, and consequently this country is at war. The Towkay sat forward, listening the way he listened to the Singapore brokers, for the number inside the words. The Bibik's needle did not stop. Bee Nio stood in the doorway with one earring on. And Boon Siew stood behind his father's chair with his arms folded, nineteen years old, looking at the wireless as though it were the merbok's cage with the door swinging open at last onto exactly the dark he had said was out there.

Europe's war reached the padang before the month was out. On Sundays now the Volunteers drilled there, clerks and schoolmasters and two of the Chamber's sons in khaki that fitted the way borrowed clothes fit, wheeling and halting under the rain trees while a sergeant of regulars called the step, and half the town found reasons to stroll past the field at that hour. Mei Foong found her own reason once, a parcel that could as easily have gone another way, and stood a minute at the fence among the trishaw men. It was not an army; even she could see that; it was the town, dressed up as one, learning to turn left together. Boon Siew watched from the fence some Sundays, too long and too still, and did not put his name down, because his father had said what he had said; and MERBOK wrote that a fortress was a wall around a full larder, and a fortress around an empty one was a trap; and she posted it on the Tuesday with the temple letter, and the paper printed it under a complaint about the blackout regulations spoiling the racing.

The Towkay's answer to Europe was given at his own dinner table, a week into the war, with the good humour of a man proved right by catastrophe. Rubber had jumped; tin had jumped; the up-country agents were bidding for anything that could be planted. "You see," he said, nodding at the wireless cabinet through the wall as at a junior partner who had finally performed. "Schoolboys read the war news and see the end of the world. Grown men read the rubber price. Wars are eaten, boy — wars are driven, wars are shod, and every tyre and gasket and groundsheet in it grows on trees, and the trees grow here." He was buying the Ayer Keroh land, he announced, all four hundred acres of it, and the aunts made the sound that money makes passing through a room, and Bee Nio asked if this meant her trousseau could have the French crepe after all.

"And if the war does not stay in Europe?" Boon Siew said. Not hotly; that was gone; he asked it the way you check an entry you already know is wrong.

"Then the naval base at Singapore will earn its money," the Towkay said comfortably, "and rubber will go higher still, and you may thank me for the four hundred acres. Europe's war is Europe's, same as China's troubles were China's. We sit at the bottom of the map, boy. News dies of the heat before it gets here. Eat."

The purchase came up to the house in the ledgers in due course and passed under Mei Foong's ink: freehold, Ayer Keroh, survey fees and broker's cut itemized, the largest single entry she had ever ruled a line beneath. In the margin beside it, in pencil, small as a fallen eyelash: four hundred acres of weather. She checked the arithmetic, which was correct, and ruled the column off through the little words, and left the tail of the y showing; and that fortnight, for the first time, the coded margins did not feel like a game between two clever children. They felt like the minutes of a meeting no one else would attend until it was too late.

In the kitchen court the war was measured otherwise. Third Aunt came back from the market reporting rice up two cents and gunny sacks suddenly precious, and observed, over the shallots, that wars in Europe raised the price of everything except wages; and Ah Yoke, who had bought her second bag of rice after the Nanking photographs and never explained it, went to the Bibik about the store-room and came away with leave to fill the far bin "against the shipping." Nobody used any larger word for it. The bin was scrubbed, lined, filled, and lidded, and the lamp oil moved to stand on the lid, so that the whole arrangement looked like housekeeping, which is what prudence looks like in the houses of the careful. Mei Foong held the lamp while the old woman levelled the rice with the flat of her hand, smoothing it the way you smooth a sickbed sheet, and neither of them said the word later aloud, because saying it would only have spent it.

She finished the second slipper at four o'clock on a December morning, alone with the one lamp, in the year's last dark.

It had come down, at the end, to the phoenix's eye. She had kept it for last on the left face as she had on the right — one rust bead in a ring of gold, the bird's whole watchfulness in five millimetres — saved deliberately for a night when the house was so deep asleep that even the merbok under its cloth had given up rehearsing. The bead went in with the small defended click. She sat back. On the frame before her the pattern lay complete: peonies opening rust into gold, leaves of jade, the night-blue ground of heaven behind the climbing bird, and the phoenix looking back down its own tail of forty suns at the flowers it was leaving — one year of evenings, some thousands of beads, she had stopped counting in June — finished, and perfect, and not hers, and she looked at it for a long time in the kind of tiredness where the edges of things grow slightly bright.

The faces would go to the cobbler in the morning to be made into slippers; the linings she sewed in herself, because nobody else's stitches were to touch the work. The lining silk was cut and waiting, the colour of weak tea. And it was while she sat looking at the two faces side by side — the year on the table, the wedding a month off, the house asleep on top of her like the sea — that she did the thing she had not known until that hour she had been intending all year.

She took up the left lining and turned it face down, and on the underside — the side that would lie against the shell, silk to leather, the side no eye would meet again once her needle had closed the seam, not the bride's, not the Bibik's, not heaven's — she beaded two characters.

She worked them in night-blue on the tea-coloured silk, small as the characters on a name-paper, in beads so fine she had to bring the lamp to its highest and her face to within a hand's breadth of the work. Mei first: plum, plum-blossom, the tree that flowers in the cold, the first character of her own name and the blossom carved on camphor chests — a stroke at a time, each bead seated straight, because the row is only what that adds up to. Then, beside it, siew: the second character of his. Not the family name; she was not a fool, and anyway it was not the family she meant. Just the two given syllables side by side on the hidden silk, plum and siew, the way two names stand together on a red packet, or on a tablet, or in a register that would never in this world be opened for the two of them — an entry in the one ledger nobody audited, kept in the one hand that never made an error.

She said no words over it, even inside her head, because words were what she could not have. It was not a spell, and she did not believe it was a promise, exactly. It was an entry. It said what entries say: this happened; these two were here in the same year; let the account show it. And if a bride walked her whole life in her first steps, then let there be, under the bride's own instep, unfelt, unfaded, in the dark between silk and leather where no one would ever see, one row of the truth — the clever wrist's single act of theft in six years of perfect service, weighing rather less than a breath.

She sewed the linings down as the roosters started up in the lanes behind Jonker Street, seams fine enough to pass the Bibik's thumbnail, and the characters went face down into the dark of the work, and were gone. What remained was a pair of wedding kasut manek, peony-and-phoenix, flawless, and no one alive but she would ever know they were signed.

The cobbler mounted the faces that week, and on the new moon the slippers were presented.

It was done in the back hall, under the good lamp: the camphor box open on the long table, the slippers side by side in their bed of tissue, toes level, a year of evenings squared to the corners; Bee Nio hopping at the edge of the lamplight, forbidden to touch them until the inspection was passed; the seamstress in attendance with the trousseau lists; Third Aunt in the doorway with flour to her wrists, because the kitchen was owed a look and knew it. The Bibik came down with her keys, put on the spectacles she wore for jewellery and contracts, and lifted the right slipper into the lamp.

She read it. There was no other word for what her eyes did: along the rows, into the ring of every feather-eye, down the shading of the peony where rust gave over to gold, her thumbnail travelling the seams like a magistrate. She took her time; the lamp hissed gently; Bee Nio vibrated. Then the left, the same, row by row — and at the left slipper the reading slowed. Mei Foong, standing where the hands of a house stand, at the edge of the light with her own hands folded, watched the old woman's face above the beadwork and saw the inspection stop being an inspection. The Bibik was no longer checking the work against a standard. She was looking at it the way you look at handwriting you know.

The silence went on past every polite length. The seamstress glanced up. Bee Nio, for once in her life, said nothing.

The Bibik set the left slipper down beside its pair, toes level, exactly as it had lain, and squared the tissue with one finger. For a moment her hand stayed on the edge of the camphor box, and when she spoke it was not to the seamstress, or to her daughter, or to any of the living in the room.

"My sister beaded like this," the Bibik said.

Then she took off her spectacles, and took up her keys, and walked out of the back hall into the dark of her own house, and her door closed somewhere far inside it, softly, the way doors close on things, and did not open again that night.

The room she left behind recovered itself the way rooms do — Bee Nio seized the slippers at last with a cry of love, the seamstress found her voice and her lists, Third Aunt pronounced the pair too good to walk in and went back to her dough — and none of them had heard anything but an old woman being briefly strange about the past, because none of them was collecting.

Mei Foong stood very still.
