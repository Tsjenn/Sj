# The Vow

The Year of the Horse came in without one firecracker, which had never happened in the history of the street. Third Aunt made the New Year kueh with half the proper coconut milk and nobody to borrow from, and set the trays to dry in a kitchen court so quiet she could hear the paste crack; and the Bibik laid out the offerings in the ancestral hall with her own hands, oranges built small, everything correct and everything less, the way a woman sets a table in a house where someone is ill upstairs. No lion came down Heeren Street. No red paper went up on the seven barred doors. The town kept the festival the way you keep a candle lit in wind, both hands cupped around it, and over the rooftops, southward, all that day, the sky carried a sound too far off to be thunder and too steady to be anything else.

On the second day of the New Year the wireless told them what the sound had been.

The set was the Towkay's walnut cabinet still, in its corner of the front hall, though the household had learned to play it low and stand close, the way you drink from a stream in someone else's country. The voice that came up out of the hum was not the calm Singapore English that had said *frozen*, that had said *prepared positions*; that voice was gone wherever the ships had gone. The new voice said that the island fortress had surrendered. It said the surrender was unconditional. It said the island would now be called by a new name, which meant, the voice explained, Light of the South; and then there was march music, and the Towkay reached out and turned the dial to silence with the exact gesture of a man closing the eyes of the dead.

Nobody in the front hall spoke. There was nothing left to fall. The whole long argument of the house — schoolboy talk, the mountains will hold, the river will hold, the base will earn its money — had run down the peninsula ahead of the bicycles and gone into the sea at the bottom of the map, and the men who had been right and the men who had been wrong stood together in the same dim room in the same silence, equally beggared. Mei Foong, from the doorway, watched Boon Siew watch his father's hand on the dial, and in the household book that evening, in the narrow backward column headed with the character for *under*, she wrote: *31. The island. There is no more south.*

Then Malacca heard the news a second time, in the language of traffic.

For two days the trunk road poured the victory southward past the town: staff cars flying pennants, lorries of soldiers singing in the open backs, guns with their muzzles capped riding like sated animals. The town was ordered to mark the fall with flags, and marked it, paper suns handed out at the checkpoints and hung obediently from shutters that had worn blackout crosses a month before; and the street under the flags was so quiet you could hear the paper move. And after the victory had gone south, the road began to send things back north — and the things it sent were stories.

They came with a rice broker's clerk who had been caught on the island and walked home. They came with a woman from Muar looking for her son. They were told in the kopitiam back rooms and the temple courtyards in the voice the kitchen had used for Penang, low, unadorned, checked against each other like sums: that in the fallen city the men had been summoned — all the Chinese men, called out of their houses to open ground, to schoolyards and road junctions; that there were queues, and tables, and hooded men who looked at faces; that some men were stamped and sent home, and some men were put on lorries, and the lorries did not come back. Whom they wanted first, the stories agreed on: men who had given to the China relief funds, men who had drilled with the Volunteers, men whose names had been in the papers. No one said numbers. No one needed numbers. The stories came up the road at walking pace, and ahead of them, through the towns the war had already swallowed, ran the long quiet of people listening for lorries.

That was the weather in which he came to the account room, on the fifth night of the New Year, and shut the door.

She was at the table with the household book open, and he sat down opposite her — his father's gesture, the flat hands, no cards — and laid it out as she had taught him without meaning to teach it, worst number first.

"The relief funds are in the Chamber's registers, and my father gave conspicuously, and I carried the tin twice round the padang myself in 'thirty-seven," he said. "That is the first entry. The second: four years of letters in the Straits papers arguing that this country should be able to feed itself, signed with a bird. Print does not burn, Ah Foong. Somewhere in Singapore tonight there is a bound volume with everything the merbok ever sang in it, and men being paid to read." His voice was level, the counting voice; only his thumb moved, once, along the table's edge. "I do not know that they will come for me. I know what I would find if I were looking, and I taught you never to hold a book I would not want audited."

"The third entry," she said, because there was one; there was always one.

"The third is Ipoh." He almost smiled. "On paper I am a betrothed man, postponed until conditions permit. Conditions will never permit anything again — the world that wrote that wire is gone; the girl herself set me free in the only honest sentence anyone wrote last year. And still, if I am taken next week, I will have died a polite fiction: betrothed to a stranger, heir to a firm with no trade, son of a house whose doors mean nothing now to anyone but us." He leaned forward into the lamp. "You said: when the sea has brought what it is bringing, we will know what everything costs, and we will settle then, at true prices. Ah Foong. The New Year came in with the island. The prices are true now. Everything costs exactly what it costs and nothing is hidden in any column anywhere, and I am asking you to settle. Marry me. Not in Singapore, not after the war, not when conditions permit. Before heaven, this week, with one witness, in the town where we were going to be impossible. The world that forbade us is gone or going. I will not spend whatever is left being forbidden by its ghost."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them the house creaked under its cargo of sleeping cousins, and beyond the shutters the occupied street lay listening to itself, and Mei Foong looked at her own hands on the book and did what she did with every account that mattered, and read it twice; and found that for the first time in eight years there was nothing in the second reading that had not been in the first. No door, no altar, no Ipoh, no tablets. Every wall she had ever refused him over had been pulled down by the same hands that were pulling down everything, and what was left standing in the ruins was one table, one lamp, and the two of them, and the truth she had carried since the bicycle.

There was one wall left, and it was hers, and it was not hers to pull down. *Some names are prayers, some are debts.* She had learned in January whose blood she was; she had bowed alone in the dark to tablets that were hers and claimed nothing; and for one long moment, in the lamplight, she stood at the edge of telling him — cousin, she thought, blood of your blood, the match that could have been made in daylight with lanterns in both air wells, if the world had been told the truth twenty years ago. But the truth was not hers alone; it was held on an oath sworn to a dying woman, and it was mortgaged to the safety of everyone who did not know it; and heaven, which kept the only register they were going to get, would know both names without being told.

"Yes," said Mei Foong.

She said it the way true things went into a ledger, without ornament, in ink; and then, because a settlement is not a settlement until the terms are read aloud: "This week. Before heaven, with one witness. And I will tell you the witness, because there is only one person in Malacca who can open the door we need, and she has spent eight years keeping us out of it."

Ah Yoke listened without moving.

She was still convalescent, propped on the pallet with a shawl over the black-and-white, her breathing carrying the dry aftermath of the fever in it; the room smelled of the herbal brick Third Aunt boiled for her twice a day. Mei Foong told it plainly, kneeling by the lamp: the island, the stories on the road, the account room, the yes. Then she stopped talking, and the shrine lamp fluttered, and the silence went on so long that the merbok, far off under its cloth in the air well, made its small folded night-sound, once.

"I told you the son of the house is a door you cannot walk through," Ah Yoke said at last. "I stood in a kitchen in this town twenty years ago and watched what that door costs, paid in full. I have been right about doors all my life, girl. Being right is not the same as being kind. It is not even the same as being obedient to heaven; it is only cheaper." She turned her head and looked at Mei Foong, and her eyes in the small light were the eyes from the rice scoop, exact, counting. "Two things I swore to your mother. You know them now. Never tell — that one I keep; it is not mine to break and not yours either, and you have worked that out already or you would not be kneeling there with your mouth shut. But the first oath was: raise her near her blood." Her breath clicked. "I have kept that oath with buckets and brooms for eight years, girl — near her blood, carrying its washing. No one on this earth can raise you nearer your blood than the thing you are asking me to witness. So heaven has audited me, and found the way I kept my oath was true and small, and is offering me once, before I die, the chance to keep it large." She pushed herself up off the pallet, refusing the arm Mei Foong offered, and stood, and was the whole of herself. "Cheng Hoon Teng. Before dawn, three days from now; the sworn sisters pray at that hour and the side hall asks no questions of anyone I bring. The old nun has known me forty years. The keeper of lamps owes me thirty years of oil. Say nothing in this house — not to the walls." And then, already turning to the case with the broken clasp, in the voice she counted rice in: "Your mother married with no one's leave, and everything was taken from her. You will marry with mine. We will see what heaven does differently."

She did not open the case that night while Mei Foong watched. But in the morning the red-thread bundle had been moved from the bottom of it to the top.

The kebaya Mei Foong went for herself, because there was only one place to go.

She walked to the Lim house on the third afternoon with a basket of laundered linens on her arm, which was a reason no sentry and no aunt would look at twice, and found Bee Nio in the back room with her feet up on a stool and the child riding high and unmistakable under her housedress now, directing her husband's one servant in the rolling of bandages she was no longer permitted to roll. She sent the servant for tea. Mei Foong set down the basket, and stood, and said what she had decided to say and nothing more.

"Lend me your good kebaya. The pale one, with the embroidered hem. And the kerongsang for it." And then, because there was no way to make it smaller than it was: "And the slippers."

The room went still. Bee Nio's hands came down slowly onto the arms of the chair, and she looked at Mei Foong — a long look, the whole length of eight years, the pallet and the fittings and the combed-up hair and the war; and Mei Foong watched a question assemble itself behind her eyes, visibly, word by word, and reach her mouth, and stop. Bee Nio was twenty; she had buried her girlhood in a twelve-day wedding and carried her first child through an invasion with her chin up; she was vain, and brave, and very much wiser than the house had ever paid her for.

"Don't tell me what for," Bee Nio said. "Then whatever I am asked, by anyone, ever — I know nothing."

She got up in the careful counterweighted way of women in her month, and went, and was gone a while, and came back with the kebaya folded in tissue and the little gold pins in their pouch, and the camphor box. At the box her hands slowed. She stood holding it, looking down at the plum blossoms carved in its lid, and when she spoke it was lightly, in the voice she used for returning a length of cloth.

"They no longer fit me, you know. My feet these days belong to somebody else entirely; everything of mine belongs to somebody else; that is the trade, nobody explains it in advance." She set the box on top of the tissue, squared it, and did not take her hands away. "I stood up in these on the worst morning of my life and my feet knew how. They make feet brave," said Bee Nio, to the box, "whoever wears them," and looked up, once, straight into Mei Foong's face, and what was in her own was not a guess completed but a guess deliberately left unfinished, folded away with its ends untied. "Bring them back when you bring them back. And tell me nothing then, either. I find I am very good at knowing nothing. It may be my life's work."

At the door, with the basket heavier by one year of her own evenings, Mei Foong turned; but Bee Nio had already put her feet back up on the stool and taken up a bandage she was not permitted to roll, and was rolling it.

---

They went to the temple in the last dark before the fourth dawn, in the hour the curfew died.

Ah Yoke walked ahead through the grey streets as she had walked ahead on eight birthdays, black-and-white, unhurried, a shape the town had trusted for thirty years; Mei Foong followed with the camphor box wrapped in the indigo cloth, dressed in another woman's good kebaya with the little gold pins cold at her breastbone. At the corner by the bridge a sentry stood in the light of a shrouded lamp, seventeen years old and chilled, and they bowed, as the town had learned to bow, and were not stopped; and the bow cost what it cost and bought the morning, and neither of them spoke of it. Boon Siew came the other way, by the lanes, in the high-collared suit he had worn to his sister's wedding feasts, and was waiting where the temple wall began, bareheaded in the dark, and when Mei Foong saw him there — still, collected, watching for her the way he watched for ships — she understood that she had never once seen him afraid in her life, and that he was not afraid now, and that this was the thing she was marrying.

The keeper of lamps opened the side gate without being knocked for. Inside, the great courtyard held its stone cold underfoot and its first-sun roof still black overhead, the porcelain dragons and phoenixes waiting up there in the dark for the light they had caught every morning for two hundred years; the coils of incense turned their smoke slowly under the beams; and in the side hall the old nun sat by her black table as though no war had been invented, with the registry book and the brush and the stone of ink, and looked at the four of them — the amah, the son, the girl, the hour — and asked nothing at all. Ah Yoke spoke to her quietly, three sentences. The old nun looked at Mei Foong for a moment, the look of a woman who had written ten thousand names and knew all the reasons two of them might come in before dawn; then she rose, and lit the altar lamps herself.

Guan Yin stood above the lamplight, dim gold, her face the face that had watched Ah Yoke's plait coiled and pinned in Canton half a century ago, mercy administered across ten thousand mornings without once changing expression; and before her, on the worn granite, they did the thing there was no other roof left in the world to do under.

They offered the joss together, three sticks each, and bowed three times. Then the tea, which Ah Yoke had carried from the house in a basket, the pot wrapped in cloth against the walk: two small cups poured, and — because there were no parents in that hall, and could be none, and both of them knew all of the reasons and one of them knew more reasons than the other — the cups were offered outward and upward first, raised toward the smoke-well where the sky was greying, *to heaven*; and then lowered, held a moment above the cold stone, *to earth*; and the courtyard took the gesture the way the air well took rain, straight down, an old channel for exactly this. Then they knelt, both of them, and served tea to Ah Yoke.

She sat for it on the nun's stool, upright in her blacks, her hands taking each cup in turn with the steadiness she had bullied back out of the fever, and she drank — which made her, by the oldest arithmetic in the Straits, the family of the marriage, the elder who receives; and if her lips moved a long time over the second cup, and if what they said was addressed less to the couple kneeling on the granite than to a woman nine years dead in Guangdong, none of the living presumed to read it.

The vows they said plainly, facing the altar, in the words each of them had been building for years without knowing it. "I, Tan Boon Siew, take this marriage of my own accounting," he said, "whatever it costs, however long the books stay open, before heaven, which audits everything." And Mei Foong — who was marrying under a borrowed surname, before a goddess who would know perfectly well whose granddaughter knelt on her stones, and who found, when the moment came, that the falseness of the name did not matter at all, because heaven reads through paper — said: "I, Ho Mei Foong, enter this marriage whole, owing nothing, everything paid at true prices. What we build, no one alive can cut."

The old nun wet her brush and drew the two names first in water on the black table, side by side, and they shone there together the length of a breath; then she wrote them on red paper, the two names under one heaven, and folded the paper, and gave it to the altar flame, and the record went up with the smoke, to where records of that kind are kept.

Then Ah Yoke stood, and took from inside her blouse the small hard thing she had carried against her heart through the walk and the bow and the sentry, and unwrapped it: the ring of green jade, warm-toned, a woman's size, threaded already on a cord of plaited red silk. She did not put it on Mei Foong's finger. She put the cord over her bowed head, and settled the ring inside the collar of the borrowed kebaya, against the skin, out of sight.

"Not on the hand," Ah Yoke said. "Hands are seen." And to Boon Siew, who was watching with the stillness of a man who knows he is at the edge of something withheld: "It is an old ring of her mother's family. It has been to China and back to reach this morning. It is the whole of her dowry, and it is worth more than everything your father sewed into the mattresses." Which was true, and was all she said; and Mei Foong, feeling the jade take the warmth of her breastbone — her mother's ring, her grandmother's before that, lifted from an altar in this town in 1921 by a girl who chose love and was erased for it, and carried into exile, and carried home — bowed to Ah Yoke, wife and niece and debtor, and said nothing either, and the two silences fitted each other exactly, like the halves of a tally.

The sun found the roof as they came out through the side gate: the porcelain garden catching first light, dragon and phoenix taking fire over the dark courtyard, heaven putting up its lanterns for whoever was getting married that morning.

Chan Brothers opened its back door to them at seven, for a dollar.

It was Mei Foong's dollar — hoarded since the flood year, the second of the two the Towkay had given her before the whole hall, kept flat in the seam of her case for six years because some money is not for spending, until you learn what it was saved for. The studio man was the same man who had arranged Bee Nio's wedding by rank on the front steps two years before, older now, thinner now, a man whose trade under the new masters was identity photographs at three for a coupon; and he took the dollar, and looked at the suit and the kebaya and the hour, and asked, like everyone worth anything in Malacca that season, no questions. One plate, they agreed. One print. And the plate itself to go with the print, into their hands; nothing to remain in the studio, in any drawer, under any name.

He posed them against the grey garden backdrop, beside the little table with its vase of lilies — the studio's lilies, which were cloth, and had stood in that vase through every wedding since before the other war, and photographed, he said, better than real ones, which hung their heads under the lights like the guilty. He set Boon Siew's hand on the table's edge, just so, the posed way; he stood Mei Foong beside him, close, at the edge of the frame's width, and stepped back under his black cloth; and it was in the last second, while the photographer's arm was going up, that Boon Siew's free hand found hers at the seam between them and took it, whole, in front of the lens and the lilies and the morning, the first time in nine years that his hand had held her hand where light could reach it.

"Full length," Mei Foong had said, when the man proposed the waist-up portrait the times could afford. "The feet also." And when Boon Siew glanced down at the peony and the phoenix burning quietly on her feet against the studio floor, she said, low, only to him: "So some part of the house walks with us."

"Solemn now, please," said the photographer from under his cloth. "A wedding portrait is solemn. The smiling is for afterwards, when you discover what you have done." And Boon Siew, instructed to be solemn by a man he found funny, on the morning he had discovered exactly what he had done, stood in his high collar with his wife's hand in his and almost, almost smiled; and the shutter fell; and the light of the fifth dawn of the Year of the Horse fixed them there together, side by side, feet neat as type, married.

---

The godown kept the last of it for them.

They came to the river separately at dusk, out of eight years of habit that had become, in five days, a discipline of war: she first, by the lane, with the print and the plate wrapped in the indigo cloth under her arm; he after, when the light was gone. The firm's godown stood shut and silent on its dead trade, the chalk ghosts of old prices on the office board, the false wall at the back breathing its rice into the dark; and above the river door, up the ladder stair, was the tally clerks' room — a camp bed from the auxiliary nights, a desk of manifests for ships that would not come, one shuttered window over the water. He had swept it. There was oil in the lamp and tea in a basket Third Aunt had packed for Ah Yoke's convalescence and Ah Yoke had silently redirected, and on the desk Mei Foong set the wrapped photograph, and beside it the camphor box with the borrowed slippers folded home into their tissue, the house's part in the day complete.

They put the lamp out early. The rain came up the strait after midnight, found the corrugated roof, and sealed the room under its long roar the way it had sealed the room below them a hundred days and one world ago — the same rain, the same roof, and everything else in the account reversed; and in the dark that was total, paper-sealed, warm, they were unhurried and quiet and entirely without doors, two people who had been careful for nine years learning, hand by hand and breath by breath, the country on the far side of care. Her palm on the beat of his heart. The rain walking the roof. His mouth at the old half-inch of her hand, paying it. Somewhere far off the night traffic of the victorious went by on the trunk road and had nothing to do with them; the house of seven doors slept up the street without them and had nothing to do with them; there was nothing left in the room that belonged to anyone else, and they spent it all.

Deep in the night, in the trough after the rain, she lay with her head on his arm and the jade ring a small warm weight fallen sideways against him, and felt him surface from half-sleep and gather her in.

"Entered," he said into her hair, the old word, and she heard the smile in it.

"Carried forward," said Mei Foong, and this once it was not a postponement; it was the whole of the arithmetic, the entry and the balance and the year to come, and they slept on it, husband and wife, one night, with the rain easing off the roof like a tide going out.

The morning was washed and bright, the first honestly beautiful morning of the occupation, which she would remember afterwards as its own kind of cruelty. They left the way they had come, ten minutes apart, out of the discipline; but at the head of the lane he was waiting, and took her hand in the open street, and they walked up toward Heeren Street together in the early light — a man in yesterday's suit and a woman with a wrapped photograph under her arm, married before heaven and no one else — and it was at the corner by the bridge that they saw the crowd.

It stood in silence, which was how the town did everything now. At the wall where the auction notices used to go, a man with a bucket was smoothing up a poster, wet paste shining at its edges, and a soldier stood on either side of him, not because the poster needed guarding but so that the street would understand it was to be read; and the street read it. It was printed in two languages, and it was addressed to the Chinese population of Malacca, and it said that all Chinese males between the ages of fifteen and fifty years were to present themselves for inspection, on the day appointed, at the padang; that they were to bring their identity papers; that no male of the stated ages was exempt; and that failure to report would be dealt with severely. The paste ran a little at the top corner, like the wall sweating. Nobody in the crowd spoke at all.

Boon Siew read it the way he read a manifest, once, completely, committing the cargo. His hand did not tighten on hers; that was the thing she carried away, that his hand did not tighten. Under her blouse, against her breastbone, the jade hung warm on its red cord, one night old in its new life. He was twenty-one years old, and every stated age in the town was standing very still around them in the sunlight; and Mei Foong looked at the wet black characters and began — because it was the one thing she knew how to do with what was coming — to count.
