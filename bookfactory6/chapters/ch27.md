# What the Bibik Knew

The mould came down on the table twice, and the second knock was the one that mattered: the first to loosen, the second to turn the little red tortoise out onto the banana leaf whole, shell-lines sharp, no torn edge. Mei Lan, five years old and up on the stool with her sleeves pushed past her elbows, got it wrong four times and right on the fifth, and the ang ku kueh came out of the wood perfect, and she looked up at the old woman in the rattan chair with her whole face open.

"Now the next one," said the Bibik. "One good one is an accident. Ten is a hand."

She sat at the head of the long table with her feet on a stool and her keys in her lap, though the doors those keys opened had for a year been opened by other people. The court behind her went about its noise — the well, the charcoal, Third Aunt quarrelling with a nutmeg — and the light of the third month came down the airwell in a column with the dust standing in it. There was flour to the Bibik's wrists most afternoons that season, because it was the one thing left that was hers, and because the doctor had said walk a little and she had translated the instruction into her own language, which was work.

"Kim," she said. "The santan. It is sitting there being nothing."

Mei Foong brought the coconut milk and set it at the Bibik's right hand, and went back to the dough.

Nobody in the house corrected her any more. Third Aunt had done it once, in February — *Bibik, that is Foong* — and had been told to mind her fire, in a voice from the old regime, and had not tried again. Bee Nio had said, on the stair, with the gentleness she used now for arrangements Mei Foong was not to be consulted about: "Let her. It costs nothing." It cost something. Nobody had asked what.

She had been Kim since the end of the wet season, and only ever after noon. Mornings the Bibik knew her perfectly and called her Foong and gave the day's orders in the flat inventory voice she had used since before the war: the wet market, the joss, the boy who delivered ice and was cheating them by the block. It was in the afternoons, with the steam up, that the name changed — as though the room did it, as though that court at that hour was a place where a different woman ought to be standing. Dr. Lim said the heart was tired and that a tired heart tires everything; and later, on the back step where he said the true things, that people do not lose their memories evenly, they lose them the way a tide goes out, and what lies deepest shows last.

So Mei Foong answered to it, and stood at the far end of the table pinching dough into cups for the mung bean, and listened.

---

The teaching that season had a hurry in it that nobody named. Before, the Bibik had stood over a kitchen and issued corrections; now she sat and dictated, and something had come into her voice that had not been there, an insistence about order — this first, then this, never this before that — like a woman reading a will aloud. She began with the kueh of festivals and worked down towards the kueh of ordinary Tuesdays. Kueh koci, the leaf folded into its cone and the gula melaka going in molten. The nine layers of kueh lapis, each given its four minutes and no argument, a whole afternoon spent on a thing eaten in six bites — "that is the point of it; anybody can feed people; patience is what you serve" — and Mei Lan made to sit and watch every layer, asleep by the eighth, and the Bibik saying over her head, "She will remember she was made to. The hands learn later."

Nothing was written down. Nothing had ever been written down in that kitchen. A handful; two fingers of water above the rice; the sugar until it smells like it is about to be angry; the fire brought up when the leaf says so. Mei Foong stood through the afternoons doing the pounding and the grating and the folding, taking the corrections meant for a five-year-old because a five-year-old cannot lift a pot, and at night she wrote the day's kueh down in a school exercise book, small and upright, ingredients at the left, method numbered at the right, a ruled line under each entry. Where the Bibik had said *until it looks right* she wrote what right had looked like. By the middle of the third month she had thirty-one: the only record in existence of a hundred years of a family's cooking, kept by the servant, in a two-cent book, at eleven at night.

*Dear husband. Your mother is teaching your niece to make ang ku kueh, and I am at the other end of the table making the filling, which is the part that takes three hours. She calls me by her sister's name now and nobody corrects her. I have started a book of the recipes, because when she goes there will be nobody left in this house who knows how much water. That is my whole reason and I have examined it twice. — Your wife, who has learned to be called by other women's names and to come when called.*

---

The stories came the way steam comes, in the gaps of the work.

"There were two of us at this table," the Bibik said, over the tarts. "The pastry was the small one's. She had cold hands. You want cold hands for pastry and a hot temper for sambal, and she had both, so she was worth two girls and a great deal of trouble."

She never used a name. The name was not on the altar in the front hall and it was not in her mouth; it had been taken out of the house in 1921 the way a tooth is taken out, and the gap had been kept clean ever since. She said *the small one*, or *my sister*, and only ever in the kitchen, and only ever in the afternoon.

The small one had broken the good ang ku mould and glued it with rice paste and served the kueh with a crooked shell for two years before anyone caught her. The small one had wanted to learn the buah keluak and had not been allowed, their mother saying the pounding was for older wrists, and had learned it anyway by standing at the door and counting.

"That is how she learned everything," the Bibik said. "At the door. Counting."

Mei Foong made her face do nothing, and her hands went on pinching the dough into cups, and she thought about eleven letters that had gone south out of Kwangtung across eleven years and never once been answered.

---

At the end of that week the Bibik had the mortar brought to the table to watch the rice pounded for the kueh bangkit, and Mei Foong sat down to it with the stone drum between her feet and did what she had done since she was twelve: pestle up, and at the top of the stroke the wrist turned, a small outward flick that shook the head clean, so the rhythm was not thud, thud, but thud-and-turn, thud-and-turn, tidy as a loom.

The Bibik watched the mortar for a long time.

"Where did you learn to turn it like that?"

"Nobody taught me, Bibik."

"No," the Bibik said. "Nobody taught her either." And she took a pinch of the flour and rubbed it between finger and thumb and said it was still coarse, which it was, and the afternoon closed over the moment the way water closes.

Two days later she sent for Mei Foong and gave her an order for beadwork, keys in her lap and spectacles on: a pair of kasut manek for her own laying-out, peony and phoenix on black, the pattern of Bee Nio's wedding year, made small — "my feet are not what they were and I am not going to be walking" — and not on any account to be worn in; new beads, unwalked soles. And a child's pair, blue on white, the grandchild's mourning slippers, because a granddaughter walks in blue behind a grandmother and Bee Nio would leave it to the last week and buy something from a shop.

"Also there is a list," the Bibik said. "Bring paper."

The list took an hour and a half: the tables; which two aunts were not to be seated within speaking distance; what the kitchen was to send out on the first night and what on the third; the band, yes; the paper house, yes, but not the paper motorcar, which was vulgar and which the Towkay would want. She dictated her own funeral the way she had dictated forty New Years, and said at the end, "Read it back."

Mei Foong beaded the slippers through the fourth month, at night, under the one lamp; and on the evening she finished them she carried them down in their tissue, and the Bibik lifted the right one into the lamp and read it — along the rows, into the ring of every feather-eye, her thumbnail travelling the seams like a magistrate. Then the left. At the left, as it had nine years before, the reading slowed.

The old woman set the slipper down beside its pair, toes level, and squared the tissue with one finger, and did not take off the spectacles.

"I said it out loud once in this house," she said. "In front of a seamstress and my own daughter. That my sister beaded like this." She looked up over the glasses, and her eyes were entirely clear, which was the worst of it. "I have paid for saying it every day since. Take them away. They are correct."

---

She was on her feet until the second week of the fourth month, and in the rattan chair four days after that, and then she was in bed, and the house rearranged itself around the fact within an afternoon.

Bee Nio moved a camp bed into the room and slept in it and got thin. The aunts came in relays and were fed. Dr. Lim came twice a day and did what a good doctor does at the end, which is mostly to make the room truthful; he told the Towkay plainly, in the office with the door shut, and afterwards the Towkay stood in the doorway of his wife's room a while without going in, and went back to his desk. Mei Lan was brought in each evening in her nightclothes to say goodnight, and reported afterwards, gravely, that Grandmother's hands were cold and that she had held them and made them warm.

And most nights, past eleven, the bell in the passage went, and Bee Nio would come out and say, in a voice that had stopped meaning anything, "She's asking for Kim."

---

On the last night the rain came early and heavily and then stopped, and the house dripped, and the room smelled of medicine and citronella and, under both, of the camphor of the almari.

Bee Nio had been sent to bed at nine by her father in one of the two or three sentences he addressed to her that year. The aunts had gone home. Mei Foong sat on the low stool with the lamp turned down to a bead, doing what she had done since Ah Yoke: the sponging, the propping, the half-spoon of water at the corner of the mouth.

The Bibik woke at about two, all at once, and was there.

"Sit me up."

Mei Foong got an arm behind the small hot shoulders and lifted, and banked the bolster, and as she leaned across, the cord at her neck swung out of her collar and the ring on it turned once in the lamplight, green as a leaf held to a window, and hung there between them.

The Bibik looked at it. Mei Foong did not move, because moving would have been an answer, and she had been trained for fourteen years by a woman who had trained her never to answer first.

The Bibik lifted one hand — it took a long time; the hand had been the fastest in that house for fifty years — and closed her fingers around the ring, not pulling it, only holding it, the way you hold a thing to read the mark on the back.

"Off," she said.

Mei Foong lifted the cord over her head and put it into the old woman's palm. The Bibik brought it up close to her face and turned it and found what she was looking for, which Mei Foong had never known was there.

"My mother wore this on her third finger, and in her last year it went loose and she wore it on the little one, and it wore a flat on the inside of the band," the Bibik said. "There. You can feel it if you have the thumb for it." She let her hand come down onto the blanket, still holding it. "It went off the altar table in the fifth month of the year twenty-one, before the fires were lit, and I stood in this house and said to my father: let her keep it, and everything else she has taken, and let this family have no more of her. I was twenty-three. My voice did not even shake. They wrote her out that week and I watched the brush do it."

"Bibik—"

"Don't." One finger came up an inch off the blanket. "You have been in my kitchen fourteen years and you have never once talked when the work was going. Don't start on me tonight."

She lay and got her breath, in the small careful instalments that were all the night would advance her.

"Eleven letters," she said. "One a year, eleven years, from a market town in Kwangtung nobody in this house could have found on a map, and every one came into my hand off the tray, because the post came to me. My father was dead by the third; my mother by the second, so the first two were addressed to a woman already on the altar." A sound that might have been the ghost of a laugh. "And every one of them asked what we had eaten at the New Year. Not for money. Not once for money. What kueh. Whether I had kept the mould she broke." Her thumb moved on the ring. "I never answered one. I could not answer and I could not burn them, so I did what this house does with what it cannot manage. I put it in a drawer and locked the drawer."

"You kept them," Mei Foong said.

"I kept them." The old head turned on the bolster. "The seventh one says: I have a daughter. It says: her name is Mei Foong, the surname is borrowed and honestly given. It says: she was born on the eleventh day of the fourth month in the year of the dog, and she has our mother's chin." The clear eyes came round and stopped on her. "I have known that name and that date for twenty-six years. And then a woman I had never seen came to my gate with a girl of twelve, in the year thirty-four, and the girl had the chin, and I gave her rice for work and a roof and a rule, and I asked not one question, because a question is a door and I had my hand on that door all my life, holding it shut."

She said it without heat, in the ordinary voice she used for the price of ice.

"I am not confused, Foong. I know which of you I am talking to. In the afternoons it suits me to have my sister in my kitchen, and I am seventy-two and dying and I will have what suits me. But I know."

Mei Foong sat with her hands in her lap, on a low stool, in a room she had cleaned a thousand times.

"The bowl," she said. "In the court. The flour."

"The wrist," the Bibik said. "The solstice, the year after you came. She turned it like that at the top of the stroke, and I used to tell her it was showing off." She closed her eyes. "And the slippers, the year of Bee Nio's wedding, when I read a left shoe and knew the hand. And your face at the New Year table for fourteen years, in the doorway, waiting to take the dishes. A house is not blind, girl. A house only agrees not to look."

The rain started again lightly on the roof and stopped again, the way it does there.

"So. Hear it once and then we are finished with it, because I am not going to be able to say it twice." The Bibik opened her eyes and looked at the ceiling and delivered it, level, the way she had delivered every decree of her life. "I punished my sister for choosing wrong. Heaven punished me by making her daughter the best of my house and sealing my mouth with shame. You will not hear me say sorry, girl. Hear me say: the recipes are yours. They were always coming back to you."

Mei Foong's face was wet. She noticed it as a fact, the way you notice rain on a shutter, and did not move to it.

"The book you keep at night," the Bibik said. "Do you think this house is so large. Finish it. Every one. Then the child."

"She's five."

"She'll be fifteen." The Bibik's hand moved on the blanket, once, as if levelling something. "You know what I do with my nights? Arithmetic, over and over, the way other women say rosaries. I took in a kitchen widow with a fatherless child and gave her to my daughter with red eggs and a gold anklet, and said heaven repays; and this year I have got the sum out at last, and heaven repaid me in the one coin I ever refused." Her fingers tightened on the ring. "The child in the east room is my sister's grandchild. I said this house was short of blood. Heaven was listening. Heaven does not like to owe, and it does not like being told which currency."

Mei Foong wet the cloth and touched it to the dry mouth and was permitted.

"My son left no child," the Bibik said, to the ceiling. "That is the other sum. It does not come out. It never will."

Mei Foong sat perfectly still with the cloth in her hand and said nothing at all, and it was, of everything she had ever done in that house, the hardest hour's work, and no ledger in the world had a column for it.

"Put it back round your neck," the Bibik said at last, holding out the ring. "If it hangs on me they will bury it, and then the whole thing has to start again." And when Mei Foong had lifted the cord over her head: "The name goes back on the altar. I cannot do it. I unpicked her out of this house with my own hands and I have not the hands left to sew her back in. Somebody in this family will have to, and I find that I mind that it will not be me." She turned her face towards the wall. "Now sit. Don't call anyone. They will only make noise, and I have arranged everything already, and you have the list."

She slept a little before four and woke near five and said, quite clearly, "The layers are only ever four minutes," and said nothing else. She died as the first light came grey into the airwell, in the hour when the kitchen fires used to be lit, with her keys on the table beside her; and Mei Foong closed her aunt's eyes with two fingers, and stood up off the low stool, and went to wake the family.

---

They buried her from the house of seven doors with every door open.

It went three days, and it was, everyone agreed, the last of its kind seen on that street: the front hall stripped and hung, the coffin on its trestles, the chanting going up into the rafters from before dawn until the lamps were lit; the aunts of two generations in white and dark blue with their hair undressed and no jewellery on them anywhere; Bee Nio in coarse hemp at the head of the mourners, holding up like a wall for three days and coming apart for four minutes on the second evening in the store passage, held while she did it by the household's amah, who happened to be standing there, and who put her back together and sent her out again.

The kitchen fed four hundred people and did not sit down; Third Aunt cooked the Bibik's own list exactly as dictated, and cried into nothing, having been raised where crying into the food was a disgrace. The Towkay received the town at the door in a black coat and said the correct things, and looked, Mei Foong thought, watching from the passage with a tray, like a man doing accounts under his hat.

On the third day the procession went up the street behind the band, past the shuttered shopfronts and the kopitiam where the old men stood out on the five-foot way with their caps off, and turned for Bukit Cina, where the hill of graves came down towards the town in its long terraces of dead. The family walked in its ranks: hemp at the front, then blue, and in the blue, five years old and gripping her mother's hand, Mei Lan, going gravely up the hill in slippers with a white ground and sky-coloured flowers, beaded for her at night by a woman at the other end of the line, where the amah walked with the syce and the kitchen girls and carried the water.

Mei Foong walked in the dust at the back of her aunt's funeral and did her own arithmetic, which was old and did not improve: that of the people going up that hill by grade of cloth, three were her blood, and none of them knew it, and one was in the coffin, and one was her daughter. At the graveside, when the earth went on, she stood where the staff stood and bowed when the staff bowed, and that was the whole of her mourning that anyone saw.

---

They opened the room on the ninth day, because the almari could not be left indefinitely and because the Towkay wanted the jewellery counted before the aunts had finished being sad.

Mei Foong wrote the inventory. She wrote every inventory in that house; nobody had thought to consider whether she should write this one. Bee Nio lifted the pieces out one at a time and named them, and Mei Foong entered them at the side table in her small upright hand — the kerongsang sets, the belts, the hair ornaments in their crumbling papers — while the Towkay stood at the window with his back to the room.

He turned round at four o'clock, when the drawers were empty, and looked at the cabinet for the first time.

"That one is short," he said.

He had built godowns; he had spent fifty years knowing the difference between what a space says it holds and what it holds. The bottom drawer ran four inches shy of the carcase. He took it out and set it on the floor and put his arm in to the elbow, and there was a small door at the back of the well, no bigger than a book, with a keyhole; and the key was on the ring that had lain in the Bibik's lap on the last night, and that Mei Foong had put into Bee Nio's hand herself.

He got it open, and looked into it, and then said, without turning his head, "That will do for today. Leave everything," and they left everything — the velvet, the papers, the half-finished list — and Mei Foong carried the inventory book down to his desk as she always did, and went to the kitchen, and the day went on being a day.

At two in the morning she came down for the ice-box key and the office lamp was burning.

The door stood half open. She stopped in the dark of the passage and looked in, because the account of that house had been her business for fourteen years and she could not have stopped reading it if she had wanted to.

The abacus was pushed away to the far corner of the desk. The shallow drawer lay open at his elbow. In front of him, squared to the corners of the blotter in three rows, the way that house squared everything, were the envelopes — small, foxed brown, foreign stamps, the postmarks of a market town in Kwangtung — and beside them, open, the household register in her own hand: the fair copy of the occupation list she had ruled out for him in 1942, twenty-two souls, name, age, sex, occupation. On the back of one envelope he was doing a subtraction in pencil.

He read Chinese the way he had been made to at seven by a tutor with a ruler and had used it since for chops and cheques and the names on the altar: slowly, one finger under the characters, his lips going. He had been at it since about six o'clock. He was on the third envelope.

She did not go in. She stood in the dark the length of ten breaths — long enough to see him stop, and take off his spectacles, and put the heels of both hands against his eyes and hold them there, an old man alone in a room with eleven letters and a list of servants and forty years to get through before morning — and then went back up the stairs without the ice-box key and lay down and did not sleep.

The lamp was still burning at half past five when she came down to open the kitchen. At six she carried up his coffee, black, one spoon, as she had carried it every morning she had lived in that house; and the door of the office, which had stood open at that hour on every one of those mornings, was shut.
