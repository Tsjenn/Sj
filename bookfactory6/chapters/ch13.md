# Sixty-Nine Days

On the first day, the Bibik sent for blackout paper before the wireless had finished saying Kota Bharu. The household was still gathered around the set in the front hall, the tea cold in the cups, the announcer's English coming through the crackle like a man speaking from underwater, and the Bibik rose from her chair with the particular stiffness of a woman who has decided that listening is over, and began to give orders.

Sentiment, in the Bibik, had always announced itself as logistics. She could not say aloud that the world had cracked in the night; she could say that every window in the house was to be papered before it was dark, and she did.

Mei Foong was sent with the second amah to count the windows, and counted them twice, because the first count seemed impossible. The house of seven doors had been built to breathe — air wells, transoms, shutters upon shutters, lattices over doors — and every opening that had spent sixty years letting in the light of Heeren Street now had to be sealed against the same sky. Seven doors' worth of windows came, in the end, to more paper than the stationer on Jonker Street had in his shop. He sold out before noon; by afternoon the town was pasting up butcher paper, newspaper, the red paper kept for weddings. Third Aunt boiled flour paste in the biggest wok, grim as a general, and the kitchen court smelled of starch for three days.

That was the eighth of December, nineteen forty-one. That evening, in the back of the household book, past the accounts, Mei Foong ruled a fresh page into a narrow column and wrote at the top of it nothing at all, and under the nothing she wrote: *1*.

She had seen Dr. Lim's fever charts: temperature taken morning and evening, marked as a dot, the dots joined, so that a sickness a body could only suffer became a line a mind could read. *You cannot argue with a fever,* he had said, showing her, *but you can watch it, and watching is half of medicine.* She had no thermometer for a country. She had the price of rice, the sound of the sky, and what left and did not come back. Each evening she wrote the day's number and one line beside it, small, in ink.

*1. Landings in the north-east. Paper on the windows. Rice at the usual.*

The drills stopped being funny on the second day. All that year the air-raid practices had been theatre — wardens self-important in new helmets, aunties fanning themselves in doorways, nobody troubling to crouch — and now the same warden came down the street at dusk and the street obeyed him like a schoolroom. The house went through Ah Yoke's dispersal drill in the dark, each child of the kitchen court to a numbered place, and nobody laughed, and afterwards Ah Yoke stood in the middle of the court with her lamp and said only, "Again on Friday," as if she had been waiting four years for the world to catch up with her arrangements.

The wireless said the troops in the north were withdrawing to prepared positions. It said it in the tone of a man declining to be alarmed, and said it again some days later about different positions, further south, also prepared. The Towkay sat by the set with his abacus silent in his lap, and at the end of each bulletin he said, "They will hold at the mountains," and then, the following week, "They will hold at the river," and Mei Foong, filling in her narrow column, understood that her fever line and the Towkay's sentences were the same chart, kept in different inks.

The planes went north at a great height in the second week, and after them, southward, at no height at all, came the news of Penang.

It came the way the true news now came, which was not by wireless. It came in motorcars with mattresses roped to the roofs, in lorries with children asleep on furniture, in the faces of families who stopped in Malacca because a Hokkien merchant could always find there a cousin, or a cousin's cousin, or at the worst a temple floor. Down the trunk road, day after day, the north emptied itself toward Singapore.

The first refugees the household saw with their own eyes were taken in by the Ee family across the street — relations from Penang, a towkay's widow and her daughters — and by evening the story had crossed the road with the food that was sent over, and been retold in the kitchen court in a low voice, and it was this: the island had been bombed, and then the island had been given up. And in the giving up, the Europeans had been taken off in ships in the night — the officials, the families, the tuans and the mems, all of them, gone south down the water — and everyone else, which was to say the town itself, all its Chinese and Malays and Indians, all its clerks and coolies and amahs, had woken to find the harbour empty and themselves left where they stood.

Nobody in the kitchen court said anything for a while after that. It was not the bombing. Bombing was the war, and the war had been coming up the peninsula all month like weather off the sea. It was the ships. Every person in that court had lived a whole life inside a promise so large and so old that it had never once needed saying aloud — and now it had been said, in the plainest language there is, which is a wake of foam headed south.

"So," said Third Aunt at last, to the stove, "we are the house, and they are the cats."

The Bibik forbade the sentence to be repeated. Then she went out in her second-best kebaya and sat one hour with the widow from Penang, woman to woman over tea neither drank; and she came back with her face closed and gave orders about mattresses in the middle rooms, because, she said, cousins would come — this house too had northern kin, and roads ran only one way now. She was right within the week.

*11. Penang. The ships. Rice half again the usual — Heng says his godown is empty. It is not empty.*

---

The men came for the lorries on a morning of white heat, a European in Volunteer uniform with a Malay sergeant and a book of chits. It was all correct: requisition, for the military, receipts given, payment promised after the war upon presentation — the chit so calm, so certain of its afterward, that Mei Foong copied it whole into the household book that night like a specimen. The firm's two lorries were driven out of the yard by strangers before noon. The syce, who had washed them every Saturday of their lives, stood in the gateway holding his rag.

The Bentley they did not take. The officer looked at it a long moment — huge, black, up on its blocks in the shed, the car no one in the household had ever learned to drive — and wrote nothing, and left it there, and Mei Foong put that in the column too, because the fever chart had begun to have a sense of humour, the way fevers do at their height: *17. Lorries requisitioned. The Bentley judged useless to the war. It is useless to everyone.*

The wire from Ipoh came the next day.

Wires had grown rare and slow — the lines were wanted for other traffic — so the buff envelope on the front hall table had gathered a small silence around itself before the Towkay opened it. He read it, grunted as if agreeing with a price, and handed it to his son: KHOO FAMILY REGRETS BETROTHAL CEREMONIES POSTPONED UNTIL CONDITIONS PERMIT STOP LETTER FOLLOWS STOP.

The letter followed two days after, addressed to the elders, full of the correct sentences — the times, the roads, the impossibility of an auspicious date in an inauspicious year, the certainty of resumption when conditions permitted. Mei Foong read the phrase and heard, precisely, a sharp lonely girl dictating form to a frightened family, and getting one page of her own into the envelope while she was at it.

Because there was one page more. Boon Siew brought it to the account room that evening and put it on the table where the ledgers were, which was where the two of them had always put the things that were true. Half a sheet, a fast, slanted, educated hand, no salutation.

*They tell me one does not write directly to a betrothed man, but the world that made that rule is being dismantled by aeroplanes, so. My father talks of after-the-war; I find I cannot make the phrase mean anything, and I refuse to write to you in sentences that mean nothing — you have had enough of those from everyone, and so have I. So, plainly: I release what was never mine to hold, and I am not sorry, and you should not be. Locked rooms survive wars. Good luck. — K.S.E.*

Mei Foong read it twice. Outside, very far away, something northward thudded, or the air pretended it did; the lamp fluttered; the house creaked with its new cargo of sleeping cousins from Taiping.

"She would have been wasted on me," Boon Siew said quietly.

"Yes," Mei Foong said. "She was the only one of all of them who saw you." She set the page down. Ipoh was up that road the planes went, and the lorries, and the news; a girl with gramophone records and a ruined father, sending good luck south like a coin flung off a sinking boat to someone swimming. "Answer her," she said. "While the wires still go."

"What is there to say?"

"Good luck," said Mei Foong. "It is what she has instead of all of us."

He sent it in the morning: two words and his name. There was no reply, and the year turned without one — Christmas passing unmarked in the shuttered town except by the church bells, which rang anyway, and the English New Year arriving with the northern sky lit low and orange where somebody's war was burning, and no one saying happy anything.

*24. Ipoh wire. Betrothal postponed until conditions permit. Some conditions permit more than others.*

---

By January the money had gone strange. Not worthless — that came later, under another flag — but unmoored, the prices casting off one by one from everything Mei Foong had ever entered in a book. Rice doubled, then doubled its double. Tinned milk vanished and reappeared at a number that would have bought the cow. Salt, matches, oil, quinine: each went up in turn like a kite with the string cut, and the household book, which had balanced every month for seven years under her hand, stopped being housekeeping and became weather — she logged the figures the way lighthouse men log the barometer, not to govern the storm but so that someone, afterward, would know it had been real.

The Towkay bought nothing at the new prices. He had no need. Behind the false wall of the godown, entered in the private ledger that only he and Mei Foong knew existed, the sacks stood ranked in the dark like an argument he had finally won — too late to be graceful, but won; and stitched into the mattresses of the house, in canvas pockets whose contents she had inventoried by lamplight in her smallest hand, the gold slept its yellow sleep and appreciated hourly.

It was Ah Yoke's hoard that opened.

The kitchen-court store — the rice bought bag by bag since Nanking, the oil, the salt fish, the tins, four years of a majie's wages and a majie's patience stacked to the rafters of the back room — had been laid in against exactly this, and when the street began to go hungry at the new prices, Ah Yoke went to the Bibik and the two old women spoke alone for a quarter of an hour; and then the side door to the lane was opened at six each morning, and the street learned, without one word being cried aloud, that rice was sold at the Tan side door at the old price, one measure to a household, no seconds, no exceptions.

Ah Yoke kept the door herself, with the brass scoop she had used to count rice into the bin every day of Mei Foong's memory. The measure was exact. It was exact for the Ee family's cook and exact for the widow who did washing and exact for the man who had money in his hand and offered triple; Ah Yoke looked at his triple as if he had offered her a dead rat, levelled the scoop with her palm, and gave him what everyone got. The queue in the lane was silent, and orderly, and long, and it moved past the scoop like a rosary through fingers, bead by bead by bead, and privately Mei Foong thought that in all her life she never once saw religion again as plainly as at that side door in January.

"Four years you have called me mad for buying rice," Ah Yoke said, on the second morning, not looking up from the scoop.

"You are mad," said Third Aunt, refilling the bin. "Praise God."

Boon Siew was gone by then, most days and half the nights. The Medical Auxiliary had taken every pair of hands that could drive, lift, or read a label, and he could do all three; Dr. Lim had signed him in, and an armband hung on the account-room nail where his ledger jacket used to hang. When the alarms went — real now, the sirens winding up over the rooftops, the town crouching, the sky full of engines going somewhere with all the time in the world — Boon Siew went toward it, with rice and bandages and morphia in a borrowed van, to the harbour district or the kampongs by the river or wherever the warden's telephone said; and Mei Foong learned the special arithmetic of women in wars, which is subtraction: the house minus one, the street minus its men, everything counted every hour against the possibility of less.

Bee Nio came home to sleep — home meaning the house of seven doors; on the nights Dr. Lim was at the hospital he would not have her alone in the Lim house, and she arrived each evening with her small case and her chin up, four months gone with her first child and rolling bandages at the district station every day among the mems and the nyonyas, refusing with her whole spine to be sent to Ayer Keroh. "My husband is here, my brother is here, my child is where I put him," she said, once, when the Bibik pressed. "We are all of us staying put." The Bibik never pressed again. Steel had turned up under the vanity the way rock turns up under a garden — you struck it, and re-planned, and privately thanked heaven for the geology.

The bombs came to the town three times that Mei Foong's chart recorded, and the third was the worst night.

It began at the black end of night, in rain. The siren wound up through the rain like a cat with its tail trodden on, and the household came down the stairs in the drill's good order and did not use the trench — the trench in the kitchen court garden, dug in October to the warden's diagram, stood knee-deep in monsoon water, and Ah Yoke, who went to see it with the storm lamp, came back soaked to her scalp and wordless with contempt — and so they did what the drill said second, and went under the great altar table in the ancestral hall, all of them, into the space where the offerings of a hundred years had been laid over their heads.

The heavy table took them the way an old tree takes fowl at dusk: the Bibik and the Towkay, Bee Nio with her arms crossed over the future, Third Aunt with her cleaver, which no one remarked on, the second amah, the kitchen girls, the two smallest Taiping cousins asleep on the drill's appointed quilt before the door had stopped rattling. Ah Yoke sat at the table's edge, dripping, refusing the dry shawl until the children had it, and coughed, twice, a small tight cough with a click in it, and Mei Foong made a note of that with the part of her mind that noted, because it was a sound she had not heard from Ah Yoke before.

And Boon Siew was there — home an hour when the siren went, too spent to stand, the armband still on his sleeve — and in the crowded dark under his family's altar his hand found Mei Foong's hand, in the black space between them where no eye could go, and held it.

They had not touched since the godown in the rain, when everything had been said and nothing decided. They did not look at each other now; there was nothing to see; the dark was total, the paper on the windows sealing out even the fires. The house shook, and the sky walked somewhere over the roofs of the town on great flat feet, wandering, as if choosing, and the brass on the altar overhead rang each time in tiny voices, and his thumb moved once across her knuckles and was still. That was all. In the ledger of the two of them, where entries were made in margins and codes and never in the open line, it was a whole page written fair. She held on. The house minus nobody, she thought, senselessly, fiercely, all of it, everyone under this table, minus nobody at all —

The Bibik began to pray.

She did it aloud, not loudly, in the dark, in Baba Malay and Hokkien plaited together, and it was not like the temple and not like the festivals; it was housekeeping; she was going through her dead the way she went through the storerooms, taking inventory, requiring things of each by name. She named her father-in-law and the founder of the firm. She named her own mother, whom she resembled; the grandmothers; the vague uncles; she informed the ancestors, in the tone she used to the market men, that this house had burned incense without one lapse for four generations and that accounts were expected to be honoured; and the sky's flat feet walked and the brass rang, and then — between one name and the next, with no change in her voice at all, so that afterward Mei Foong could never say whether anyone else under the table had even heard —

"Guat Kim. If you are anywhere. It is your sister. Watch the roof."

The floor tilted. Not from any bomb: the bombs by then were marching away east, the ringing in the brass going smaller. It tilted the way the world tilts when a word you have carried privately, folded to thumbnail size, soft as cloth with secret handling, is suddenly spoken into a dark room by the very last voice on earth — two syllables Mei Foong had seen exactly once, drawn in water on a black temple table, shining and drying: moon, and gold; the name with its first stone missing; her mother's name, in the mouth of the Bibik of the house of seven doors, between the ancestors and the aeroplanes, with an instruction attached: watch the roof.

*It is your sister.*

She sat perfectly still in the dark with her hand in Boon Siew's hand and her heart going like a drum sending a message she could not read. Ah Yoke — three feet away, invisible, wet, oath-bound — made no sound at all; and that, from Ah Yoke, was itself a sound.

The all-clear went an hour before dawn. They crawled out stiff as old dogs; the Bibik was the Bibik again by lamplight, ordering tea, ordering the quilts aired; smoke stood up thin and black from down by the river where the godowns were, and Boon Siew put his cup down half-drunk and went toward it. On the chart that evening, in her smallest writing, Mei Foong entered the raid, and the trench, and the rice sent to the burned-out kampong; and then sat a long time with the pen filled and did not write the other thing, because there was no line in any book she kept where it could go. A name is not a figure. It did not balance. It sat in the dark of her the way the sacks sat behind the false wall: provisioned against a day she could not yet imagine, and could not now avoid.

*33. The worst night. Godowns burned. All safe. The trench is a pond. A.Y. coughing.*

---

On the thirty-fifth day the wireless said that Kuala Lumpur had been given up, and after that, for a while, it said very little, and the road said everything.

The road said it in lorries going south with soldiers who did not look up. It said it in staff cars, in ambulances, in a gun towed by a lorry towed by another lorry; then it said it in smaller and stranger vehicles, and then, which was worse, it said it in nothing at all. The stream that had run down the spine of the peninsula since the eighth of December thinned, and stuttered, and stopped, and Malacca lay in the emptied middle of a Tuesday with its paper-blinded windows and heard, for the first time in weeks, its own doves.

There was no battle for the town. There was nothing to have a battle with, and everyone knew it; whatever would stand and fight was gathering far south, at the island, and the town lay on the map like a name the war had decided not to pronounce. For three days, the strangest weather of all: no sirens, no engines, shops shuttered, streets bare, the whole town indoors around its altars and its rumours — and the silence banked up over the roofs hour upon hour until it had a pressure, until Mei Foong caught herself wishing for the sirens, because the siren at least was somebody saying *we see it coming*, and the silence was nobody saying anything at all. In the air well the merbok, who had sung through every raid like a fool, sang into the hush, and the sound went up the shaft of the quiet house like a bead dropped down a well.

On the thirty-ninth day, in the morning, the bicycles came down Heeren Street.

Mei Foong saw them through the seam at the edge of the blackout paper, the shutter cracked the finger's-width that every shutter in the street was cracked. Soldiers, on bicycles, two by two, in a long easy column: dusty to the eyebrows, rifles slung, riding past the seven barred doors with their tyres making the sound of paper tearing slowly and their bells never once rung. They did not shout. They did not look at the houses. They had come the length of the peninsula down roads the empire had built for its rubber, and they pedalled down Heeren Street unopposed, at the pace of men with somewhere to be and no need to hurry, and the town watched them through a thousand seams and made, all together, no sound whatever.

By afternoon the flag over the Stadthuys was a white flag with a red disc, and the one that had flown there was gone, and that was all. That was the whole of the battle of Malacca: a column of bicycles, a change of cloth on a pole, a proclamation pasted where the auction notices used to go. Four hundred years of the town passing hand to hand — Portuguese, Dutch, English, each with their guns and their sieges — and the newest hand had taken it with bicycle bells it had not even needed to ring.

Later — much later, when there was a later — men in the kopitiams would do the arithmetic of that season the way men pick at a scar: sixty-nine days, they would say, from the landings in the north-east to the surrender of the island fortress in the south, sixty-nine days to take down a hundred years, count it yourself. Malacca's share of the sixty-nine was thirty-nine, and the last of hers took no longer than it takes to ride a bicycle down a street.

That evening the household ate together, all of it, family and kitchen court in one room by one shaded lamp, which had never happened before in the history of the house and happened without anyone proposing it. Nobody said much. At the end of the meal the Towkay said, to no one, "Whatever paper they bring, whatever queue they order — the house goes together, and the house comes back together," and it was the first time in eight years Mei Foong had heard him say a thing in which the kitchen court was inside the word *house*, and she understood that the word had been resized by fear, and that fear had, for one evening at least, made it accurate.

She filled in the chart while the others laid out sleeping mats downstairs, because upstairs and downstairs had quietly stopped being separate countries. *39*, she wrote, and beside it: *Bicycles in Heeren Street. The flag changed before the rice was cooked. No battle. No sound.* And then she ruled the column off — the double line, the way you close an account whose page is full though its business is anything but finished — and turned the leaf, and at the head of the new page, in the space where all December she had written nothing, she wrote the character for *under*, small and upright, and beneath it: *1*.

Begin again. Count everything. Someone would want to know it had been real.

From the amahs' room behind the kitchen, through the paper-blinded dark of the occupied house, came the small tight cough with the click in it, once, and again; and Mei Foong sat listening with the pen in her hand, and did not yet know that she was hearing the door of the next thing beginning, very slowly, to open.
