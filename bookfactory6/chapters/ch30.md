# The Question

On the last Sunday of July the whole street stood in the five-foot way outside Raju's kopitiam to hear the results come in, and Mei Lan, who was twelve and would not be moved, kept the tally in chalk on the back of the shop's shutter because the wireless said the numbers faster than the men could hold them. Fifty-one, she wrote at last, and under it, one. Then she stood back with the chalk in her fist and looked at the two figures the way her father used to look at a column that had come out clean, and said, "That is not an election. That is a landslide," in the flat pleased voice of a girl who had learned a word that week and had been waiting for weather to use it on.

"Where does she get it," said Raju's son from the coffee shop step. He was thirty that year and ran the shop while his father sat in the good chair and corrected him.

"School," said Mei Foong, who was in her doorway with a bride's slipper in her hand, and who had come out for the numbers and stayed for the girl. It was the answer she always gave and it had the advantage of being true.

Inside, the shop smelled as it had smelled since 'forty-nine: starch, beeswax, camphor, and the faint metal of the glass beads themselves, which had a smell if you had spent nine years with your face six inches above them. Two apprentices sat under the shaded lamps and had not moved for the election, because a Chief Minister may come and go but a wedding has a date. The ledger lay squared at the counter's end, and beside it, on its folded square of blotting paper, the pen.

Malaya had voted. That was the thing the street turned over that week, the way it had turned over the word endure ten years before: fifty-two seats, one government, a Chief Minister who was a prince from Kedah, and the wireless calling all of it by a word that had until recently belonged to the British. Malayan. In the kopitiam Mei Foong heard it tried on and taken off a dozen times a day, like a coat in a shop with no mirror. Old Toh the goldsmith said he was a man of Fukien province who happened to be in Malacca, and had happened to be so for fifty-one years. Raju's father said he was a Malayan and had the papers, and dared the shop to argue. A young clerk from the land office said the word meant nothing until it meant everybody, and got shouted at from four directions, and drank his coffee looking pleased; and Mei Foong, going past with her basket, thought: he sounds like a merbok. She thought it without pain now. That was one of the things the years had done.

At the end of that week Mei Lan came in after school with an exercise book and a face full of business, and Mei Foong knew before the book was open that something was coming, because the girl had her father's way of arriving fast at a table to get a thing past the sentries.

"Civics. We have to make a family tree — Sister Perpetua says a citizen should know where she comes from." She flattened the book on the beads, which was not allowed, and neither of them mentioned it. "Grandfather and Grandmother-in-heaven I have done, and Papa's side is easy because Papa writes everything down. But Sister says three generations. Auntie Foong, you were in that house before I was born. Who is who?"

Mei Foong put down the slipper. Out in the street a trishaw bell went; behind her a needle picked up a bead, a sound like a small clock.

"I was the amah's girl," she said. "I carried trays. Ask your mother."

"I asked Mother. She said do the Lim side, it is neater." Mei Lan pushed the hair off her forehead with the back of her wrist, the exact movement, and Mei Foong looked at the ledger a moment before she looked back. "Nobody in this family answers a question in a straight line. It is like buying fish."

"Then you had better learn to buy fish," said Mei Foong.

---

The questions did not stop, because Mei Lan was not a girl who stopped; she was a girl who filed. There were three of them, and within a year Mei Foong could have recited them in her sleep, and by the year after that she did, sometimes, and woke with her heart going.

The first was the slipper. It stood on the altar shelf in the middle hall on its square of faded silk: one child's slipper the length of a palm, peony and phoenix worked so small that the flower was no bigger than a five-sen coin, its sole white and unmarked. She had set it there in her first month of owning a roof, in a house where nobody could ask her by what right, and had allowed herself that one visible thing in a life otherwise entirely invisible. Eight years of customers had said what a pretty thing and passed on. Mei Lan at thirteen stopped in front of it and stayed.

"Whose foot?"

"Mine."

"It is too small for a baby's second week and too fine for a village."

"A baby's slipper is beaded before the foot is measured. The mother guesses. That is why it never fitted and never will." Mei Foong did not look up. "Move. You are standing in my light."

"Where is the other one?"

"There never was another one." Which was true, and was the whole of the truth about that slipper that any living person could safely be given, and she gave it in the voice she used for the price of gold thread.

The second was the pen. An apprentice had knocked it off the counter in her first month and been spoken to in a way she remembered for a year, and after that the shop treated the pen as furniture: green, mottled like river water under trees, its gold worn and unfaded, lying on blotting paper at the ledger's edge in a place where every other tool belonged to whichever hand was nearest. Mei Lan had been coming to that counter since she was six and had never once looked at it. At thirteen she picked it up, uncapped it, and read the two English words on the barrel, then the name of the school, then the year.

"This is Uncle Boon Siew's prize pen. Grandfather has the certificate in the hall — nineteen thirty-eight, English composition." She looked up. "Why is my dead uncle's prize pen in your shop?"

"Because your grandfather's account room was dismantled in the war and I was the one keeping the accounts," said Mei Foong, "and a pen goes with a ledger." She held out her hand, palm up, and got it back, and set it down squared to the corners, no more carefully than she would have set down a nib.

Siew Ean, who came on Thursdays to do the books and stayed for the arguing, said afterwards without looking up from her column: "Nineteen thirty-eight was a good year for composition."

"Do not help me."

"I was not helping. I was admiring the child." She ruled a line. "She is going to be a nuisance to somebody. It should have been a husband. I think it will be all of us."

The third Mei Foong could not touch at all, because it stood in the ancestral hall of the mansion, where she had no standing to answer anything. The Bibik had died in 'forty-eight, and when the hall was set right again there were two new tablets where there should have been one. Neo Guat Eng, in gold, with her dates and her titles and her married house. And beside it, a hand shorter, plain, no dates on it at all: Neo Guat Kim. The Towkay had ordered it from the carver himself in the week after the burial, alone, and had set it there with his own hands, and in nine years had explained it to nobody, including the daughter of the house.

"Grandfather. Who is Neo Guat Kim?"

"Your grandmother had a sister."

"When did she die? There are no dates."

"Before you were born."

"That is not a date, Grandfather."

"Then it is what you have got," said Tan Hock Lye, seventy-one years old, folding his newspaper along its crease and going out to the car he still could not drive. And Mei Lan came down Heeren Street that evening and stood in the shop doorway with her satchel still on and said, "Auntie Foong. Nobody in that house has a face."

Mei Foong's needle went in and came out. "What?"

"There is no photograph of Uncle Boon Siew anywhere. There is a tablet, and a certificate for a pen, and a school cricket group where Papa says he is third from the left, except third from the left is a smudge. I have been through every drawer in that house." She dropped the satchel. "Every family on this street has a big brown photograph of the son. Why haven't we?"

"Because in nineteen forty-two we burned everything with a name on it," said Mei Foong. "Books, letters, photographs, anything English, anything a soldier could ask a question about. Your mother burned her school reports. Your grandfather burned his contracts. Nobody was thinking about faces." She threaded a bead, and it took her two tries, and she made sure the two tries were slow ones. "We were thinking about the morning."

Mei Lan sat down on the stool by the counter and was quiet a while, which was rare enough to be a weather event.

"That is the first answer anybody has given me in a year," she said, "with a reason in it."

---

In February of 'fifty-six the town filled up in a way Mei Foong had seen only twice before, and neither of the other times had been happy.

They came in from Jasin and Alor Gajah and the coast road, on bicycles and lorries and on foot, and the shops shut at noon because there was nobody left in them to sell to; and the padang at Bandar Hilir went black with people from the sea wall to the road. Mei Foong took Mei Lan because Bee Nio's knees would not stand it, and because the girl would otherwise have gone alone, having said so.

The men had come home from London with the thing in their hands, and the date was announced in Malacca of all the towns in Malaya, out of an old courtesy to the oldest town; and when it came over the loudspeakers, out across the heads of every kind of person that country contained, Mei Lan was up on the sea wall with her sandals in her hand.

The thirty-first of August, nineteen fifty-seven.

The sound the padang made was not a cheer. Mei Foong tried more than once afterwards to put a word to it in a letter and never managed better than this: it was the sound a house makes when a door swollen shut for years comes open all at once, and everyone who has been pushing falls forward together. Around her, men she had watched bow at a bridge for three years and seven months were shouting a word with both fists in the air. Old Toh of Fukien province was weeping and denying it. A Malay schoolmaster in a songkok had his hand on the shoulder of a Tamil boy from the ice factory, and neither of them appeared to have noticed, and neither took it away.

And Mei Lan turned round on the sea wall and shouted down over the noise, with her whole face open: "Auntie Foong! I will be fifteen! I will be fifteen when it happens!"

That was the year the cages opened. Mei Foong, who had spent her life being told which room she might stand in, watched a country be told it would soon be allowed to say its own name, and caught herself doing a sum she had no business doing: eighteen months. Then twelve. Then the whole of it counted down in the newspapers like a wedding.

It was also the year Mei Lan began, without knowing it, to walk straight at the middle of her mother's life. The family tree had never really stopped. She had drawn it and been given a mark for it, and now she wanted the dates in it to agree with one another, having discovered in Form Two that dates are the bones of the thing.

"Papa. Where was I born?"

"At the country house at Ayer Keroh," said Dr. Lim, who had thirteen years of practice. "In the war. There was no petrol to bring your mother in."

"Mother says I was born in the town."

A pause of the exact width a clever girl notices and cannot yet name. "Your mother was very ill," said Dr. Lim, "and does not remember the month clearly, let alone the road."

"But you are a doctor, and you remember."

"I am a doctor," he said, "and I remember."

She brought it to the shop, of course. She brought everything to the shop, and that was the part Mei Foong had never learned to be strong against — that in the whole household the girl had chosen, from the age of six, the amah's counter as the place where things got answered.

"Auntie Foong. You were there in the war. Where was I born?"

The bead went on. Six to the stitch. Mei Foong watched her own hands do it and was distantly proud of them.

"I was in town. I heard about you two days late, from a lorry driver, and I put down what I was carrying." She looked up and let the girl see her, because a face that gives nothing at all is itself an answer. "That is the whole of what the amah knows. Now hand me the jade greens and stop interrogating your father. He has a heart the size of a fist and it gave him trouble two years ago, and every one of these questions costs him a night's sleep."

Mei Lan flushed to her ears, because she was fourteen and not cruel. But she handed over the greens, and said to the counter, low and mulish:

"Somebody in this family is lying to me. I do not mind being lied to about money. I mind being lied to about me."

---

The photograph came out of the mansion in October, in the ordinary way that dangerous things do: in a schoolbag, on a Tuesday, with a mango.

Mei Lan had been set to clear the camphor press in the back passage, on the theory, popular among adults that year, that a girl of fourteen with a grievance should be given cupboards. Under a stack of the Bibik's unused kebaya lengths she had found a flat parcel of studio prints tied in string, and had gone through them on the floor for an hour and a half with the mango going soft in her pocket, and had come down Heeren Street at a run with one of them held in both hands like a full bowl.

"Look. Look what I found. It is all of us."

She put it on the clean cloth, which was correct, and Mei Foong looked down at it and the shop went a long way off.

She had not seen it in twelve years. The photographer had come in 'forty-four because the Bibik, after a bad wet season and a worse dry one, had decided the house should be recorded while it still had people in it; he had come with a plate camera and a boy to carry it, and had arranged the household on the front steps by rank, the way such things were arranged. The Towkay and the Bibik seated centre in the two blackwood chairs. Dr. Lim and Bee Nio flanking. Standing behind: the cousins, the clerks, the two Tan aunts down from Muar. The children on stools in front, and in the middle of the children, in a white pinafore, gripping the edge of her stool with both hands and glaring at the lens as though it had insulted her, a girl of not quite two.

And at the very end of the back row, half a step out of the line, in the white blouse and black trousers of the household's amahs, a young woman looking not at the camera but slightly down, at the head of the child seated in front of her.

Mei Lan put her finger on the figure. "That is you."

"That is me."

"Auntie Foong." And her voice changed, and the change was what did the damage, because it was warm; it was full of a fourteen-year-old's clean, appalled, generous indignation, the exact quality of feeling her father had once carried to a kitchen table with a cast-off primer under his arm. "They made you stand. Look where they put you — right at the end, behind everybody, like furniture. And you kept those accounts. Papa says you saved the firm twice—"

"Three times," said Mei Foong. "Twice is your father being polite."

"Three times! And they sat the aunts from Muar down in front, and put you at the back at the edge with the amahs." Mei Lan looked at the print with her jaw set, then up, blazing. "Poor Auntie Foong. It makes me sick. When I am older I am going to have this enlarged and framed and hung in the front hall of that house, and I am going to write your name under it in gold, and let them all look at where they put you."

The lamp hummed. At the long table a needle went on picking up beads, six to the stitch, because Poh Geok had the sense of a good employee and had gone deaf on purpose.

Mei Foong heard herself say, perfectly level, "You will do no such thing. It is not your photograph."

She could not have explained it to anybody then and did not try, and it was years before she wrote it down even in a letter that would never go anywhere. The place at the end of the back row was not where they had put her. It was where she had asked to be.

She had gone to the Bibik on the morning of the sitting, in the kitchen court, with a wet cloth still in her hand, and said that the amahs should be in the picture, since a house is its people and the frame would look empty at the ends. The Bibik had considered this from a great height and granted it as she granted rice. And then Mei Foong had walked out to the steps ahead of everyone and chosen, from all the standing room in the world, the four square feet directly behind a stool with her daughter on it — near, never inside; the architecture of her whole life given as one instruction to a photographer — and when the man went under his cloth and said hold still, she had looked down at the crown of that head, at the two whorls in the hair that nobody else in the household knew were there, and had held perfectly still for eleven seconds, and had been, for eleven seconds, standing behind her own child in a family photograph, in front of everyone, with the town's photographer for a witness.

It was the single happiest object of her life, and her daughter had looked straight at it and seen a servant put in her place, and had been sorry for her.

"Take it back to the press," said Mei Foong. "Tie the string as you found it. Your grandfather does not like his cupboards disarranged."

"But—"

"Mei Lan." Very quietly. "Take it back."

The girl took it back. She was a good child; she went out with her shoulders up and her ears red, and Mei Foong heard her sandals all the way to the corner.

Then she cleared the counter and turned the lamp down and went into the middle hall, and took the small slipper off the altar shelf, and the silk from under it, and knelt and worked the loose brick out of the left-hand lamp niche, and put them back into the lacquered box in the dark where the paper was, and set the box square to the shelf, and put the brick back, and pressed the join with her thumb until the dust of it lay right.

Nine years it had stood in the light. She had thought that was safe. She had thought she had earned the one thing.

*Dear husband. Your daughter is fourteen and cannot pass a locked door without trying the handle, which is your fault entirely. Today she found the photograph from your mother's last good year and told me she was sorry that they made me stand at the back. I said thank you. She meant it kindly. Nobody has ever wounded me so thoroughly while being kind, and I could not even bleed in front of her, because bleeding is a thing amahs do in the kitchen. I have put the little slipper back behind the brick. The house is tidier. — Your wife, who is running out of answers that are also true.*

---

They met in the shop after closing on a Sunday in April, with the shutters down and the lamp over the long table and nothing on it, for once, but four cups: the doctor, his wife, the schoolteacher from Ipoh, and the woman who owned the room.

It was Siew Ean who called it, being the only one with no blood in it and therefore the only one who could say a thing out loud without the ceiling coming down.

"She has asked her father where she was born, and she has asked her mother, and she got two answers. Last Thursday she asked me — me, the bookkeeper — whether the Lims kept a bill from any hospital in nineteen forty-two, because she has worked out that a doctor's wife does not deliver on a rubber estate without paperwork. She is fourteen. In two years somebody will hand her a form to fill in. So. Four people know a thing and there is no fifth. When does she hear it, and from whom?"

Bee Nio's cup went down too hard. "Never."

"That is a position," said Siew Ean. "Say the rest of it."

"Never. She is my daughter. Not on paper — in the morning, when she is sick, when she is disappointed, at the school gate for eleven years." Bee Nio's chin was up and her eyes were streaming and her voice did not shake at all; and Mei Foong, watching her, thought that whatever else the war had made of this woman, it had made her brave in a room. "I know what I hold and how I came to hold it, and I have not had one clean night since 'forty-three. But if you tell her, you do not give her a mother. You take away the one she has and hand her two strangers with a story. She will hate me. And she will be right, and she will still hate me, and she will have nobody."

"At twenty-one," said Dr. Lim. He had a doctor's way of laying a thing on the table like an instrument. "At twenty-one she is nobody's business but her own. Her schooling would be finished. If it were done then, with the paper, by the three of us together—"

"The paper," said Mei Foong.

"Yes."

"Cheng Hoe. Say what the paper says."

He looked at his hands. He was fifty-one and had had one warning from his heart and had been told to give up the night rounds, and had given up half of them.

"It says the mother's name," he said. "The father's column is empty."

"It is empty," said Mei Foong, "because in November of nineteen forty-two I could not write his name in it without hanging four people, and it will be empty when they burn it with me. So hear the whole shape of the thing, since we are honest in a locked shop for once. Tell her at twenty-one, and what have you told her? That her mother is the amah. That is the small half. The moment she has it, she is a clever girl with a date and a house and two ears, and she will do the sum every one of us has done, and inside a month she will arrive at whose child she is. Then she is not the doctor's daughter with a strange story. She is the dead heir's daughter, and there is an estate, and there is her grandfather, and there are cousins in Muar who count on their fingers, and there is a town that has spat at one closed door for eleven years and would enjoy something new." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the wood. "I have spent fifteen years making that girl unremarkable. It is the only thing I have ever manufactured."

"So never," said Bee Nio.

"I did not say never."

"Then what?"

And Mei Foong, who had an answer for the price of gold thread and the weight of a nib and the hour at which a bride should be measured, sat in her own shop with none, and let the room see it, which she had done perhaps four times in thirty years.

"When she asks me straight," she said at last. "Not the family. Me, in this room, with the door shut. If she ever comes and puts it to me straight, I will not lie to her a second time. That is all I have. I cannot promise you a year. I can promise you I am not going to look at my own child and say no twice."

Nobody agreed to anything. That was the whole of the council: four people round a lamp discovering that each had a different door, and every door was locked from a different side. Siew Ean walked home at ten. Bee Nio wept in the five-foot way for four minutes, precisely, then repaired her face at the shop's mirror and went up the street with her back straight; and Mei Foong watched her go and thought, not for the first time, that the two of them together made one whole woman, and neither was allowed to say so.

It did not matter. It was settled eleven days later by a man who had not been in the room and did not know there had been a room.

The Towkay called the family to the front hall on a Thursday evening and announced, in the manner of a man reading a resolution to a board, that the firm's registered office would move to Kuala Lumpur before the year's end. There was a house arranged off the Ipoh road. The country was going to have its own government and its own capital, and business follows government the way water follows a drain, and any man who thought the rubber trade would still be run out of a river town in ten years was welcome to stay in Malacca and find out. Dr. Lim's practice would be transferred and a specialist found for his heart. And his granddaughter, who was fourteen and had a mind, would finish her schooling at a proper convent in the capital, where the examinations were marked and the girls went on to university.

"Modern country," he said. "Modern capital."

Mei Lan made noise for two days and was thrilled underneath it by the end of the first, because she was fourteen and it was nineteen fifty-seven and a new city was being written about in every paper she read.

He came to the shop himself the following week, which he had done twice in nine years. He stood in the middle of the floor with his hat in his hand and looked at the lasts on the wall, and the trays, and the two apprentices, who had gone rigid; and at last at the woman behind the counter, who was his wife's sister's daughter, and to whom in eight years he had never once said so out loud.

"The shop cannot move," he said. "You have brides coming from three states to this street. A shop is where it stands."

"Yes."

He turned the hat by its brim. "The house is yours. The deed is clean; I made it clean. There will be no argument out of Kuala Lumpur about it, ever, and if there is, you will write to me."

"Yes."

He looked at the counter, at the ledger, at the green pen lying on its blotting paper, and something crossed the old face and did not stay.

"You will not follow," he said.

It was not a question and it was not permission. It was an entry, closed and ruled under. And Mei Foong, who understood ledgers better than anybody in Malacca, understood that the man was doing the only decent thing available to someone who knew half a secret: taking his granddaughter three hours up the road, away from a woman who kept looking at her too long, before the town noticed what he had noticed and could not unnotice.

"No," she said. "I will not follow."

What Mei Lan was told, by her mother, in the mansion, was that Auntie Foong had her own business now and could not be expected to leave it. What a girl of fourteen heard in that sentence, for the rest of her life, was that the family had let the amah go.

---

The street began putting flags up a fortnight early, and by the last week of August there was hardly a shutter on Heeren Street without something nailed to it; the tailors ran out of blue cloth, and a man came up from Bunga Raya selling paper flags on sticks at ten cents and had none left by four o'clock. The Tan lorries stood loaded under a tarpaulin in the mansion yard. They would go on the third of September.

On the night of the thirtieth Mei Lan came down the street with a pillow under her arm and no explanation, and Mei Foong opened the shutter and let her in and did not ask for one.

They kept the wireless on the counter. Half the street was in the five-foot way outside, and Raju's son had carried his set out to the step and turned it up, so that the two sets a few yards apart went slightly out of step and everything arrived twice. Just before midnight the town went quiet in a way Mei Foong had heard only once before, in a different August, in a front hall, with a man's hand on a walnut cabinet.

They could not see it, three hours south of it. They heard it. They heard a crowd in a padang in Kuala Lumpur go silent for the lowering; they heard God Save the Queen played through for the last time over a colony that had been begun, a hundred and seventy years before, out of that very town at the mouth of that very river; they heard the silence in the dark while the flag came down. And then they heard the anthem of a country, and eleven stripes and an eleven-pointed star went up a pole in the dark, for eleven states, one of them this one.

Outside, Heeren Street took the top off itself. Firecrackers went the whole length of the terrace; somebody's uncle had rockets from god knew where, and they cracked over the roofs and lit the five-foot way blue-white and pink; the ice factory sounded its siren; a lion dance troupe with no lion ran up the middle of the road banging a drum. Old Toh the goldsmith, a man of Fukien province, stood in the road outside his own shop with a paper flag in each hand and let the tears go wherever they liked.

Mei Lan was on the step screaming with the rest of them at half past twelve, hoarse at one, sitting down at half past. At two she was on the shop floor with her back against the counter and the pillow behind her head, among the trays and the paper packets and the day's sweepings of beads, a flag still in her hand, arguing about the proclamation in the morning and whether the word would be shouted seven times as the papers said, and telling Mei Foong that in the capital they would have a stadium for it, an actual stadium, with a roof.

"You will see it. You will be there in a fortnight."

"You should come up for the parade next year. You could come for a week."

"Somebody has to mind the shop."

"You always say that." A jaw-cracking yawn. "Auntie Foong. When I am grown up and have my own money, I am going to—"

Mei Foong waited a while, then leaned over the counter and looked. The girl was asleep, mouth open, flag slack in her fingers, one foot in a sandal and one out: fourteen years old on the floor of a bead shop, three days from a modern capital, in a country eleven hours old.

She did not move her. She fetched the blanket from behind the door and put it over her, and shifted the tray of jade greens out of the way of her elbow, and turned the lamp down to nothing, and sat on the stool with the shutter half up while the street burned itself out in front of her.

At six the sky over the shophouses went grey, then the colour of the inside of a shell. Two doors down and across, on the roof of the school, two men in singlets got a pole up and argued a long time about the guys before they ran the new flag out; and it went up crooked, and came down, and went up again straight, and the light caught it; and there was nobody in the road to see it but a woman in a doorway and a dog.

Mei Foong went to the counter and took the green pen off its blotting paper and filled it, and wrote standing up, because her daughter was asleep in the only chair-height space in the room and she was not going to move her for a letter.

*Dear husband. The country got its name back tonight. I am still waiting to be introduced. There were fireworks the whole length of the street, and old Toh cried in the road, and the school has a new flag on it, crooked at first and straight now, eleven points for eleven states, and not one of the eleven keeps a column on any paper anywhere for what I am. Our daughter is asleep on my shop floor with a flag in her hand. In three days they take her to the capital, which your father says is modern, and which will not know her, that being the point of it. I am sending nothing of yours with her. She has your brow and your handwriting and your bad habit of arriving at a table too fast, and nobody up there will know enough to say so; and she will grow up straight in a free country and be a nuisance to somebody, which is the whole of what I asked for. She says she will come back for the parade. They all say that. — Your wife, who kept the accounts through two flags and will keep them through this one.*

She folded it small and tied it into the year's bundle with red thread, and put the bundle in the tin, and put the lid on, and set the tin back in the chest under the window while the light came up on the loudest morning Malaya had ever had.

Then she went down and rolled the shutter the rest of the way up, and stood in her own doorway, in her own house, in a country three hours new, with her daughter asleep at her feet, and waited for the first customer of the first day.
