# The Question

On the last Wednesday of July the whole street stood in the five-foot way outside Raju's kopitiam to hear the results come in, and Mei Lan, who was twelve and would not be moved, kept the tally in chalk on the back of the shop's shutter, because the wireless said the numbers faster than the men could hold them. Fifty-one, she wrote at last, and under it, one. Then she looked at the two figures the way her father used to look at a column that had come out clean, and said, "That is not an election. That is a landslide."

"Where does she get it," said Raju from the coffee shop step.

"School," said Mei Foong from her doorway, a bride's slipper still in her hand. It was the answer she always gave and it had the advantage of being true.

Behind her the shop smelled as it had since 'forty-nine, of starch and beeswax and camphor, and two apprentices worked on under the shaded lamps, because a Chief Minister may come and go but a wedding has a date. The ledger lay squared at the counter's end, and beside it, on its folded square of blotting paper, the pen.

Malaya had voted, and the wireless called all of it by a word that had until recently belonged to the British. Malayan. Mei Foong heard it tried on and taken off a dozen times a day in the kopitiam, like a coat in a shop with no mirror. A clerk from the land office said the word meant nothing until it meant everybody, got shouted at from four directions and drank his coffee looking pleased; and Mei Foong, going past, thought: he sounds like a merbok. She thought it without pain now. That was one of the things the years had done.

At the end of that week Mei Lan came in after school with an exercise book and a face full of business, and Mei Foong knew before it was open that something was coming, because the girl had her father's way of arriving fast at a table to get a thing past the sentries.

"Civics. We have to make a family tree — Sister says a citizen should know where she comes from." She flattened the book on the beads, which was not allowed, and neither of them mentioned it. "Grandfather and Grandmother-in-heaven I have done, and Papa's side is easy because Papa writes everything down. But Sister says three generations. Auntie Foong, you were in that house before I was born."

Mei Foong put down the slipper.

"I was the amah's girl," she said. "I carried trays. Ask your mother."

"I asked Mother. She said do the Lim side, it is neater." Mei Lan pushed the hair off her forehead with the back of her wrist — the exact movement — and Mei Foong looked at the ledger before she looked back. "Nobody in this family answers a question in a straight line. It is like buying fish."

"Then you had better learn to buy fish," said Mei Foong.

---

The questions did not stop, because Mei Lan was not a girl who stopped; she was a girl who filed. There were three, and within a year Mei Foong could recite them in her sleep, and did, and woke with her heart going.

The first was the slipper: one child's slipper the length of a palm on its square of faded silk on the altar shelf, peony and phoenix worked so small the flower was no bigger than a five-sen coin, its sole white and unmarked. She had set it there in her first month of owning a roof, where nobody could ask her by what right — the one visible thing she had allowed herself in an otherwise invisible life. Mei Lan at thirteen stopped in front of it and stayed.

"Whose foot?"

"Mine."

"It is too small for a baby's second week and too fine for a village."

"A baby's slipper is beaded before the foot is measured. The mother guesses. That is why it never fitted and never will." Mei Foong did not look up. "Move. You are in my light."

"Where is the other one?"

"There never was another one." Which was true, and the whole of the truth that any living person could safely be given, and she gave it in the voice she used for the price of gold thread.

The second was the pen: green, mottled like river water under trees, its gold worn and unfaded, lying on blotting paper at the ledger's edge in a shop where every other tool belonged to whichever hand was nearest. Mei Lan had passed it a thousand times without seeing it. At thirteen she picked it up and read the English on the barrel, then the school, then the year.

"This is Uncle Boon Siew's prize pen. Grandfather has the certificate in the hall — nineteen thirty-eight, English composition. Why is my dead uncle's prize pen in your shop?"

"Because your grandfather's account room was dismantled in the war and I was the one keeping the accounts," said Mei Foong, "and a pen goes with a ledger." She held out her hand, palm up, took it back, and set it down squared to the corners, no more carefully than a nib.

The third she could not touch at all, because it stood in the ancestral hall of the mansion, where she had no standing to answer anything. The Bibik had died in 'forty-eight, and when the hall was set right again there were two new tablets where there should have been one. Neo Guat Eng, in gold, with her dates and her titles. Beside it, a hand shorter, plain, no dates at all: Neo Guat Kim. The Towkay had ordered it himself in the week after the burial and had never explained it to anybody, including the daughter of the house.

"Grandfather. Who is Neo Guat Kim?"

"Your grandmother had a sister."

"When did she die? There are no dates."

"Before you were born."

"That is not a date, Grandfather."

"Then it is what you have got," said Tan Hock Lye, seventy-one, folding his newspaper along its crease and going out to the car he still could not drive.

That evening Mei Lan stood in the shop doorway with her satchel still on. "Auntie Foong. Nobody in that house has a face. There is no photograph of Uncle Boon Siew anywhere — a tablet, a certificate for a pen, a school cricket group where Papa says he is third from the left, except third from the left is a smudge. Every family on this street has a big brown photograph of the son. Why haven't we?"

"Because in nineteen forty-two we burned everything with a name on it," said Mei Foong. "Books, letters, photographs, anything English, anything a soldier could ask a question about. Nobody was thinking about faces." She threaded a bead, and it took her two tries, and she made sure both were slow ones. "We were thinking about the morning."

Mei Lan sat down on the stool and was quiet a while, which was rare enough to be weather. "That is the first answer anybody has given me in a year," she said, "with a reason in it."

---

In February of 'fifty-six the town filled up in a way Mei Foong had seen only twice before, and neither of the other times had been happy. They came in from Jasin and Alor Gajah and the coast road, and the padang at Bandar Hilir went black from the sea wall to the road. The men had come home from London with the thing in their hands; and it was the Tunku's voice that came over the loudspeakers, and the date was given out in Malacca, of all the towns in Malaya, out of an old courtesy to the oldest town.

The thirty-first of August, nineteen fifty-seven.

The sound the padang made was not a cheer. It was the sound a house makes when a door swollen shut for years comes open at once and everyone pushing falls forward together. Around her, men she had watched bow at a bridge for three years and seven months were shouting a word with both fists in the air. A Malay schoolmaster in a songkok had his hand on the shoulder of a Tamil boy from the ice factory, and neither appeared to have noticed, and neither took it away.

And Mei Lan turned round on the sea wall with her sandals in her hand and shouted down over the noise, her whole face open: "Auntie Foong! It has a date! It has an actual date!"

That was the year the cages opened. Mei Foong, who had spent her life being told which room she might stand in, watched a country be told it would soon be allowed to say its own name, and caught herself doing a sum she had no business doing. Eighteen months. Then twelve. Then the whole of it counted down in the newspapers like a wedding.

It was also the year Mei Lan began, without knowing it, to walk straight at the middle of her mother's life. She had drawn the family tree and been given a mark for it, and now she wanted its dates to agree, having discovered in Form Two that dates are the bones of the thing. She asked her father where she had been born and was told the country house at Ayer Keroh; she asked her mother and was told the town; and she brought the difference to the shop, because from the age of six the girl had chosen the amah's counter as the place where things got answered.

"Auntie Foong. You were there in the war. Where was I born?"

The bead went on. Six to the stitch. Mei Foong watched her own hands do it and was distantly proud of them.

"I was in town. I heard about you two days late, from a lorry driver, and I put down what I was carrying." She looked up and let the girl see her, because a face that gives nothing at all is itself an answer. "That is the whole of what the amah knows. Now hand me the jade greens and stop interrogating your father. His heart gave him trouble two years ago, and every one of these questions costs him a night's sleep."

Mei Lan flushed to her ears, because she was fourteen and not cruel. But she handed over the greens, and said to the counter, low and mulish:

"Somebody in this family is lying to me. I do not mind being lied to about money. I mind being lied to about me."

---

The photograph came out of the mansion in October, in the ordinary way that dangerous things do: in a schoolbag, on a Tuesday, with a mango.

Mei Lan had been set to clear the camphor press, on the theory, popular among adults that year, that a girl with a grievance should be given cupboards. Under the Bibik's kebaya lengths she found a parcel of studio prints tied in string, and came down Heeren Street at a run with one of them held in both hands like a full bowl.

"Look. Look what I found. It is all of us."

She put it on the clean cloth, which was correct, and Mei Foong looked down and the shop went a long way off.

She had not seen it in twelve years. The photographer had come in 'forty-four because the Bibik had decided the house should be recorded while it still had people in it, and he had arranged the household on the front steps by rank: the Towkay and the Bibik centre in the two blackwood chairs, Dr. Lim and Bee Nio flanking, the two Tan aunts down from Muar seated at the ends; behind them the cousins and the clerks; the children on stools in front — and in the middle of the children, in a white pinafore, gripping her stool with both hands and glaring at the lens as though it had insulted her, a girl of not quite two.

And at the very end of the back row, half a step out of the line, in the white blouse and black trousers of the household's amahs, a young woman looking not at the camera but slightly down, at the head of the child seated in front of her.

Mei Lan put her finger on the figure. "That is you."

"That is me."

"Auntie Foong." And her voice changed, and the change was what did the damage, because it was warm — a fourteen-year-old's clean, generous indignation, the exact quality of feeling her father had once carried to a kitchen table with a cast-off primer under his arm. "They made you stand. Look where they put you, right at the end, behind everybody, like furniture. And you kept those accounts. Papa says you saved the firm twice—"

"Three times," said Mei Foong. "Twice is your father being polite."

"Three times! And they sat the aunts from Muar down in front and put you at the edge with the amahs." She looked at the print with her jaw set, then up, blazing. "Poor Auntie Foong. When I am older I am going to have this enlarged and hung in the front hall of that house, with your name under it in gold, and let them all look at where they put you."

At the long table a needle went on picking up beads, six to the stitch; the apprentice had the sense of a good employee and had gone deaf on purpose.

Mei Foong heard herself say, perfectly level, "You will do no such thing. It is not your photograph."

She could not have explained it to anybody and did not try. The place at the end of the back row was not where they had put her. It was where she had asked to be.

She had gone to the Bibik on the morning of the sitting and said the amahs should be in the picture, since a house is its people and the frame would look empty at the ends; and the Bibik had granted it as she granted rice. Then Mei Foong had walked out to the steps ahead of everyone and chosen, from all the standing room in the world, the four square feet directly behind a stool with her daughter on it — near, never inside; the architecture of her whole life given as one instruction to a photographer. When the man went under his cloth and said hold still, she had looked down at the crown of that head, at the two whorls in the hair nobody else in the household knew were there, and held still for eleven seconds; and had been, for eleven seconds, standing behind her own child in a family photograph, in front of everyone.

It was the happiest object of her life, and her daughter had looked straight at it and seen a servant put in her place, and been sorry for her.

"Take it back to the press," said Mei Foong, "and tie the string as you found it."

"But—"

"Mei Lan." Very quietly. "Take it back."

The girl took it back. She went out with her shoulders up and her ears red, and Mei Foong heard her sandals all the way to the corner. Then she turned the lamp down and went into the middle hall and took the small slipper off the altar shelf, and the silk from under it, and worked the loose brick out of the left-hand lamp niche, and put them back in the lacquered box in the dark where the paper was, and pressed the brick home until the dust of the join lay right.

Nine years it had stood in the light. She had thought that was safe. She had thought she had earned the one thing.

---

They met in the shop after closing on a Sunday in April, shutters down, four cups under the lamp: the doctor, his wife, the schoolteacher from Ipoh, and the woman who owned the room. It was Siew Ean who called it, having no blood in the matter and therefore able to say things aloud.

"Last Thursday she asked me — me, the bookkeeper — whether the Lims kept a hospital bill from nineteen forty-two, having worked out that a doctor's wife does not deliver on an estate without paperwork. She is fourteen. Four people know a thing and there is no fifth. When does she hear it, and from whom?"

Bee Nio's cup went down too hard. "Never. She is my daughter. Not on paper — in the morning, when she is sick, at the school gate for eleven years." Her chin was up and her eyes were streaming. "Tell her, and you do not give her a mother. You take away the one she has and hand her two strangers with a story. She will hate me, and she will be right, and she will still hate me, and she will have nobody."

"At twenty-one," said Dr. Lim, laying it on the table like an instrument. "Or the week she marries, when a family asks for her particulars and she must answer for herself. Not before, and not by one of us alone."

"The paper," said Mei Foong. "Cheng Hoe. Say what the paper says."

He looked at his hands. "It says the mother's name. The father's column is empty."

"It is empty because in 'forty-two I could not write his name in it without hanging four people, and it will be empty when they burn it with me. So: tell her at twenty-one, and what have you told her? That her mother is the amah. That is the small half. She is a clever girl with a date and a house and two ears; inside a month she will arrive at whose child she is — and then she is not the doctor's daughter with a strange story, she is the dead heir's daughter, and there is an estate, and cousins in Muar who count on their fingers." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the wood. "I have spent fifteen years making that girl unremarkable. It is the only thing I ever made."

"So never," said Bee Nio.

"I did not say never. When she asks me straight — not the family; me, in this room, with the door shut — I will not lie to her a second time. That is all I have. I cannot promise you a year. I can promise you I will not look at my own child and say no twice."

Nobody agreed to anything. That was the whole of the council: four people round a lamp, each with a different door, every door locked from a different side. It was settled eleven days later by a man who had not been in the room and did not know there had been a room.

The Towkay called the family to the front hall on a Thursday evening and announced, in the manner of a man reading a resolution to a board, that the firm would move to Kuala Lumpur before the year's end, to a house off the Ipoh road. The country was going to have its own government and its own capital; business follows government the way water follows a drain. Dr. Lim's practice would be transferred, and a specialist found for his heart. And his granddaughter, who was fourteen and had a mind, would finish school in the capital.

"Modern country," he said. "Modern capital."

Mei Lan made noise for two days and was thrilled underneath it by the end of the first.

He came to the shop himself the following week, which he had done twice in nine years, and stood with his hat in his hand, looking at the lasts on the wall and the two apprentices, who had gone rigid, and at last at the woman behind the counter — his wife's sister's daughter, to whom he had never once said so out loud.

"The shop cannot move. A shop is where it stands." He turned the hat by its brim. "The house is yours. The deed is clean; I made it clean, and there will be no argument out of Kuala Lumpur about it, ever."

"Yes."

He looked at the counter, at the ledger, at the green pen on its blotting paper, and something crossed the old face and did not stay.

"You will not follow," he said.

It was not a question and it was not permission; it was an entry, closed and ruled under. And Mei Foong understood it: the only decent thing available to a man who knew half a secret — taking his granddaughter three hours up the road, away from a woman who looked at her too long, before the town noticed what he had noticed.

"No," she said. "I will not follow."

What Mei Lan was told, by her mother, in the mansion, was that Auntie Foong had her own business now and could not be expected to leave it. What a girl of fourteen heard in that sentence, for the rest of her life, was that the family had let the amah go.

---

By the last week of August there was hardly a shutter on Heeren Street without something nailed to it. The Tan lorries stood loaded in the mansion yard; they would go on the third of September.

On the night of the thirtieth Mei Lan came down the street with a pillow under her arm and no explanation, and Mei Foong opened the shutter and let her in and did not ask for one.

They kept the wireless on the counter. Just before midnight the town went quiet in a way Mei Foong had heard only once before, in a different August, in a front hall, with a man's hand on a walnut cabinet. They could not see it, three hours south of it. They heard it: God Save the Queen played through for the last time over a town that had flown a sultan's flag, and Portugal's, and Holland's, and this one; then the anthem of a country, and eleven stripes and an eleven-pointed star going up a pole for eleven states, one of them this one.

Outside, Heeren Street took the top off itself: firecrackers the whole length of the terrace, rockets lighting the five-foot way blue-white and pink. Old Toh the goldsmith, who had said all his life that he was a man of Fukien province who happened to be in Malacca, stood in the road with a paper flag in each hand and let the tears go where they liked.

Mei Lan was on the step screaming with the rest at half past twelve, hoarse at one, sitting down at half past. At two she was on the floor with her back against the counter and the pillow behind her head, among the trays and the day's sweepings of beads, telling Mei Foong that in the capital they would have a stadium for it, with a roof.

"You will see it. You will be there in a fortnight."

"You should come up for the parade next year."

"Somebody has to mind the shop."

"You always say that." A jaw-cracking yawn. "Auntie Foong. When I am grown up and have my own money, I am going to—"

Mei Foong waited, then leaned over the counter and looked. The girl was asleep, mouth open, the flag slack in her fingers, one foot in a sandal and one out: fourteen years old on the floor of a bead shop, three days from a modern capital, in a country two hours old.

She did not move her. She put the blanket from behind the door over her, shifted the tray of jade greens away from her elbow, and sat on the stool with the shutter half up while the street burned itself out.

At six the sky went the colour of the inside of a shell. Across the road, on the roof of the school, two men in singlets got a pole up and ran the new flag out; and it went up crooked, and came down, and went up again straight, and the light caught it; and there was nobody in the road to see it but a woman in a doorway and a dog.

Mei Foong took the green pen off its blotting paper and filled it, and wrote standing, her daughter being asleep in the only chair-height space in the room.

*Dear husband. The country got its name back tonight. I am still waiting to be introduced. Eleven points on the new flag for eleven states, and not one of the eleven keeps a column on any paper anywhere for what I am. Our daughter is asleep on my shop floor with a flag in her hand. In three days they take her to the capital, which will not know her, that being the point of it. She has your brow and your handwriting and your bad habit of arriving at a table too fast, and nobody up there will know enough to say so; and she will grow up straight in a free country and be a nuisance to somebody, which is the whole of what I asked for. She says she will come back for the parade. They all say that. — Your wife, who kept the accounts through two flags and will keep them through this one.*

She folded it small, tied it into the year's bundle with red thread, put the bundle in the tin, and set the tin back in the chest under the window. Then she rolled the shutter the rest of the way up and stood in her own doorway, in her own house, in a country six hours old, with her daughter asleep at her feet, and waited for the first customer of the first day.
