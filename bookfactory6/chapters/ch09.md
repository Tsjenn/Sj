# The Photograph

The agent was due at nine on Saturday morning, and at twenty to nine Su-Lin stood in the airwell of the shophouse with her phone in her hand, composing the message that would cancel him. The Nokia's predictive text kept offering *sell* when she wanted *see*, which she chose not to take as an omen, and after three attempts she settled on POSTPONING VALUATION. WILL CALL MONDAY, sent it, and felt the weekend change shape around her, the way a site changes when the drawings are set aside and you finally just look at the building.

She had slept in the hotel off Jonker Street for exactly one night, as booked, and had lain awake most of it thinking about the tin. The topmost bundle had defeated her within twenty minutes on Friday evening: she had untied the knot, unfolded the first letter with the care of someone defusing something, and met a wall of small upright characters of which she could confidently read perhaps one in six. Her grandmother's Saturday-morning workbooks had got her as far as Standard Five, and Standard Five, it turned out, did not survive contact with a disciplined adult hand from before the war. She had retied the thread — badly; her knot beside the dead woman's knots looked like a child's shoelace — and put the letter back, and lain in the hotel dark being annoyed at her eleven-year-old self.

So. If she could not yet read the house's papers, she would read the house. That, at least, she was qualified for.

She bought graph paper and a clutch of pencils from a stationery shop near the bridge, and a litre of drinking water, and a steel tape from a hardware shop where the proprietor sold it to her without once ceasing his conversation with a man about fighting fish. By half past nine she was working the ground floor in her site clothes, tape singing out and snapping back, and the shophouse began to assemble itself on paper the way buildings did for her and people didn't.

Fifteen feet, one inch across the front — she had guessed fifteen from the car and took the inch as a small politeness from the dead. But the depth kept going: shop, then the middle hall with its altar and its wall of photographs, then the first airwell, then a back hall, a second, smaller airwell green with fern, then kitchen, then a store-room she hadn't opened on Friday, the rooms running back from the street in single file like a sentence being remembered one clause at a time. A hundred and eleven feet from shopfront to the back wall. Land taxed on frontage, so the house had done its living lengthwise, in the dark, which was either a metaphor or just Dutch-era tax policy, depending on how much sleep you'd had.

It was in the store-room that she found the flood.

The room had been the working back-of-shop once — she recognized the grammar of it, deep shelves, a long table scarred with knife-cuts, hooks in the beam overhead. On the highest shelves sat ledgers and box files, and when she lifted one down, its bottom third was stiff and tide-marked, the pages welded into a single brown wafer that broke like biscuit. Every ledger on the lower shelves was the same: a clean upper body and ruined feet, and on the wall itself, when she looked, a faint horizontal line of stain at the height of her knee, the signature of standing water. Old, old damage. She peeled two pages apart in the best-preserved book and found columns of figures in a hand she was coming to know, small, upright, disciplined, the ink blurred into grey clouds below the water line, and dates she could read because numbers were numbers: 1937. 1938. The house had drowned once, quietly, sixty-seven years before she arrived, and someone had dried the books and kept them anyway, ruined, in order, squared to the shelf edge. Nothing removed. Untidied.

She noted *water table? river? drainage* on her sketch and moved on, and the house handed her its second question at the back door.

There was a door to the back lane — there had to be; every house in the terrace would have one, the servants' and deliveries' door, the door night-soil carts had once served, the escape valve of the long floor plan. She found it behind the kitchen: a heavy plank door in a frame, and it had been closed for a very long time. Not locked. Closed. The bolts were painted into their sockets under layers of blue-green, the hinges furred solid, and someone had driven a rank of flat-headed nails through the leaf into the frame with a spacing so regular it looked like decoration, then painted over those too. Su-Lin stood looking at it for a while with her thumb rubbing one nail head. A door was the cheapest thing in the world to fix. This one had been not-fixed with great care, decades of paint deep. From the lane side, when she walked round the block to check, there was nothing to see at all: just wall, a rain-stained expanse of it, the doorway's outline barely legible under render, like the ghost of the shop sign out front. A house with one mouth sealed.

The third question she nearly walked past.

She was measuring the middle hall — the ancestral hall, she supposed it should be called, the altar wall with its brass and its fresh ash — cross-checking room depths against her running total, and the numbers refused to close. Eighteen inches refused to close. She measured twice more, then went along the side wall rapping her knuckles at intervals, an architect's habit older than her degree. Hollow, hollow, hollow, and then, in the bay to the left of the altar, dead. Not the dead of solid masonry; the flat, packed dead of infill. She fetched the torch and raked the wall with light and there it was, once you knew to look: a rectangle the size of a tea-tray, its plaster a shade smoother than its neighbours, its surface minutely proud of the wall's plane, the patch of a repair made by someone good but not invisible. On the right of the altar, symmetrical to it, the wall held a small arched niche with a lamp shelf, sooted with a century of wicks. On the left, where its twin should have been: the patch.

Houses on this street did not lose niches. Niches were where the house kept its small gods and its lamps. Somebody had bricked this one up and plastered over it and, judging by the soot gradient, had done it long enough ago that lamps had burned beside the scar for decades and nobody had thought it worth remarking.

Su-Lin drew the wall elevation on her graph paper, marked the patch with a neat rectangle, and wrote beside it a single question mark. Then she stood in front of it for a moment with her palm flat on the plaster, which was a thing she would have mocked a junior for doing on site, and went to find coffee.

---

The kopitiam — the old-style coffee shop — was two doors down towards the bridge, and it declared itself at thirty paces by smell: charcoal, kaya, the caramel edge of beans fried in margarine. It had marble-topped tables gone grey at the edges like old men's hair, a mirror advertising a beer nobody drank anymore, and a wall calendar from a hardware supplier showing a waterfall in Perak. A man of about fifty worked the coffee sock behind the counter with the wrist action of a lifetime. And at the corner table, in a short-sleeved shirt ironed to architecture, sat a very old man doing the crossword in the *Straits Times* with a pen, which told her most of what she needed to know about him before he said a word.

"Kopi-o," she said at the counter. "Kosong." Black, no sugar; the counter man's eyebrows delivered a short lecture on KL people, but he made it.

"You are the niece," the old man said, without looking up from the crossword. "Grand-niece. Something-niece. The one Devan gave the keys."

"Word travels."

"Girl, this street is eight feet wide. Word doesn't have to travel. It just leans." He put the pen down and looked at her, and his eyes were bright and amused in a face like a well-used leather bag. "Sit, sit. Raju. Eighty years old, so I am allowed to talk to strange women. This is my son's shop now. It was my shop when coffee cost ten cents, and my father's when it cost five. I remember everything," he said, with immense satisfaction. "Ask anybody. More than everything, some of them say, but they are jealous."

She sat. The kopi-o arrived, thick as engine oil, in a cup with a green rim, and Uncle Raju — he installed the *uncle* himself within two minutes, as a fact of geography rather than an invitation — talked. He talked the way the rain came here, in an unhurried settled way that you gave up resisting: about Devan's father, about the terrace's roofs, about the trishaw men who now wore fairy lights and played love songs on loudspeakers for the tourists, *aiyo*, when he was a boy a trishaw man would be ashamed. About the weekend night market up Jonker Street — three years old now, brought the crowds, brought the money-changers, brought people from KL to buy up dying shophouses and stuff them with air-conditioning. He said *your one* — meaning her house — the way you'd say *your sister*.

"You knew her," Su-Lin said. "Miss Ho."

"Sister Foong." He turned his cup a half-turn on its saucer. "Sixty years, two doors apart. She drank tea, not coffee, but she bought her tin milk from us because my father under-charged her once in 1948 and she would not let the debt die." He smiled at something a long way off. "Best hands on this street. Brides came from Muar, from Seremban. Kasut manek." He looked at her sideways, sly as a boy. "You found the beads, lah. Everyone wants to know what you found. The street has been discussing your inheritance for two weeks. Current opinion is gold under the floor."

"Ledgers," she said. "Mostly what I've found is ledgers."

"Ha. Gold under the floor was better."

She had brought the photograph. She had not fully admitted to herself that she was bringing it, but it had come along in its stiff paper folder, and there was a moment — his cup empty, the crossword abandoned, the son wiping the far counter — when the table seemed to be waiting for it. She laid it down, folded side down, the young man face up in the thirties studio light with his hand on the table edge and his almost-smile.

"Uncle. Do you know who—"

"Tan Boon Siew." No pause at all. Not a heartbeat. His whole face had changed, the slyness gone out of it like water through a grating. "The old Towkay's son. Died in the war."

The Towkay — the big boss, the head of the family firm — she knew that much vocabulary. "The Tans from the mansion," she said. "Up the street. My family."

"Your family." He said it carefully, as if setting the words down on the marble without letting them click. "Tan Hock Lye's boy. Rubber and tin shares and two lorries and a Bentley motorcar the old man never learned to drive; it went up and down this street twenty miles an hour with a syce in front and the Towkay in back like a temple deity on procession day." His finger, big-knuckled, hovered over the young man's face and did not touch it. "This one used to sit where you are sitting. Schoolboy. High School badge. He would buy the coffee for the clerks, all of them, and argue with my father about whether Malaya could feed itself. Nineteen years old, arguing rice yields with a coffee-shop man, both of them enjoying it, *wah*." The finger withdrew. "The Japanese took him in the screening. Nineteen forty-two. He did not come back. That house burned joss for him until they sold it."

Su-Lin turned her cup a half-turn, mirroring him without meaning to. "Uncle — it's a wedding photograph."

"No."

"There's a bride. Her hand — you can see he's holding—"

"Girl." He said it gently, the way you correct a child's homework. "I am telling you the street's own knowledge. There was no wedding, girl. He died betrothed to an Ipoh Khoo. Tin money, big family. The match was fixed the year before the war came — the betrothal gifts went up in a motorcar, the whole street watched. Then the war postponed the wedding, and then the war ate the boy, and the Khoo girl — " he opened one hand, releasing something invisible " — went back to being an Ipoh Khoo. No wedding. Not in the mansion, not in any temple in Malacca. A thing like that, in that family? The kueh alone would have taken a month. We would have eaten for a week, and I would remember the menu." He looked at the photograph a while longer, and some of the slyness came back into his face, but changed, quieter. "Where did this come from?"

"The house. Her house."

"Mm," said Uncle Raju, and turned the photograph ninety degrees with one finger, and studied the young man's face, and said nothing at all, which from a man who remembered more than everything was its own kind of sentence. Then he tapped the mount's lower corner, where a name was blind-stamped into the card in flaking gilt. "Chan Brothers. The studio was on Jonker Street, where the shoe shop is now. Everybody's face on this street went through Chan's, weddings, funerals, New Year." He slid the photograph back to her, squaring it to the table edge first, precisely, the habit of a man who had handled other people's precious things across a counter for sixty years. "Finish your coffee. And girl — when you find out who was standing in the folded part, come and tell an old man before you tell your family. I will trade you the parts of the story I am too polite to guess in daylight."

---

She walked back the two doors with the folder under her arm and the kopi-o humming in her blood, and the contradiction walked with her, keeping step. *There was no wedding.* And in her folder, in studio light, a wedding: lilies in the vase, the high collar, the held hand, the fold. Either the street's memory was wrong — the street that was eight feet wide and leaned — or the photograph was lying, and she had spent enough of her life with drawings and as-builts to know which she trusted less. When the record and the site disagree, you go back to the site.

Inside, she cleared the shop counter's glass top, laid the photograph flat in the full daylight from the doorway, and looked at it the way she looked at a survey: no story, just information.

And the daylight gave her what the rainy dusk on Friday had not. The old fold did not run true. Whoever had made it — pressed it flat with a thumbnail sixty years ago and pressed it again with decades of tin lid — had folded from the top, and the crease drifted as it descended, so that at the very bottom edge of the card a narrow wedge of the hidden image survived, a half-inch of it, no more, easy to read as shadow unless you were the kind of person who checked. Su-Lin bent close, then went and dug her scale rule with its little magnifier out of her site bag, and bent close again.

The hem of a kebaya, embroidered. Two feet, side by side, neat as type, in beaded slippers.

The magnifier trembled very slightly, which she noted with professional disapproval, and steadied. The slippers were worked in an intricate repeating figure, and small as the image was, grey as the studio light rendered everything, the pattern's logic came through the way pattern always did: the big open bloom, the sweep of tail feathers curling to meet it. Peony and phoenix.

She was up the stairs before she had decided to be. The tin. The indigo cloth. The slipper — the left slipper, heavier than it looked — down to the counter and laid beside the photograph, and she stood with her palms flat on the glass, looking from beadwork to image, image to beadwork, rust and gold and jade against studio grey, doing what she was trained to do, which was to compare a detail against a drawing and certify a match.

The bloom sat the same distance above the toe. The tail feathers made the same turn.

The bride folded out of the photograph was wearing this slipper. This one, or its lost right-foot twin. On her feet, at her wedding, at Chan Brothers on Jonker Street, holding the hand of Tan Boon Siew, who had died in the war, betrothed to an Ipoh Khoo, and had never had a wedding — the street would have eaten for a week.

"Then who are you?" Su-Lin asked the fold, quietly, in the empty shop.

The phone rang.

---

It was her mother, of course; the universe had comic timing where her mother was concerned. Su-Lin sat down on the beader's stool behind the counter and answered on the fourth ring, which in her family was a form of standing up straight.

"You're still there," her mother said. Not a question. Behind her voice was the sound of the KL house, a kettle, the maid's radio somewhere. "Auntie Poh saw your car was gone from your block. Still in Malacca, on a Saturday, and Jason says you postponed the valuation man."

"I postponed the valuation man."

"Su-Lin." Her mother had a particular voice, the one she used when a table was badly set, and it came down the line now, patient as a dripping tap. "Listen. A developer called your Uncle Beng on Thursday. Thursday! The probate ink is not even dry. A KL company — they are doing those boutique hotels, they've bought three on that street already, they do the whole thing up, air-con, rain showers, they pay in cash and they pay for facing units what nobody in Malacca will ever pay again. Beng says they mentioned a number." She mentioned the number. It was, Su-Lin had to concede, a serious number. "For a dead servant's shophouse. This is not a moment, it is a *window*. You get the valuation, we sign with the agent, the family settles it nicely — Ah Ma agrees, by the way. She said sell it. She said it twice."

Su-Lin looked at the counter. The slipper and the photograph lay side by side on the glass, over the trays of beads sorted rust to rose, and it occurred to her that she was, at this moment, the only person alive who knew that these two objects agreed with each other.

"Did Ah Ma say anything else?"

"What else is there to say? She said sell." A pause, and her mother's voice tacked, casual in the way that meant not casual. "Why? What did she say to *you*?"

*I have never owned a beaded slipper.* The sentence arrived flat and complete, a door meeting its frame, answering a question nobody had asked.

"Nothing," Su-Lin said. "Valuation Monday. I'm staying the weekend — there's a full house of contents to inventory, and if you sell to developers with the contents in place they'll skip a lorry and burn the lot." All true. Each sentence individually true, and arranged, she was aware, the way you arrange true sentences when you are lying to your mother. "Tell Uncle Beng not to call the developer back yet. It's my signature, so it's my window."

Her mother took that about as well as a badly set table, and said several things about sentimentality, and about Malacca, damp, and about people who at thirty-four had every reason to be putting money into their future instead of driving away from it, which was as close as her mother came nowadays to mentioning the fiancé, and Su-Lin held the phone a little away from her ear and looked at the beaded feet of a bride who did not exist, and said mm at intervals, and eventually was released.

She put the Nokia on the counter and it sat there, mute, next to the slipper, one object from each of her lives.

---

The knock came on Sunday morning, while she was on her knees in the middle hall with the torch, working along the skirting under the patched niche — she had promised herself she was only *looking*. Through the shop's shutters she saw a man on the five-foot way: mid-thirties, work shirt, jeans, a clipboard, a small canvas tool bag at his feet, studying her upper facade with his head tilted back and an expression of open interest, and her first thought was, with real venom: *Thursday. They're not even waiting for the body to cool.*

She opened the door already at full KL. "If you're from the developer, the answer is no, and if you're photographing for them, that's the second answer."

The man lowered his gaze from the roofline without hurry, taking her in with the same open interest he'd been giving the eaves, which was somehow more annoying.

"That's a shame," he said mildly. "I came about the third thing."

"Which is?"

"Your roof. Ariff Hafiz." He didn't offer the clipboard like a warrant, just held it, easy. "Conservation contractor. The heritage trust engaged me to survey this terrace — a roof came down over a kitchen three doors that way in April, nobody hurt, but the trust would prefer to be surprised zero more times, so I'm doing the row. Letters went out to all the owners last month." He glanced past her shoulder, into the dim shop, not moving his feet an inch over the threshold. "Yours would have been addressed to Miss Ho."

The mail. There was a wire basket behind the door with a month of the dead woman's mail in it, which she had been avoiding the way you avoid a dead person's answering machine, and when she went through it with him standing patiently on the five-foot way, there it was: the trust's letterhead, the survey schedule, the polite paragraph. She read it twice and felt her indignation looking for somewhere to sit down.

"You could have led with the letter."

"I could," Hafiz agreed. "But then I wouldn't know how you'd treat a stranger you thought was a developer, and now I do, and I have to say I broadly support it." Not quite a smile; the architecture of one. "Twenty minutes in your roof space. I bring my own torch and I don't sell anything."

She should, she knew, have verified him with a phone call. Instead she did something more effective and less polite: she tested him. As he set his ladder in the back hall she asked, in the tone of someone making conversation, what he'd use to repoint the facade, and watched for the word *cement* the way you watch a candidate's hands in an interview.

"Lime," Hafiz said, going up the ladder. "Same as she was built. Cement's the worst thing that ever happened to this street — hard render on soft brick, traps the damp inside the wall, and the wall rots behind it while everyone admires how neat it looks. Half my work is undoing the confident repairs of 1985." He paused, head and shoulders in the dark of the ceiling manhole, and his voice came down dry: "You can come up, by the way. You've been vibrating like somebody's site architect since I picked up the ladder."

She went up.

The roof space ran the length of the house, a long low tent of heat and old dust smelling of clay and bat, the underside of the tiles glowing here and there where daylight found a slipped edge. Hafiz moved along the trusses in a crouch with an economy she recognized — a man who knew where weight could go, the roof-space walk you couldn't teach — and his torch beam travelled the timber like a finger going along a line of text.

"Selangan batu, these," he said, patting a truss. "Heavy hardwood, still sound, most of them. One purlin aft with beetle — there, see the frass — that's a splice job, not a catastrophe. Maybe thirty tiles slipped or cracked. Somebody has kept this roof, you know. Patched, cleaned gutters, kept the load off. For a house whose owner was eighty-two, this is a very honest roof." His beam stopped on the face of a tie beam, close to the joint, and held. "Here. Look at this. You'll like this."

She duck-walked over, sweating, and looked. Cut into the timber near the joint were marks — deliberate strokes, chisel-made, a small constellation of them, and once she'd seen the first set she saw the others, one at each joint down the run of the roof, each set different.

"Carpenter's marks," Hafiz said. "The frame was cut and fitted on the ground, every joint made to its partner — then they knock it apart, haul it up, and these tell them what goes back where. That's the third truss telling you it's the third truss. And this stroke here — " his light touched a longer, curved mark, made with a different confidence, almost a flourish " — that's the tukang himself, the master carpenter. His mark. Houses sign their letters. You just have to read."

Su-Lin crouched in the hot dark under the tiles of a house she had owned for nine days, looking at the signature of a man who had been dead for a century, and felt something shift in the weekend, quietly, like a load path finding a truer bearing.

"There's a niche downstairs," she heard herself say. "Left of the altar. Bricked up and plastered over. And a door to the back lane nailed shut under about nine coats of paint."

Hafiz was silent for a moment. The torch beam stayed where it was, on the carpenter's flourish.

"Old houses close doors for reasons," he said at last. "Sometimes the reason is rats. Sometimes it isn't. Either way — " and now he did smile, and started moving back towards the manhole " — you don't open a wall until you can read the letter it's in. I'll put the roof report through the trust. The splice and the tiles can wait for the monsoon to threaten. Nothing here needs doing this month except reading."

He was off the five-foot way and folding his ladder before she remembered she hadn't offered him so much as water, and at the last moment he paused, looking up at the facade again, then in through the open door at the long counter with its trays of beads.

"This is Sister Foong's shop," he said. It wasn't a question. "My mother was married in slippers from this house. Nineteen seventy-one. She still has them in a box with tissue paper. She'll want to know it's in good hands." He hefted the ladder onto his shoulder and gave Su-Lin a nod that managed to include her, the house, and some obligation between the two of them that she had apparently just accepted without signing anything. "Are they? Good hands?"

"Ask the roof," Su-Lin said. "Apparently it's honest."

That got the full smile, briefly, like sun between shophouses. Then he was two doors up the five-foot way, exchanging Hokkien with Uncle Raju's son in front of the kopitiam, and Su-Lin was alone again in a house that signed its letters.

---

By evening the street had turned over into its weekend self: the night market waking up along Jonker across the way, loudspeakers testing love songs, the smell of frying carrying down the lanes, tourists drifting past her shuttered shopfront with the peaceable aimlessness of grazing animals. Su-Lin ate tau fu fah standing at the counter like a woman at her own wake, and then she switched on the one good lamp over the shop counter, and brought down the tin.

She had bought a Chinese-English dictionary that afternoon from the bookshop near the bridge, a fat student's edition smelling of new glue, and the transaction had cost her a small piece of pride she decided her grandmother never needed to hear about. Radical index. Stroke counts. Standard Five, reporting for duty.

The envelopes she could not read. But the letters inside were dated — she checked five, ten, at random, handling each like site glass — dated at the head in the western numerals of a woman who had kept accounts, the year plain as a survey stake even where everything after it was forest. So she cleared the whole counter, and untied bundle after bundle, and did the one thing she was unambiguously qualified to do with sixty years of someone's heart: she sorted it.

It took two hours. The tourists thinned outside; the love songs gave way to a man singing keroncong somewhere with more feeling than pitch; the beads in the counter under her hands held their rows of rust and rose and jade. When she finished, the letters lay in one long ordered rank down the glass, oldest at the left, and the oldest of all — alone, before even the war, before everything — was dated 1941.

Su-Lin sat down on the beader's stool with the dictionary at her elbow and the lamp making its warm circle, and began, character by character, stroke count by stroke count, at the pace of a child or an archaeologist, to read.

The first sentence took her most of an hour. When it finally stood whole in her notebook, in her own blunt pencil under that small upright disciplined hand, she read it three times, and the house behind her went back and back into the dark, room after room, holding its breath.

*Tonight I write what I cannot say aloud in this house.*
