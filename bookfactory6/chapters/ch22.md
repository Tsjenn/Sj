# The Man from Ipoh

The man from Ipoh came down to Malacca on the mail lorry at the end of the dry weeks, and Mei Foong, standing in the rice queue on Kling Street with the household's cards, saw him arrive. He was lifted down the way furniture is lifted — carefully, by two men thinking about the corners — and set on his feet on the five-foot way, where he stood swaying a little among his own possessions, smiling at a point just above the shophouse roofs, at something pleasanter than anything on Kling Street. Old Khoo Teik Swee of the Kinta Valley, who had gone into the ground poor and come up out of it with six dredges, machines the size of temples that ate the earth day and night. The dredges belonged to the war now. What was left of the man they had made had come south with one rattan case, one wooden crate with the black horn of a gramophone showing through the slats, and his daughter.

She got down last, and paid the lorry man, and Mei Foong watched her do it: the fare counted into his palm at the printed rate, coin by coin, and nothing over — not because nothing was owed but because nothing over existed. She wore a grey dress of good cloth, cut in a year when cloth was cut generously, and it had been taken in and taken up and mended at the cuff and once at the shoulder, in stitches so small they had stopped being repairs and become a position. Then she took her father's arm, not the way daughters take an arm but the way porters take a load they have carried a long way and know the balance of, and steered him in under the cousins' signboard, and the crate with the horn went in after them, and the five-foot way went back to its queues.

Mei Foong had seen her once before, upside down, on a teak table, in a photographer's chair: a girl looking past the camera, counting the exits politely. There were no exits now to count. She stood in the rice queue with the cards in her hand and found she had taken the stranger's inventory without meaning to — one case, one crate, one father, one dress worn like full ceremony — and that the total had a name, and the name was her own arrival, ten years ago, coming up from the jetty behind Ah Yoke: everything owned, carried; everything else, carried too.

By evening the kitchen court had the whole of it, the way the court had everything by evening. The Khoo dredges seized in the first year and worked for the war until they broke, and no Khoo paid a cent; the marble house outside Ipoh full of officers, its stone floors from Italy under their boots; the son who had gone to study in Hong Kong, and no letter out of Hong Kong since the first month of the war; and the pipe, which had been at its patient work in old Khoo since before the matchmaker's fan ever opened, and had finished the work the war began. The cousins on Kling Street kept a chandler's shop, old stock and older debts, and were the only kin left with a roof to spare; their reply to Ipoh, the court reported, had taken four days to compose and two lines to say: that of course there was room, and that times were hard everywhere.

"Tin," said Third Aunt to the stove, by way of a funeral oration for the House of Khoo. "Everyone said tin was solid. Solid sinks."

On the third day Siew Ean called at the house of seven doors, because the call was owed and there was no name for it. There is no etiquette for visiting the mother of a man you were betrothed to who has not come back: not condolence, for no death had been declared and the Bibik would have no death by inference; not congratulation; not nothing. The two of them performed it faultlessly anyway, tea and correctness in the front hall for the better part of an hour — the Bibik upright with the good cups out, the girl upright in the mended dress — two proud houses, one broken and one bleeding, agreeing across the teak table to speak only of journeys and weather and the price of nothing. Mei Foong carried the tray in and out and was not looked at, being the tray's attachment merely. At the door, leaving, Siew Ean bowed to the depth the house was owed, exactly, and the Bibik watched her go down the five-foot way and said, to the doorway, in the voice she used for closed subjects:

"That one would have done."

Then she went in to the ancestral hall and fed the incense, and nothing further was said in that house about the Khoos for a month, except by everybody, constantly, below the level at which the Bibik heard.

---

That year the war belonged to the women, the way debts belong to whoever stays.

The men held what offices were left to them — queue, bow, sign, endure — and the women fed everyone. It was done the way everything was done now, sideways, in kitchens, in the second language of the town: tuition taught over cooking fires, jewellery that went visiting and did not come home, rice appearing in houses that could not have bought rice. Within a fortnight of the mail lorry, Siew Ean was teaching. Arithmetic she taught openly, at the front of the cousins' shop, for a cup of oil or four eggs; English she taught in kitchens, quietly, after the shutters went up — because English had been sent out of the schools, and the mothers of Malacca, who did not discuss the war, had made a private finding about how it would end, and paid in sweet potato to be right. Mei Foong sent her the first of those pupils. Among them was the tally clerk's small daughter, a solemn child who could carry anything in her head, whose father had once carried something in his head the length of a bad road; Mei Foong asked him with her eyes only, and he looked at her for a moment and sent the child.

The Bibik's jewellery began going out the same season, through Ah Yoke, piece by piece: the kerosang pins, the hairpins, the bangles that had come down to her through women whose photographs hung in the ancestral hall. Ah Yoke was past seventy now, and the pneumonia had left its flag planted in her; on wet mornings there was a click in her cough, and her hands, which had counted rice for a street, had begun to refuse fine work as an old servant refuses — without a word, by doing it slowly until the task was taken away. But she could still walk to a certain discreet shop off Java Lane with a knotted handkerchief in her sleeve, and be watched going by nobody at all, because who watches an old amah walk; and the handkerchief came back flat, and three households ate — the big house, the doctor's, and the Taiping cousins, who had stayed and multiplied. The Bibik called it lending. She said it the first time with her chin set for argument: the pieces were lent, gold was always only lent, gold knew its way home. Nobody argued. The word did more feeding than the gold did, in that house, and every woman in the court knew it, and the word was respected like a load-bearing wall.

And on Kling Street the gramophone records began to go, one at a time.

Mei Foong heard of it before she understood it — a dealer in such things had opened where the umbrella-mender had been, because the officers were fond of dance bands, and there was custom in other people's evenings. Later she would watch the whole method: each record played once through before it went, on the wound-up machine in the back of the shop — you do not sell a horse unwalked, Siew Ean said — then sleeved, and carried down, and sold, and its number crossed through in the violet-inked catalogue with a single ruled line, so that the ledger of her mining filled slowly with closed accounts. The violet numbers were a cruelty she had built herself, years ago, in a house where it was never evening: she knew exactly what was gone.

---

The cousins' shop had supplied the firm's godowns since before the war — rope, lamp oil, dammar, sundries — and supplied them still, in the shrunken way of everything, because Nagata preferred suppliers who were already in the book. A statement came up monthly to be set against the firm's account. The cousins' old bookkeeper had died in the second year of the occupation, and since then the statements had been the work of the shop boy, who added like a hen, in pecks, and Mei Foong had corrected him monthly with a patience she costed against the rope and oil.

Then, in the seventh month, a statement came up in a new hand.

She knew the hand before she knew she knew it. Fast, slanted, educated, not one stroke spent for show — she had seen it once, three years ago, on half a sheet of paper with no salutation, read twice by lamplight and handed back: *locked rooms survive wars.* She stood with the statement by the account-room window a long moment, and checked its column out of duty, and found she was checking it out of pleasure, because it was flawless, and because it was itemized the way she herself itemized, worst figure first. At the foot, where the boy used to attempt a flourish, was a note in the same fast hand: a discrepancy of some dollars between the shop's book and the firm's, pre-war credit against present payments, the rate in dispute; the shop would send someone.

On the Thursday, Khoo Siew Ean came up Heeren Street with the book under her arm, and was shown back through the first air well to the account room, and the two of them sat down on either side of the black desk like two clerks sent by their firms.

The work went quickly, because both of them were exact and neither performed the exactness. The discrepancy was found inside the half hour — the old credit converted twice at two different proclamation rates, once by the boy in hope and once by the firm in correctness — and they split the difference the way honest houses did that year, in favour of whoever was hungrier, which was not discussed and took one glance to settle. Siew Ean entered the correction in her fast slant. Mei Foong entered it in her small upright hand. It was, though neither said so, the pleasantest hour either had spent at a desk since the war began: work that added, done with a stranger who could add.

Then Mei Foong opened the shallow left-hand drawer for the blotting sand, and Siew Ean stopped in the middle of a figure.

The pen lay where it lived, on its folded square of blotting paper, squared to the corners: dark green, mottled like river water under trees, the gold of it worn and unfaded. Nothing else in the drawer but nibs, sand, and a hank of red thread. Siew Ean looked at it and did not move, and the room changed its weather.

"In Ipoh," she said at last, "he turned that pen in his fingers whenever the pauses came. Other men touch cigarettes. I made an inventory of him — it was all I was going to be allowed of him, an inventory, so I made a good one. Item: one fountain pen, old, worn gold. Not a rich boy's pen. I remember the conclusion I wrote against the entry: *somebody's pen that he keeps.*" She raised her eyes, and went over the room the way she had gone over the column — the ledger, the desk, the drawer that opened at that hand's touch and no other reason — and came back to Mei Foong's face. "I had it wrong by the grammar. Somebody's pen. He was keeping it for somebody."

Mei Foong stood with the sand in her hand. She heard her husband's method in her own head, the worst number first, no cost hidden, and gave it.

"He left it with the accounts," she said.

"And whose are the accounts?"

She did not answer, and the not-answering answered, and Siew Ean sat back very slowly, the way her father's clerks had sat back from a yield confirmed.

In the silence, Mei Foong did the sum she could not help doing, because she had been made a certain way and the times had finished the making. On the elders' paper, nothing had ever been dissolved. *Postponed until conditions permit* — the wire was still in the firm's file; no ceremony had released him, no ceremony ever would now; and in the reckoning both families lived by, the woman across the desk was owed this house's precedence, its mourning when mourning came, its truth. She held, without knowing she held it, one sentence — a question about the February of 1942, asked in the front hall in that convent voice — and the sentence would unstitch everything: the vow, the photograph with its folded bride, the tissue refolded in a camphor box, and a child's dates, which sharper fingers than a shop boy's could count backward as easily as forward. Ten years of walls, and the door in them stood open opposite her, in a mended dress, holding a chandler's book.

Siew Ean looked at the pen a while longer. Then she said, in the tone of a woman confirming a delivery date:

"He kept no locked room from you, then. Good. It was a fair marriage he escaped."

The sand went back in the drawer. The drawer closed on the pen. Neither of them ever said, then or after, one word more about the law of it; the sentence had been in the room, and weighed, and put away, and both of them had watched it done, and that was the whole of the transaction — off every book in Malaya but the one under Mei Foong's heart, where it was entered at full value.

"In December," Mei Foong said — it was the only payment she had, and she made it — "when your note came. He did not know what there was to say back. I told him: send good luck; it is what she has instead of all of us. He spent a wire on it in the morning."

"I know," said Siew Ean. "I kept the wire." She gathered her book, squaring it, and for one moment the clerk in her voice stepped down. "Two words and his name. It is the only letter from a man I have ever kept. He was economical." She stood. "So was I. We would have run a sound firm and died of politeness. What he did instead—" she looked once more at the closed drawer, and did not finish it, and picked the sentence up somewhere else, dry again: "Your figures are the first honest arithmetic I have met south of Tapah, Miss Ho. I will bring the book myself after this. It spares the boy, and the boy adds like a hen."

They went out together through the second air well, into the court, where the afternoon was letting go its heat. Across the well-swept bricks a small girl came at a run — escaped from a kitchen girl, going hard for the hens, who had wronged her — and Mei Foong's eyes went to her and steadied on her, the way a compass needle is not consulted and answers anyway; and came back. It was done before it could be prevented. Siew Ean had seen. She looked at the child for the space of one step, and at Mei Foong for less than that, and asked nothing at all, then or in all the years that came after; and she went down Heeren Street with the chandler's book under her arm, carrying, by Mei Foong's exact accounting, enough to feed every clerk and every informer in Malacca for a year, and never in her life spent a syllable of it.

Weddings are owed gifts, and the poor bring what they have. What Siew Ean had was silence, and she gave it entire, unasked, without letting it be seen given — the only wedding gift Mei Foong ever received; and the truth that four had carried now rested, part of it, in a fifth pair of hands, and was safe there, though no oath was sworn and no candle lit. Some keepers are appointed. The best are volunteers.

---

After that she came monthly with the book, and then the book began to be a formality, and then it stayed at the shop and she came anyway.

She called Mei Foong *Miss Ho* always, to the end; it had gone into her mouth as irony the day the drawer closed, and stayed as rank. She had a tongue like a tailor's scissors and used it on everything, most of all her own estate. Asked after the records, she consulted the air: "One hundred and forty-one when the war began. One hundred and nine as of this morning. My father spent thirty years taking things out of the ground," she said. "I am arranging restitution. The evenings are going back into the ground, one at a time, at a fair price, to men who will scratch them with bad needles. If you stand far enough away and have no heart, there is a rightness to it." And she wound the shop's machine and played number one hundred and eight once through before its sale — a dance band, London, ten years gone, the sound of people who thought the world was going to last — and the two of them listened to the end without speaking, and then she sleeved it and ruled the violet line, and that was the funeral it got.

She found Mei Foong at the beading one evening — the hired work, another bride's kasut manek under the one shaded lamp, because Ah Yoke's hands had put down the needle for good and the lamp's income now had one pair of hands to earn it — and she stood watching the beads go on, six to the stitch, and delivered her finding: "You keep this family's books by day and Malacca's feet by night. What do you keep for yourself?" "Tuesdays," said Mei Foong, "for the supervisor," and Siew Ean laughed her short laugh, one syllable, spent like the wire.

Ah Yoke's verdict came the way her verdicts came, sideways, after weeks of appearing not to look. She watched Siew Ean check a column once, down and across, finger straight, mouth shut; and later, at the stair, she said to Mei Foong, "That one counts like us." In the whole currency of Ah Yoke there was no larger note. The friendship was never announced, being merely built, the way the two of them built everything, entry by entry, and it would outlast the war, the Emergency, and every flag either of them would live to see raised; but that came later. That year it looked like a chandler's book that no longer needed carrying, carried.

---

In the last weeks of the year the aeroplanes came back over Malacca, and this time no siren knew what to say about them.

They went over very high, southward, over the strait, in the early morning: small, silver, in loose spaced rows, drawing long white lines that hung in the upper air after the sound had gone. The town came out into its doorways and looked up without pointing — it had learned not to point — and nobody said anything at all, because there was nothing sayable; the sky had changed hands, at a very great height, and everyone under it knew the feeling of watching money move.

The studio man on Jonker Street, who had photographed the town's weddings for thirty years and its identity papers for three, went up to his roof with the camera, because recording was his office and he held it. The print went round after that under the counters of the town, palm to palm, a small grey square: sky, the roof-line of the street, and above it a thin scratching of crosses, very high, very small, countable. People held it the length of two breaths and gave it back. Mei Foong held it once, in the rice queue, between a washerwoman and a clerk's mother, and thought of another photograph in another year, passed silent through a front hall — men going grey around a newspaper — and understood that the town was reading this one exactly as that one had been read: not as news. As a date, arriving.

Nagata read it before any of them, because reading such things was his trade.

The Tuesdays changed. For two years the man had come to her columns the way other men went to a bathhouse; now the dry finger went down the page too fast and settled nowhere, and the joke about the one honest thing in Malacca was retired without ceremony. He took more. The entries she was instructed to make — *condemned*, *destroyed by damp*, *written off in transit* — multiplied past what damp could be asked to carry, and the drawer of his desk at the firm grew a second lock, and his signature, which had always had its price, repriced itself weekly, like the eggs. He watched the harbour, which said less and less; then he watched the sky like everyone else. He was a man reading the tide he had stolen by, and finding it turned.

On the second Tuesday of December he stayed after the books were closed.

He sat while she squared the ledgers away, and when the desk was clear he laid on it a schoolboy's exercise book, new, cheap, the grey ration paper of the times, and set his own pen beside it, and did not uncap it.

"A private account, Miss Ho," he said. "Not the firm's. Mine." And then, dictating, in the flat voice of the Tuesdays, while she sat unmoving: what he had, item by item. The taels, by weight, and in whose keeping. A packet of stones, so described, and its keeper. Sums standing with a goldsmith in Singapore under a name not his; sums owed him by men in three towns, with the men's names, and against each name the favour that had made the debt — the pass, the permit, the exemption, the looking-away, each one priced. It was his whole war, converted and itemized: every condemned bale, every gift of whisky and quinine, every stroke of the signature that had been the only honest currency he dealt in, rendered down into a page and a half of portable value. When he was done he looked at her over the desk.

"In your hand," he said. "Your figures do not lie. And custody, Miss Ho, is everything. A man does not keep his own book; in his hand it is a confession. In yours it is housekeeping." He believed it, she saw; it was the tide's own logic, and he had lived by it nine years in Singapore and three here, and it had never yet been wrong for him. He stood, and took his hat, and at the door his hand went briefly to his breast pocket — to the flat place where the certificate girl lived, second in her prefecture — the first time in a year she had seen the hand go there. "Head it *Sundries*," he said, and went down through the house and out, correct at every door.

She wrote it fair that evening, at the black desk, under the shaded lamp: *Sundries*, and the taels, and the stones, and the goldsmith, and the men in three towns with their favours priced beside them. She wrote it with the office pen, the steel nib kept for rough work. The shallow drawer stayed shut. Some things in that room were not for hire.

Then she sat with the finished thing in front of her, and made herself read it as she had been taught to read everything, both entries at once.

Every transaction lives twice — the correspondence course had put it like a secret, and it was one: once as what is gained, once as what it cost, and the books do not balance until both are written. For three years the man had banked the gains and left the cost side blank, trusting the times to keep no records. Now the times were ending, and he had done what every prudent firm does when the auditors are sighted: he had brought his cost ledger up to date. He had simply had the wit — she gave it to him coldly, at full value — to bring it up to date in someone else's handwriting. If his own police found this book, the hand that wrote it would be asked why, and hands were hanged for less that year. And if the flags changed — the thought stood up in her plainly, and she let it stand — then what lay on the black desk was worth more than the taels it counted: every theft dated, every bribe priced, every name named, a collaborator's confession lacking only a signature, and better than a signature, because a signature can be denied and a witness cannot.

She made the tea she had not made him, and drank it herself, and washed the cup twice.

Then she came back to the desk and looked a long time at the grey exercise book squared on the black wood, and understood, exactly and finally, what had been put into her keeping. It was not a book. It was a rope: measured, coiled, patient, its weight honest in the hand — a rope that would fit whichever neck the changing flags required, his or her own, no third choice offered; and she had woven every inch of it herself, at dictation, in the smallest and most upright of hands.
