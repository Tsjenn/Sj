# The Hungry Year

In the third year of the new money Third Aunt stopped carrying a purse to market and began carrying a pillowslip. She set it down on the kitchen table one morning — limp, half full, the brown notes inside bound into bricks with string, the way old letters are bound — and set down beside it what the pillowslip had bought: two eggs, a hand of kangkong going yellow at the stem, and a piece of dried fish the size of a playing card.

"Money," she said, in the voice of a woman finishing an argument she had begun in 1942.

Mei Foong entered it without comment. The dollar column in the household book had died quietly the year before, of causes nobody bothered to certify; she ruled it off the way you close the account of a man who has left town owing everyone, and kept the true book in rice, and then, when rice itself became a rumor, in tapioca — because tapioca was what there was, and a ledger is kept in what there is. The town had learned the root's whole family that year. Boiled, it filled you the way a promise fills you. Grated and steamed in a cloth it could be made to imitate rice at a distance, in bad light, by the willing; Third Aunt produced this dish daily and called it rice with a face that forbade discussion, and everyone called it rice, and everyone knew. Sweet potato was wealth. An egg was a week's negotiation. White rice — true rice, the ration dwindled now to a measure that would once have insulted a bird — was medicine, and was hoarded and administered as medicine: for the sick, for the very old, for the child.

The child ate first. That was the one law left in the house of seven doors, never announced and never once broken; the amahs went short, the aunts went short, the Towkay himself sat before tapioca in the front hall with the mother-of-pearl gleaming around him like a joke at his expense; and Mei Lan, two years old and then three, ate — an egg beaten into her porridge while the kitchen court, collectively and without a word, did not watch. The wall behind the kitchen had given up the last of Ah Yoke's war-hoard before the year's first monsoon: the final bag, sewn shut since 1941, opened under her own hands, and she measured it out over months, level scoop by level scoop, and when it was done she folded the sacking flat and put it away, because sacking was also currency now.

"Seven years I fed that wall," she said. "It has fed us four. No investment of the Towkay's ever paid half so well." It was as near to boasting as she came, and she was entitled to it, and even so she said it quietly, because it was not a year in which you said aloud that your house had eaten.

---

The street was sick that year the way a man long underfed is sick: of everything, one thing after another, each small illness renting the space the hunger had cleared. Fevers walked the five-foot way. The wet season brought the flux; the dry brought a swelling sickness that puffed the ankles of grown men until they dented like dough, and old people who had been thin went transparent, and children who should have been loud were quiet, which was the worst sound in Malacca.

Dr. Lim's clinic no longer had a fee book worth the name. He kept one anyway, from habit and from principle, and Mei Foong, who helped him with his dispensary two evenings a week now — grinding, folding powders into papers, boiling what stood in for bandages — sometimes read the column where the fees went and found it had become a market list written by a poet: *three eggs. A hand of bananas. One umbrella, mended. Two catties tapioca, grated. Paid in full.* Gratitude he did not enter, though it was the bulk of his income and arrived daily, carried in both hands. He treated the swelling sickness with what the swelling sickness actually wanted, which was food, and since he could not prescribe what did not exist he prescribed patience and rice-water and lied as little as he could manage, and came home in the evenings with his face folded shut, and unfolded it at the door because Bee Nio was watching and the house had agreed without a meeting to go on being survivable.

Death that year moved into the street like a relative come to stay: given the small room, worked around, mentioned in lowered voices. It took the old songbird seller from Jonker whose linnets had outlived his customers; it took the towgay man's mother, and a kitchen girl's father up-country, known to the court only as a name that had sent dried greens once a year; and the street wore white so often that white stopped being news. The funerals went by short-handed and short-papered — grief itself on the ration — and the house of seven doors stood at its door for each one, correct, and gave what could be given, and each time the procession passed, the deaths remained at arm's length, which is where the times had taught everyone to hold them. You bowed. You entered nothing. You went back in to the pot.

---

Nagata came to the accounts on Tuesdays still, but the rest had gone out of the ritual.

He was thinner — even the tide he stole on had ebbed; there was less to take and he took a greater share of it, with the diligence of a man filling sandbags — and he had developed a habit of standing at the office window looking at the harbour, which said less every month, and reading it the way he had once read her columns. The war news came now only from the loudspeakers, and the loudspeakers announced victories the way the banana notes announced value, in denominations that rose as the substance fell; and men of Nagata's sort had stopped listening to the announcements and begun listening to the geography. The victories were nearer than victories ought to be. The aeroplanes that crossed the strait crossed it high, unhurried, in daylight, and nothing went up from anywhere to argue with them.

Since the year before, there had been the second book.

It lived in the account room, plain as an exercise book because it was one, and every entry in it was in Mei Foong's hand, because he had dictated it to her across the desk in the flat voice of a man dictating inventory: what had been taken and from whom, what returned; sums, dates, the yen and the taels of it; the names of men his signature had unhooked from lists, set down with particular care, like receipts against a debt not yet presented; and the names of the men who had sold to him and bought from him, which was half the street, because eating had required it. A confession, kept as collateral. He believed, she understood, that the book was his passage out of whatever was coming — proof for one side that he had stolen gently, proof for the other that he had been owed — and he had lodged it with her the way you lodge valuables with a bank: because her columns did not lie, and he had spent three years confirming it, every Tuesday, with a dry finger.

She kept it because refusing had not been among the choices. She knew to the ounce what she was holding. It sat in the account drawer where a fountain pen had once been left for her, and she thought about that sometimes, about what the drawer had held first and what it held now, and made no entry on the thought.

---

Ah Yoke sat to the scoop now. That was the year's plainest sentence, and the court read it daily and said nothing: the stool at the side door, placed by Third Aunt each dawn and each dawn not remarked on, and the old woman on it with the bin at her knee, levelling the measure against her palm for a queue that was longer than it had ever been and quieter, because queues too were starving. The measure was smaller. The level was exact. She had a flag planted in her lungs — Dr. Lim's word, from the pneumonia year — and the flag had stood there since, and each December came to her now like a creditor, and each December she paid, and the paying showed. Her hands, which had failed first for fine work, had begun to fail for coarse; she who had ironed creases that could cut paper let Mei Foong iron now, supervising with one eyebrow; and through that whole year, with the method of a woman closing up a house room by room, she handed things over. The scoop's discipline. The head-count of the lane's households and which of them would lie about a death to keep a ration card, and which would starve first rather than lie, and how you fed the second kind without being caught respecting them. The accounts of borrowed cupfuls she had kept in her head to the grain since 1942, recited once, in order, like scripture.

Dr. Lim listened to her chest at the New Year and said stairs, wet cloth, and hurry were forbidden her, and she thanked him and obeyed the parts that did not interfere with work. She paid the December of 1944 like the others. But it took its interest, and the court heard the interest at night now through the boards of the amahs' room, the small tight cough with the click in it, patient, keeping its own books.

On an evening in the fourth month of 1945, at the hour when the swallows come out, she called Mei Foong up the back stair, and when Mei Foong came in she was sitting on her bed with her hair down.

Mei Foong stopped in the doorway. In eleven years she had seen the plait combed a thousand mornings and had never once seen it undone at night; the taking-down was private the way prayer is private. It hung now over the old woman's shoulder in a grey rope come unravelled at the end, and on the bed at her side lay the comb in its cloth, unwrapped, and Ah Yoke looked at her and then looked at the comb, which was the whole of the request, because she had never in her life asked twice for anything, or, until that evening, once.

Mei Foong sat behind her on the bed and combed her hair.

She did it the way she had been taught by watching: from the ends, patiently, the left hand following the right through the grey, and Ah Yoke sat straight under her hands and issued corrections. "Oil first. Have you learned nothing." Then, later: "Long strokes. It is hair, not accounts." The window went from mangosteen to dark. Below in the court the pots were being scoured; somewhere a child — her child — was being put to bed by another woman, in the east room, in the light; and Mei Foong combed, and the old head grew heavy and easy under the comb the way heads do, and for a long while neither of them said anything, because the combing was the conversation, and both of them had always preferred work that could stand for speech.

"The scoop," Ah Yoke said at last, "keeps its level when the bin is low. Especially then. A short measure from a full bin is theft. A short measure from a low bin is despair, and despair travels down a queue faster than fever. They watch your hand, girl, every morning; they cannot see into the bin; your hand is the only report they get of how the world stands. Keep it level and they will queue like citizens through the end of anything." A breath, with the click in it. "The bin is my forty years of standing at that door. I leave you the bin too, but the bin is nothing. The scoop is the estate."

"Yes, Ah Yee."

"The case is not locked. It was never locked; locks are for people who have not arranged their lives." The comb went down the grey; the old woman's eyes were closed now. "You know what is at the bottom, under the paper. You know the knots. It is lighter than it was — a ring went to its duty, and a slipper went to its duty, and what is left is a letter, and a curl of hair that weighs what you weighed, and the thread. Keep it as I kept you. Under everything. In reach." And then, in the same voice exactly, the voice for orders: "I paid her, girl. Whatever the count says, tell her I paid."

"You overpaid," Mei Foong said. Her hands did not stop. "I will tell her. She will have to bear the difference."

Something went through the old body that in any other body would have been a laugh. Ah Yoke sat while her hair was braided — one plait, laid over one shoulder, the work checked by touch and, a miracle, not corrected — and then she said, "Enough. Sleep. The queue comes early," which had been the last sentence of her every evening for eleven years; and Mei Foong wrapped the comb in its cloth and set it where it lived, and lay down on her own bed an arm's length away, and matched her breathing to the old woman's, one beat behind, the way she had on her first night on that side of the sea.

In the morning the queue came early, and the stillness in the corner had a shape.

She had gone in her sleep, in the amahs' room, at the top of the house she had run from the bottom of; on her back, straight, the plait over her shoulder where it had been laid, the blanket undisturbed — she had died the way she paid for everything, exactly, without owing, and without one grain of fuss spilled anywhere. Mei Foong stood over her in the grey light for a long time before she called anyone. The face had let go of its discipline at last, and what was under the discipline, it turned out, had been the discipline, all the way down; and that was so like her, so entirely and finally like her, that Mei Foong's grief came up through the middle of something that was almost, almost laughter, and met itself, and she sat down on the edge of her own bed with her hands in her lap and let the two of them argue it out.

Below, faint through the boards, she heard Third Aunt set the stool at the side door, and then the small wooden sound of the scoop going into the bin, and stopping.

---

The sisterhood came before noon.

Word had gone from the kitchen court to the vegetarian hall off the third street — the sworn spinsters' own house, where the aunts of Ah Yoke's kind retired, and prayed, and kept each other — and they came through the occupied town in their black and white, seven of them and then eleven, unhurried at the checkpoints, bowing their exact bows, and took possession of the death the way a firm's head office takes possession of a branch. The household was thanked and set aside. This was hall business. They washed her and dressed her in garments she had lodged with the hall years before, folded against this day and paid for by instalments across four decades, because a majie carried her own funeral the way she carried everything, arranged, level, owing nothing to anyone's charity; and the eldest of them, a small iron woman past eighty whom the others called simply Eldest Sister, unpicked the plait Mei Foong had braided, and washed the grey hair, and oiled it, and braided it again with her own hands, and dressed it with white cord — the vow, dressed for presentation; sixty years of no, worn at the last like regalia.

The wake was kept at the hall, before their Guan Yin, with the chanting going in shifts through the curfew nights on a permit that appeared without being applied for, stamped by a signature the account room knew. The Towkay sent the coffin — true planks, found in a town where wood had been eaten like everything else, at a price he never disclosed — and stood at the hall door with his hat in his hands like a visitor, which in that house of women he was. The Bibik came leaning on a kitchen girl's arm, and stood before the coffin of her servant of thirty years, and bowed lower than any employer bows, and no one remarked it; and afterwards she sent to the hall, for the funeral meal, rice — white rice, a full bag, in that year — and where it had been living no one asked.

On the second evening Eldest Sister sent for Mei Foong and received her sitting, with two others standing by as witnesses, because the hall did its business as business.

"Sister Yoke left her wishes with us, as we all do," she said, in a Cantonese so like the dead woman's that hearing it was like a hand on the back of the neck. "Her passage was paid these ten years. The remainder is short and soon said. The case, and everything in it, to the girl she raised." She looked at Mei Foong for a moment with eyes that had inspected sixty years of novices. "She never once called you daughter to us. She was sworn; she kept her words the way she kept her measures; you know how she kept her measures. She called you *the girl*, twenty years and more, and we understood her perfectly. Now. Tomorrow she goes up the hill, and there is the question of who walks first. Among us, the one who walks first is the child of the dead. She has no child," and here one of the standing sisters made the smallest motion, a settling, the way a court settles when the verdict is known, "she has what we have, which is each other, and she has what none of the rest of us managed, which is you."

She stood, which took a while, and the two sisters stood straighter.

"The amah's daughter walks first," she said. "Be at the hall before light."

Mei Foong had heard the phrase all her life. It had been said over her head at the gate when she was twelve, and said in the front hall at two dollars' worth of praise — *the amah's girl, proof that anyone can be trained* — and written by her own hand in the registration list, *servant*, and said in a dozen mouths in two languages, and always, always, it had been a small door, held open just long enough to show her which side of it she lived on: *nobody's girl. Staff.* In the Eldest Sister's mouth the weight moved along the phrase like a hand moving along a beam, and the door turned out to open the other way. It was a lineage. It said: raised by Ho Ah Yoke of this hall, formed by her, finished by her, and presented to us as her one work of that kind, the way another woman presents a child; there was no higher paper in the sisterhood's possession, and they had just, before witnesses, given it to her. She took it the way you take a full measure. She bowed to the three of them, lower than any sentry ever got from her, and could not speak, and was not expected to.

She walked first up the hill in the morning, in mourner's white over the black and white they lent her, carrying the lamp; behind her the sisters in their ranks, the chanting, the coffin, the household, the street. It was a thin procession by the old measures and a rich one by that year's, and the town stood in its doorways for it, because the town knew what was passing: forty years of level scoops going up the hill. At the grave — the sisterhood's own ground, high, facing the strait — they buried her with her comb, and with a paper twist that Third Aunt had made up herself and placed with her own hands: one full scoop of the Bibik's white rice, level, no seconds. "No short measures," Third Aunt said, to the coffin, in the voice she used for correcting seasoning, "where you are going," and stepped back, and was thunderous all the way down the hill, which was her weeping.

Mei Foong wept on the hill itself, at the end, when the earth went in — openly, helplessly, her back present and the whole of her present with it, the first time the street had ever seen her do it; and it was there, in the middle of that, that she felt a small tug at her white sleeve and looked down into her daughter's face.

Mei Lan was in white too big for her, turned up twice at the wrists, her hair in the plaits Mei Foong had made that morning with hands that shook. She had been solemn and good through everything, and had reached the end of solemn. She looked up from under the two fast strokes of her brow with a child's exact, terrible directness and asked, in the fiddle-language of the house:

"Why do you cry the most?"

The hillside went quiet in the particular way of a crowd that has heard a child ask the one question in the room. And Bee Nio, standing on the other side of her, in white, steady as a shelf, answered before the silence could become anything.

"Because Ah Yoke raised her," she said. "As she helps raise you."

Mei Lan considered this, found it in order the way children find true things in order, and turned back to the grave; and after a moment she reached up and took hold of Mei Foong's hand, with both of hers, the two-handed grip, the settled ownership, and held it for the rest of the rites. It was the truth. It had gone by in the open, in front of the whole street, wearing the only clothes it was permitted in that family; and of everyone on the hill, only the two women in white on either side of the child knew that anything had passed at all. Their eyes met once over the small plaited head. Neither of them looked away quickly. That was all, and it was a great deal.

---

The queue came the next morning, because hunger keeps no mourning, and the scoop was at the side door at first light, and the hand on it was level.

Mei Foong sat to the bin on the stool Third Aunt placed and pretended not to have placed, and measured out the lane's morning, household by household, exact, and the queue watched her hand the way it had watched the other hand for forty years, and read in it the only report it would get of how the world stood, and queued like citizens. She held the office from that day. The rest of the estate she took up piece by piece: the head-count of the lane, the accounts of borrowed cupfuls, and, when her birthday came round, the walk to Cheng Hoon Teng, alone now, to have a name-paper written — two characters, as written, no surname — and to stand a while in the smoke doing what the paper did, which was remember without claiming.

The bundle lay at the bottom of her own case now, under the paper lining, the red thread wound the way she had first seen it wound by starlight when she was twelve. She was the last keeper. *When there is one again, and the one is you, you will decide what I have had to decide, and you will be alone in it, as I have been.* The inheritance had arrived, exactly as promised, and it was not money.

She did not write to him that year. The pen came out of the drawer twice and went back twice, and the tin stayed shut, as it had stayed shut through the whole of the hunger; the letters were statements of account, and she would not render the man she loved a statement made of nothing but subtractions. She waited, the way a sound firm waits out a bad year, for one entry worth his reading. It was coming. The whole town could feel it coming, and no one dared say toward what.

---

The rumors arrived before any fact did, the way smoke arrives before a fire: landings, in the north, in the south, on the peninsula, nowhere, everywhere; great ships; an end. The wireless said nothing a sane person could spend, and the harbour said everything, by saying absolutely nothing at all — empty water, day after day, under high unhurried aeroplanes. The queue traded rumors like currency now, and like the currency you needed a sackful to buy anything; but the sacks were filling.

Then, on a morning in the eighth month, before any broadcast the street could trust had trusted itself to say one word, the supervisor's office read its own future and passed sentence on its own files.

Nagata burned papers in the courtyard of the firm for a day and a night. The clerks carried them out by the armload — registers, requisitions, correspondence, three years of the administered hunger in its own handwriting — and fed them to a drum fire that he stood over himself, jacket off, sleeves turned, feeding and stirring with an iron rod, patient as a cook; and the ash went up over the roofline and drifted down the five-foot way, and settled on the queue, which watched in silence and understood the news better than any loudspeaker had ever told it anything. He burned through the heat of the day and by lamplight against every blackout rule there had ever been, because the rules were in the drum, and in the deep of the night the fire was still muttering, and in the morning it was embers and he was still standing at it, grey to the elbows, making sure of the corners the way she checked a column, twice.

He did not once ask her for the second book. That told her everything about what the book was to him, and everything about what it would shortly be to everyone else, and by evening she had decided that its address was no longer good enough.

She went out without a lamp, the exercise book wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, down the curfew-empty lane and in through the back door of the shuttered rental two doors past the kopitiam — the firm's narrow dark house whose key answered to her, where a lacquered box had kept its vigil since the year of the exchange.

The dark in there was total, and it did not matter; she had learned its middle hall by heart the day she chose it. To the left of the altar, where the wall had once held the twin of the surviving lamp niche, there was now a rectangle of raw brick under limewash — closed since the year of the exchange, laid up course by course at night by her own hands and Ah Yoke's, mortar mixed in a kueh basin, a wall built by an amah and an accounts girl and built well, because everything those two hands had ever done together had been done level. The plaster would come later, when there was plaster in the world again. One brick in the third course sat dry, without mortar, square and innocent among its bedded neighbors, and her fingers found its edges and walked it out of the wall.

The cavity breathed at her: camphor, ash, held air. On the shelf inside, square to the edge, sat the lacquered box, and in the box, on its silk, slept the small slipper her mother had beaded for a child already past the size of it, peony and phoenix, the first step that had been missed the most. Her daughter's truth, walled into the dark of the house that must not know it, an arm's length from the tablets of the blood it came from.

She slid the oilcloth parcel in beside the box, against the other wall of the niche, not touching it. Then she stood a moment with her hand flat on the cold shelf between the two of them — a child's slipper and a collaborator's confession, her daughter's truth and a frightened man's, the dearest thing she guarded and the most dangerous thing she held, lodged an arm's length apart in the one bank that had never yet failed anybody: brick, and dark, and a woman who could keep a level.

"Not the same account," she told the dark, in Cantonese, in the voice for orders. "Do not let them touch."

She fitted the dry brick home and swept the joint with her palm. Somewhere beyond the roofs the last of Nagata's fire put its thin smoke up between the town and the stars, three years of paper going wherever paper goes; and Mei Foong went back up the amahs' stair through the sleeping house — the oldest keeper now of everything in it — and lay down an arm's length from an empty bed, and waited, with the whole of the town, for the flags to change.
