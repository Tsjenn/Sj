# A Name Written in Water

On the morning she turned sixteen, Ah Yoke woke her in the dark with a hand on her shoulder and the smell of sandalwood already on her sleeves. "Wash," she said. "Wear the good blouse. We go before the street wakes."

This was the one day of the year Ah Yoke asked for, and the household had long ago learned to give it without comment. Three hundred and sixty-four mornings Ah Yoke rose first to light the kitchen fire; on the morning of Mei Foong's birthday the fire was left to the second amah, and the two of them went out through the side door into a Heeren Street still blue with night, and walked to the temple.

Mei Foong had made this walk every year since she was thirteen, and she loved it the way you love things that are yours by repetition: the fish smell from the market carts on Jonker Street, the kopitiam boy setting out marble tables in lamplight. Ah Yoke walked fast, in the black-and-white she wore every day of her life, and did not talk. Talk was for the return journey.

Cheng Hoon Teng took the first sun before any house in the town. The roof caught it — dragons and phoenixes worked in cut porcelain along the ridges, a garden of broken bowls made into heaven — and under the roof the granite of the courtyard stayed cold underfoot all day, a coldness Mei Foong privately considered part of the praying. Inside, the great coils of incense hung from the beams, turning smoke slowly in the light from the smoke-well. It was the oldest temple in Malacca, the aunties said, older than any of the families that prayed in it.

Ah Yoke was different here. The difference, when Mei Foong finally named it, was simple: everywhere else, Ah Yoke served. Here, she was served. She had combed up her hair before Guan Yin as a girl in Canton — sworn before the Goddess of Mercy never to marry, her plait coiled and pinned — and in the respect of the old nun who kept the side hall, in the particular nod of the keeper of lamps, Ah Yoke had standing. She bought oil for the lamps, lit her joss, and stood before Guan Yin with her feet together like a schoolgirl, and her lips moved a long time.

Then came Mei Foong's part, the part that made it a birthday. Ah Yoke bought a second bundle of joss and put it in her hands. "For your mother," she said, every year, the same three words, and stood beside her while she offered it; and afterwards there were longevity noodles at the stall outside the gate, mee sua in soup with one red-dyed egg, the whole indulgence of the year.

The year she turned sixteen there was one thing more.

After the joss, Ah Yoke did not turn toward the gate. She led the way instead to the side hall, where an old nun sat at a black table with a registry book, a brush, and a stone of ink, writing name-papers for the dead — the slips that would be read in the prayers, folded, and given at last to the fire, so that merit went where it was addressed. The nun wrote with an unhurried brush, first wetting it and drawing the strokes on the black table in water, a habit of testing, so that for a moment each name lived on the wood, shining, and then dried into nothing before she committed it to paper.

When their turn came, Ah Yoke put down her coins and gave the names of her dead in a low voice. Her father. Her mother. The surname Ho, then the given names; three characters each; the brush went down the paper like a man descending a stair. Then Ah Yoke said, "One more" — and instead of speaking, she took from inside her blouse a slip of red paper folded to the size of a thumbnail, soft as cloth from years of carrying, and opened it on the table. On it, in a hand that was not Ah Yoke's, were two characters.

The nun's brush waited. "Family name?"

"Write it as it is written," Ah Yoke said.

The nun looked at her for a moment — not unkindly; the look of a woman who had written ten thousand names and knew all the reasons a name might come in short — and wet her brush, and drew the two characters in water on the black table, moon and gold, and they shone there and began to dry, and then she wrote them on the paper, two characters alone at the top of the slip with the space above them empty where every other name in the pile began with a family.

Mei Foong stood very still. She was sixteen years old and she had never seen her mother's name written down. In Guangdong the sisterhood women had called her mother Ah Kim; her mother had been a voice and two hands and a smell of camphor and then a sickness and then a boat; and here was the name at last, moon and gold, and it was missing its first stone.

She said nothing in the hall. She carried the question out to the noodle stall, and held it while the mee sua came and the red egg, and Ah Yoke poured the tea, and finally she could not hold it.

"Ah Yee," she said — aunt; she had called Ah Yoke aunt since the boat — "the paper. My mother's paper. My grandfather and grandmother had three characters. My mother had two. You told the sifu to leave it. My mother was your sister. Why did you not write Ho?"

Ah Yoke ate a while. Around them the stall clattered, and the temple roof over the wall held up its garden of broken porcelain to the sun.

"Some names are prayers," Ah Yoke said. "Some names are debts."

She said it the way she counted rice into the bin, exact and final, and then she took the red egg and put it in Mei Foong's bowl, which was the end of the subject as surely as a door is the end of a corridor.

Mei Foong did not ask again. She had learned Ah Yoke's silences the way she had learned the account books: what was missing told you where to look, and also told you not to. But while the tea went cold she traced, with one finger in a spill of it on the marble table, the two characters the nun had tested in water — moon, gold — and watched them shine, and wiped them away with her sleeve before Ah Yoke could see, and found she had them by heart anyway, the way she had the unfinished story of the two sisters, one who stayed and one who ran. She was collecting these things now. No one was going to hand her the rest.

That was the year the house put Boon Siew into the firm.

He had finished at the High School in December with a certificate his father framed and a set of opinions his father did not, and in January he was given a desk in the firm's office by the river, between the chief clerk and the telephone. The office had a chalkboard on which the day's rubber prices were written and rubbed out and written again, a telephone line to the brokers in Singapore that crackled like frying, and Tan Hock Lye presiding with his abacus, buying and selling.

What he bought and sold, Boon Siew discovered, was mostly weather.

"We hold contracts for rubber that is still inside the trees," he told Mei Foong, the first month. "We sell it to men in Singapore who will never smell it, who sell it on to London before it is tapped. When the committee releases more quota, the price drops and my father groans; when they tighten it, he sings. Nothing anywhere has moved except a number on a board. This is called business."

It was a bad year for the singing. Prices had been sliding since before the New Year, and the Towkay stalked between chalkboard and telephone with the expression of a man at the cockfights insisting he was at a board meeting. Boon Siew did his work — he was too stubborn to do it badly — but he did it like a man carrying something at arm's length.

What he loved, he found at the other end of the firm, where the godown gave onto the river mouth. Malacca had no deep harbour; the steamers stood off in the roads, and the lighters went out to them loaded to the gunwales with smoked sheet, and Boon Siew would stand at the jetty checking cargo against the manifest long after the tally clerk had it done. Within a month he knew the ships in the roads by name, and where each was bound. Real things going to real places, he called it.

Mei Foong saw all this from the books, because the books came to her. Twice a week the ledgers were brought up to the house and she checked them at the long table in the back hall, as she had since she was fourteen, because she was never wrong. Now Boon Siew's entries began appearing among the clerks', a hand she would have known in the dark, and in the margins, in pencil, small enough that only someone who read every line would ever find them, the entries began to talk to her.

Beside the monthly account for the Bentley's petrol and polish — the Bentley no one in the household could drive, that went out only with a hired syce at New Year: a tiny pencilled bird, and the words *the merbok ate again.*

Beside a forward contract, rubber sold in June for October delivery at a price everyone in the office pretended to be certain of: *sold — four months of weather.*

She kept her face still the first time, and lost; the laugh got out of her sideways, half a snort, and she had to tell the second amah the clerks had entered a figure twice. After that she answered him. Her marks were smaller than his, and in ink, disguised as checking: a small character beside the weather contract, *wind* — as in, sold wind; a neat *water* beside a sum the flood had taken, because in this house even the losses had ancestors. He found them; she knew, because the next week's margins answered hers. Nobody watching them at dinner would have seen anything but the son of the house and the amah's girl. The conversation was elsewhere, in pencil and ink, an intimacy conducted entirely in the space where the accounts were ruled off, and Mei Foong, who owned nothing, owned it.

In April he was waiting for her by the account drawer, the fountain pen — the one that lived there as an unsettled account of its own — already out, and a sheet of paper folded twice.

"Read it," he said. "You first. Before anyone."

It was addressed to the editor of one of the Straits papers, and it was about rice.

It said, in English better than the High School had any right to take credit for, a simple thing: that Malaya planted rubber to the doorstep and bought its rice across the sea; that a country selling smoked rubber and buying its own dinner was rich exactly as long as the sea lanes stayed polite; and that anyone who thought sea lanes stayed polite forever should read the newspapers from China, any of them, any week. Let some rubber land go back to padi, the letter said. Let the Straits learn to feed the Straits. A garden that grows only flowers must beg for rice, however fine the flowers.

"They will say it is against the estates," Mei Foong said.

"It is against the estates. It is also true." He was watching her the way he watched the ships. "My father would call it schoolboy talk. If it carried my name, it would be the firm speaking — Tan Hock Lye's son says plant padi, sell the news to Singapore by lunchtime." He turned the pen in his fingers. "So it will not carry my name."

At the bottom, where a signature would go, he had written a single word: MERBOK.

Mei Foong looked at it and then at him, and the back stair was in both their faces, never once mentioned since — the merbok sings because it cannot do anything else, he had said, over the cage in the dark; and she had said, the bird eats every day.

"Let them think it is only singing," Boon Siew said.

She posted it herself. That was her own condition: not from the office, where the message boy read everything he carried; not from the house, where the mail went out through the chief clerk's hands; and never himself, because the counter clerks knew every face in the town, and the son of Tan Hock Lye buying a single stamp for a letter to a newspaper would be repeated at six kopitiam tables by evening. But an amah's girl posted household letters twice a week, and no one on earth looked at her twice. Invisibility, which she had worn four years like weather, turned out to be equipment.

"No one else," he said, at the drawer. "Not Bee Nio. Not anyone. Just us."

"Just the accounts," she said, and filed the letter among the household mail, and posted it on market morning from the counter on Kling Street between an order for sewing thread and the Bibik's monthly letter to the temple.

Three weeks later the paper printed it. MERBOK, Malacca, at the foot of the letters column, between a correspondent signing himself Pro Bono Publico on the subject of drains and a planter's long complaint about labour. Boon Siew laid the page where she would find it under the ledger, and stood at the window not looking at her while she read it. A month later a planter answered MERBOK with half a column of scorn — sentiment, schoolroom economics, does the correspondent propose we tap padi? — and Boon Siew answered the answer, and by the end of the year MERBOK had been called a dreamer, a menace, a merchant's catspaw, and, once, unforgivably, *sensible*; and the letters went out always the same way, from the girl nobody saw, with the household mail. In the margin of the December accounts, beside the entry for postage, he pencilled the smallest bird yet. She ruled the column off through it, the way you close a good month, and left one wingtip showing.

Bee Nio's storm broke in September.

The matchmaker had been coming to the house all year — a widow with a lacquered fan and a memory that held every marriageable person between Muar and Port Dickson filed by fortune — and photographs had gone out and come in, and Bee Nio had turned over each new one with a cinema queen's disdain that fooled everyone but the girl she slept top-to-toe with. Then one evening the fan stayed two hours, and the Bibik came out to the kitchen court with her face shining.

The name was Lim Cheng Hoe. A doctor, Straits-born on both sides, trained at the college in Singapore; twenty-six years old; known, it appeared, to everyone except Bee Nio, whose life he was to be.

Bee Nio raged for five days. It was magnificent and pitiable, and Mei Foong was the only audience for the true performance, given at night behind the mosquito net. A doctor! — a man who smelled of iodine and other people's illnesses. Twenty-six! — eleven years older; she did the arithmetic, made it worse than it was, and wept at her own result. She had seen him once, she claimed, and he wore *spectacles*, and his shoulders were disappointing. She would run away to Singapore and act in the pictures, where at least the men were chosen for their faces.

"You have not met him," Mei Foong said, on the fifth night.

"I have seen his photograph. His ears stick out."

"So does the temple roof, and you pray under it."

Bee Nio laughed before she could stop herself and was furious about it, and then, because it was late and dark, the real thing surfaced, the same white fish as the year before: I will belong to a house where I know nobody. Nobody will tell me what he is like when the guests go home; there is no way to find out; that is the whole trick of it. Her hand found Mei Foong's under the sheet, and Mei Foong held it and said what there was to say, which was nothing, and said it well.

The formal tea was fixed for October.

The house made ready as for a state visit. The Bibik was in her element: the best kamcheng jars came down and were washed; the pink nyonyaware tea service was counted twice, its saucers forbidden to the clumsy; three days of kueh-making filled the kitchen court, the Bibik commanding, tasting, rejecting a whole tray of kueh bangkit for being proud in the middle — and Mei Foong pounded and folded and steamed with the rest, and watched the house arrange itself into its finest lie, which was effortlessness.

The Lim party came at four: an uncle, an aunt, the matchmaker glowing with her fee, and the doctor. Mei Foong, carrying in the second tray, got her first look at the man from the doorway, and her honest first thought was that Bee Nio had been right about the ears.

Her second was that he could not stop being kind even for the length of a doorway: he had stood to take the tray of oranges from the Lims' own servant, who was old, before remembering himself, and given the whole room a small apologetic smile and sat down again.

The talk went as such talk goes, elders trading genealogy like commodities: this cousin, that estate, the weather, the rubber, the weather again. Bee Nio was brought out in blue kebaya to serve the tea, eyes down, movements drilled until they poured like syrup — and she did it beautifully, and the room admired her the way a room admires a vase, and Dr. Lim thanked her for his cup with exactly correct formality and did not stare, which Mei Foong marked in his favour.

It was the Towkay who set off the first joke, by observing there were a great many Lims in medicine.

"Four of us in this town alone," Dr. Lim agreed gravely. "Patients say you cannot so much as break a leg in Malacca without a Lim attending. I tell them that is only right and proper. A limb for a Lim. It is the fairest trade left on the Straits."

There was a silence of a certain shape while the room worked through it — the uncle got there first and produced a short bark, the matchmaker's fan went up like a shield — and Mei Foong watched Bee Nio's lowered profile and saw the corner of her mouth spasm once and be put down like a rebellion.

The tea went round again; it was Bee Nio's office to refill the doctor's cup, and perhaps because she was near, or perhaps because he was the kind of man whose nerves ran to jokes, Dr. Lim offered the company his second.

"My professors in Singapore despaired of me, you know," he said, to the room at large, confidingly. "They said I should never make a surgeon — I hadn't the stomach for it. I told them it hardly mattered." A pause; he was, Mei Foong saw with something near horror, timing it. "In surgery, the patient supplies the stomach."

The snort escaped Bee Nio complete. Tea went into the saucer. The Bibik's head turned with the slow inevitability of a lighthouse; the matchmaker's fan achieved full spread; and Dr. Lim produced a large white handkerchief, dropped it on the spill as if the fault were entirely the saucer's, and said to the Bibik, "Madam, forgive me. My jokes have injured better people than this. In my own defence, I warn everyone."

"He warned nobody," the uncle said.

"I am warning them now," said Dr. Lim.

And Bee Nio laughed — openly this time, three seconds of her real laugh, the loud one the convent school had failed to file down — and looked at the man she was to marry for the first time directly, and he looked back, spectacles flashing, pleased as a boy, and the delicate machinery of the afternoon wobbled, righted itself, and went on.

That night, top-to-toe under the net, Bee Nio was silent so long that Mei Foong thought she slept.

"The patient supplies the stomach," Bee Nio said at last, to the dark, in a tone of deep clinical disgust. "It is the worst joke I have ever heard in my life." A long pause. "He does that thing — he waits before the end. Like a fisherman." Another pause, longer. "His ears do stick out."

"Yes," said Mei Foong.

"I suppose somebody must marry him," Bee Nio said, with enormous indifference, and was asleep in a minute; and Mei Foong lay listening to her breathe and thought that of all the ways a fortress could be taken, laughter was the cheapest and the most complete.

The Bibik moved before the month was out. That was her way: sentiment in her was a signal for logistics, and a daughter successfully amused by her betrothed became, within days, a bride to be equipped, because the things that mattered could not be hurried and the Bibik knew to a day which things those were.

She summoned the seamstress on a Thursday, and with her, to the back hall where the light was best, Mei Foong.

The seamstress would cut and fit; velvet was discussed, and silk for the lining, and the lasts to be made to Bee Nio's foot; and then the Bibik set down on the table a pattern drawn in careful ink — peonies, full-headed, and a phoenix rising through them with its tail carried like a promise — and a shallow wooden tray in which beads lay sorted by colour, rust and rose and gold and jade and a blue that was almost night: glass seed beads, the fine ones you could no longer easily get, each smaller than a grain of padi, thousands upon thousands of them.

"The wedding kasut manek," the Bibik said. "You will bead them."

The seamstress's face performed a small economy of expressions and settled on relief. Everyone in the house had known since the year of the mended slipper which pair of hands under this roof did the finest work, though it was not said, because of what saying it would rearrange.

"A year, if you do not hurry," the Bibik said. "You will not hurry. One hour by daylight every day; I will tell the kitchen. If a bead sits wrong you will take out the row. I will look at them every new moon, and I will know." She paused, looking at the pattern and not at Mei Foong, and her voice moved some distance from command, into a country Mei Foong had never heard it enter. "Make them perfect. A bride walks her whole life in her first steps."

Then she was the Bibik again, and there were instructions about the tray, and damp, and lamps.

That evening, when the house had gone through its nightly diminishment — the seven doors barred, the kitchen court down to one lamp — Mei Foong sat at the long table with the tray before her and began, because beginnings were her own and she wanted this one to happen on a day nobody had chosen for her. Across the table Boon Siew sat with the fountain pen, drafting MERBOK's answer to a planter who had called self-sufficiency a fantasy for people who had never met a ledger; the pen scratched; the wall clock walked; neither of them spoke, and the silence was the good kind, the margin kind, wide enough for two.

She did not thread a needle that night. That was not how you began. She counted beads, sliding them across the partitions with the flat of a finger the way she carried figures across the abacus, learning the weight of what a year would be — a bead at a time, ten thousand small decisions adding up to another girl's first steps into her one life; and she found she did not mind, or rather that what she minded had folded itself neatly, as she had learned to fold everything, and gone in with the rest. Bee Nio would walk into her marriage on Mei Foong's year of evenings and never need to know it. That was what perfect meant. Perfect work is work that disappears into someone else's story.

Under the lamp the beads shone like wet ink, and for a moment she saw the old nun's brush again, moving on the black table at Cheng Hoon Teng — her mother's name drawn shining in water, moon and gold, whole for the length of a breath before it dried into the wood and was gone, and then written small on a paper with its first stone missing, and folded, and given to the fire.

Some names are prayers, some are debts. She had turned the sentence over for months the way you turn a coin found in a drain, and tonight, counting another bride's beads in a sleeping house, she thought she understood which kind goes unwritten. Prayers you say aloud. Debts you carry quietly, and pay, and pay, until the day someone opens a ledger and finds the whole account, kept all along in a small upright hand, waiting to be read.

She slid the first row of gold into its channel, even as a rank of soldiers, and put the cover on the tray.
