# The Amah's Shop

The signwriter finished a little after noon and backed into the road to admire himself; and the board above the door said, in gold leaf on black lacquer, HO MEI FOONG, and beneath that, in four characters, BEADED SLIPPERS, and beneath that nothing at all.

"No prosperity in it," he said sadly. He had proposed, across three visits, three prosperous names. "Nobody puts a woman's own name over a door."

"Then it will be easy to find," Mei Foong said, and paid him, and went in.

It was January of 1949, and she was twenty-six. The counter had come out of a chandler's that closed after the war: twelve feet of teak, glass-topped, the glass gone green at the edges, and under the glass, in trays made from cigar boxes and stiff card, the beads — rust, gold, jade and the blue that was almost night first, because those were the ones her hands went to without asking, then everything else, running colour into colour along the whole length. Behind it, hooks and more hooks, waiting for lasts. In the window, one finished vamp under glass: a peony opened full, rust shading into gold, the phoenix beginning to climb out of the flowers at the edge of the frame and going nowhere, because the frame ended. She had put it there for the people who could not read the board.

Her day had a shape by then and kept it for eight years. Up at half past four; out of her own door at twenty to six with the street dark and the drains still cool; four doors up past the kopitiam and the goldsmith's grille to the mansion, where the side gate was unbolted for her by a kitchen girl born the year of the surrender. She woke the child, or found her already awake reading under the sheet with a torch, punishable in that house, and twice punished by a woman who then bought her a better torch. She plaited the hair. She walked her daughter to the convent gate and stood on the far side of the road, as a servant stands, until the small blue-and-white figure had gone in among the others; and then she came back down the street and turned her own sign and was something else until three o'clock.

The Rajus sent coffee at seven that first morning, unasked, and every working morning after, for fifty years. The old man did not like to see a shop open, he said, with nothing in it but hope.

---

The first apprentice was not an apprentice at all. She was Sister Kam, sixty-one, a majie of Ah Yoke's lodge, whose hands had gone at forty from twenty-two years of other people's linen, and who arrived at the door in the black-and-white with her life in a rattan case because the sisterhood had told her to. "They said you would find me something," she said, without hope. Mei Foong looked at the hands — knuckles swollen, fingertips gone smooth and shiny, no needle ever to sit in them again — and asked whether she could see colour, and was told that she could.

"Then sit here and do not let anyone move these trays."

That was the arrangement for eleven years, and Mei Foong never once let it be called charity in her hearing. Sister Kam sorted. She counted stock, watched the door, poured tea for the mothers, learned to say the price and let the silence after it do the work — the one thing Mei Foong could never do — and built a system of tobacco tins and paper packets and pencilled numbers that outlived her by forty years. The colour rows under the glass were hers. She had never owned anything either.

After Kam came the girls: two, then four, then never fewer than three, sent along the majie network the way a bucket goes along a fire line — nieces from villages the sisterhood still wrote to, daughters of amahs whose households had never recovered from the occupation, girls with nowhere, who could be fed and taught a trade in four years. Kwai Lan came in the second year: nineteen, from a smallholding past Jasin, hands so fast that the first instruction was to slow them and the second was to unpick everything done since the first. Poh Choo came in the third: fifteen, silent, so slow that Kwai Lan called her the tortoise, until the day Mei Foong held their two vamps up to the window and said, "That one you will sell. That one you will sell twice," and never said which was which, because they both knew.

She taught them the way she had been taught, which was badly, in the sense that nobody had taught her at all. Never haul a stitch; let it settle. If the light is bad, stop — a bad hour costs a good week. And on Saturdays, wages: coins counted onto the glass, every girl's tied before it went into her pocket with a length of red silk thread off the hank in the drawer. Everything in that drawer was Ah Yoke's, except the green mottled fountain pen, which nobody was permitted to touch, and which Mei Foong used every night to write the day's figures in a ledger nobody audited.

*Dear husband. I have four women in the front room and none of them can be spared. The signwriter wanted to call the shop Prosperity, and I would not, and I am afraid he was right and I was only proud. The child is seventh in her class in arithmetic and first in argument. Yesterday she informed a teacher that a rule which cannot say why it exists is only a habit wearing a uniform. She is yours all the way down. — Your wife, who has a name over a door.*

---

Siew Ean came back to Malacca in July of 1950 and did not say she was staying. She had taken a post teaching across the padang; it was temporary; Ipoh was finished for her anyway, the dredges sold, her father four years dead. She said all of it at the counter in a schoolteacher's white blouse without once putting down her umbrella.

"Sit," said Mei Foong.

"I am not stopping."

"Sit anyway."

She stopped two hours, came back on the Thursday, and after the fourth gave up announcing that she was not stopping. In September she looked at the ledger, the way a doctor looks at a wound the patient has been dressing herself.

"Your hand is beautiful. Your figures are honest. Your prices are an insult."

"They pay them."

"They pay them because you name them apologetically. A full pair, peony-and-phoenix — four hundred hours if it is one. You charged the Ong woman thirty-eight dollars. The tailor on Jonker Street charged her twenty-two for the kebaya and it took him nine days. You are not selling slippers, you fool. You are selling four months of your eyesight. Charge sixty."

"And if she goes elsewhere?"

"Then she wears somebody else's peony to her own wedding and is quietly sad about it for forty years." Siew Ean took out her own pen, the fast slanted hand, ruled a column at the back of the ledger and headed it HOURS. "Every job. Every girl's hours separately, and yours, and I want the eyesight priced or I shall lose my temper. Give me the book on Thursdays."

"I cannot pay you."

"I have a salary and nobody to spend it on, and I am extremely bored on Thursdays. You may feed me. Not kueh — everyone in this town feeds me kueh; I am the town spinster; I am fed like a hen."

So it began and so it went on: Thursday evenings at the end of the counter with the lamp pulled over, the two of them arguing across the glass about money, about the girls, about everything except the one thing, which sat between them permanently and needed no mentioning. She began saying we about the shop in the second year and never noticed. Mei Foong noticed.

Once, late, Siew Ean looked up from the hours. "Do you know what you have made here?"

"A shop."

"A household," said Siew Ean, and went back to her figures. "Which is the only kind either of us was ever going to get. You may not thank me for the observation."

"I don't."

"Good. Pass the sand."

---

The pattern got its name from a woman from Seremban who stood at the counter forty minutes, unable to decide.

"That one," she said at last, pointing at the framed vamp in the window. "The Malacca one."

It was not the Malacca one. It had come to Malacca in 1934 in the head of a twelve-year-old girl off the Guangdong boat, who had it from a woman who died in a village north of Canton, who had it from her own mother in a kitchen at the top of this same street forty years before that; and the only two people who had ever ground that pattern into their hands together were two sisters, one of them ash on a Guangdong hillside with no tablet anywhere, the other four doors up this street, dead the year before in her own bed.

Mei Foong took the frame down and set it on the glass and said, "Yes. That is the Malacca pattern," and quoted sixty dollars without apologising.

By 1952 they were coming from Muar and from Seremban and once, memorably, from Penang, which thinks it invented everything; and the mothers said Malacca pattern in three dialects as though the town had always made it. Every bride had her feet measured on the shop floor, Mei Foong kneeling with tape and pencil and newspaper, and every bride got a last: a wooden foot turned in Bandar Hilir at a dollar-fifty the pair, marked in ink with a name and a date, hung on the wall afterwards and never thrown away. The wall filled, and went on filling for forty years, small to smaller, until the rows had to go above the doorway.

---

The Emergency came into the shop the way it came into everything on that street: sideways, as paperwork and hours. Curfew meant the lamp went out at a set time and the girls who lived out went home before it. Roadblocks meant stock from Singapore took nine days instead of four, and the interior bus travelled in convoy in the middle of the day, and everybody knew the constables at the Bukit Katil block by sight, and everyone was polite, because everyone was afraid.

In April of 1951 Kwai Lan's brother was taken. He was a lorry attendant on the Jasin run, twenty-two; he was taken at a block on a Tuesday and not seen for five weeks. There was no charge and no news; there was a form, an office, and a queue of women outside it, his mother among them every Monday with a tiffin carrier that was never accepted. Nobody in the shop asked what he had done, because nobody knew, and because the question had an ugly shape. Mei Foong put Kwai Lan on plain black ground for five weeks — no counting, no colour, the kind of stitching hands can do while the head is elsewhere — kept her wages coming, and said nothing beyond, once: "Eat something. He will need you fed."

He came back in May, thinner, and went back on the lorries, and never told his sister one word about where he had been, and his sister never asked. There were men in the jungle and men in the police and men who were simply on the road at the wrong hour, and the street held no opinions out loud; the street had a curfew and a ration and a queue, and got on.

The wedding at the new village was in the March after. The bride's family had been moved off their smallholding the year before along with everyone in that valley, resettled behind wire past Jasin; the wedding was to be inside the fence, and the slippers — ordered eight months earlier, paid in two instalments — had to get there. It took eleven days to arrange, and Siew Ean did most of it: a pass from the District Office for one Ho Mei Foong, beadworker, to enter the area between eight and five on a stated day, a seat on the convoy bus, and a permit for the goods.

At the gate the constable turned the basket out onto a trestle table in the sun: two slippers, tissue, a card of spare beads, a needle case, a cold bun. He picked up the left slipper and turned it over twice, entirely at a loss — a boy of about twenty, rifle slung, collar dark with sweat.

"What is this for?"

"A wedding, tuan."

He turned it once more, and something moved in his face — an aunt, a sister, some other kitchen a long way off — and he set it down on the tissue far more carefully than he had picked it up, and waved her through, and shouted at the next in the queue about rice.

Inside the wire the lanes were new and raw and laid out straight, and there was a wedding: a house of unseasoned planks with a red cloth over the door, forty people, a gramophone, and a girl of nineteen who put the slippers on in the back room and stood up and looked down at her feet a long time without saying anything.

*Dear husband. A girl was married today behind a wire fence, in shoes I made, and she looked at her feet the way your sister did in 'thirty-nine, and it is the same look in every year and no government has yet found a way to tax it. They have made the whole country carry papers proving who it is. I carry mine. It is entirely accurate and completely false and nobody will ever check it, and that is the joke, and there is nobody left to tell it to but you. — Your wife, licensed to enter and depart between eight and five.*

---

Mei Lan began coming to the shop after school in the year she turned nine, and by ten it had stopped being a thing anyone announced. The mansion four doors up had a cook, a piano, a grandfather who kept sweets in a drawer and handed them out one at a time like receipts, and a mother at home most afternoons. The shop had beads. She came down the five-foot way with her satchel banging her knee, said "Auntie Foong, I have finished everything," which was true perhaps half the time, and was at the counter with a tray in front of her before anyone answered.

She was ten and she was, everyone agreed, a trial. She wanted to know why Poh Choo was paid less than Kwai Lan when Poh Choo's work was better, and would not accept years as an answer, and won; the following Saturday Poh Choo's wage went up eighty cents and nobody was told why.

And she beaded. She had wanted the tray from the day she was tall enough to reach it; she was quick and neat and she settled her stitches instead of hauling them, without ever having been told — and when she leaned over the frame she put her left hand flat on the black cloth with the thumb tucked under, which is not a thing anybody teaches, and which the apprentices saw, and Sister Kam saw, and about which nobody in that room ever said one word in eleven years.

A bride's mother said once, comfortably, from the customer's side of the glass, that the child had good hands. "She has been taught," said Mei Foong.

It was a Thursday in the November she turned ten. The lamp was on early because the sky had gone the low iron colour it goes before the year's last rains; Siew Ean was at the far end of the counter with the hours column; Kwai Lan and Poh Choo were at the frames by the window; and Mei Lan had the small tray with a practice vamp on it, a peony in its second row, worked in the cheap beads that were for mistakes.

She had been quiet a long while. Then she sat back on her stool and looked at the two rows on the cloth, and at Mei Foong's hands on the larger frame beside her, and said:

"Auntie Foong. Why do I bead like you?"

Behind her, in the doorway, with the sky going iron over Heeren Street, Bee Nio stopped.

She had come for the child as she did two or three times a week, with her umbrella and her key basket; and she stopped one step inside the door with the light behind her and did not come further, and did not speak, and did not go. Kwai Lan glanced up and went back to her frame. Siew Ean did not look up at all; her pen stopped, and stayed stopped, over the column.

Mei Foong finished the stitch she was in. She let it settle. Then she laid the needle along the top of the frame, which she had never once done in the middle of a row in her life, and turned on her stool to face the child.

"Because I taught you," she said. "That is what teachers are for."

Mei Lan considered it. She had her father's way of taking an answer apart in her hands before deciding whether to keep it, and Mei Foong let her, and did not breathe.

"Chai Hoon's mother taught her the piano," Mei Lan said at last, "and Chai Hoon plays nothing like her mother."

"Then her mother is a worse teacher than I am."

The child laughed — the loud unlovely honk the convent was working on and nobody in the shop wanted corrected — and picked up her needle and went back into the second row of the peony, and the moment closed over like water.

Mei Foong looked up. Across eleven feet of shop floor and ten years, Bee Nio was looking at her, and had been the whole time, and her face was doing something complicated and not at all vain. Then she came the rest of the way in and set her basket on the glass and said, in an ordinary voice:

"Sifu. My daughter's shoes are through at the toe again. What can be done with a child who walks on the front of her feet?"

"Nothing," said Mei Foong. "It runs in the family."

Siew Ean's pen started up again.

---

They stood together on the five-foot way afterwards while the child collected her satchel, in the smell of rain arriving over the sea.

"I nearly spoke," Bee Nio said.

"I know."

"For four years I have been —" she stopped, and did the thing she had learned in the occupation, which was to say the worst figure first. "Crowding you. The school. The dresses. The temple, the church, the piano. Because while I am deciding it I am her mother in my own head for the length of the deciding." She looked out at the road. "Cheng Hoe told me a year ago and I said he was talking rubbish, and then did not speak to him for two days, which is how he knew he was right."

Mei Foong said nothing, which was an answer.

"Tuesdays and Thursdays she is yours," Bee Nio said. "Not sent. Yours. And the beading is yours entirely and I will not have an opinion about it." She picked up her basket. "And you will tell me when I am doing it again. Out loud. In words. You have a bad habit of forgiving people silently and it is very expensive to live with."

"Yes," said Mei Foong.

"Say one now."

"You are doing it about her hair. She is ten. She can plait her own hair. You do it because it is four minutes in the morning when she cannot go anywhere."

Bee Nio stood very still for a second. Then she made a small sound that was almost the old amusement-park laugh. "Four minutes. That is exactly what it is. Nobody in this town sees anything except you." And then, briskly, to the child coming out with her satchel: "Say thank you to Auntie Foong."

"Thank you, Auntie Foong."

"Go carefully," said Mei Foong, and stood in her own doorway under her own name and watched them up the street, the woman and the girl, until the goldsmith's grille took them out of sight.

---

Dr Lim's heart went in the second week of December, in the surgery, at a quarter past four, between a boil and a vaccination certificate.

Mei Foong was there because he had asked her to hold the arm of a frightened old man while he lanced something. She saw the colour leave his face, and saw his left hand go flat and open on the desk instead of gripping, and she had him on the floor with his collar open before he had finished saying that it was nothing, it was the fish at lunch.

Grey. Sweating. The pulse under her fingers doing something disorderly and fast. What she knew of this she had learned from him, in an occupation dispensary at night, when his only second pair of hands had been hers; and she used it now, flat on his own linoleum, and sent the compounder for the doctor two streets down and Kwai Lan for Bee Nio, and knelt there talking to him steadily about nothing at all — the stock, the Seremban order, the price of glass beads — because his eyes kept coming to her face and going away and coming back.

He did not die. He was fifty-three; it was, the specialist said in January, a warning, and warnings are given to be used. He was to give up the night calls, the cigarettes and eleven committees, and he gave up the cigarettes.

Mei Lan came into the shop the following evening and stood in the middle of the floor with her satchel still on her shoulder.

"He is not to walk upstairs for a fortnight," she said. Her voice was perfectly level, which frightened Mei Foong more than crying would have; the child had her father's habit of going quiet and orderly at the top of a fall. "Mother says it is nothing. When people say it is nothing they mean they have counted something."

"They mean they are frightened," said Mei Foong. "Come here."

The girl came, and stood, and after a moment leaned her weight against Mei Foong's side at the end of the counter, in front of forty thousand beads; and Mei Foong put her arm round her daughter in her own shop, under her own roof, which she was permitted to do, and which she did.

---

Ten days later she was sent for at the mansion.

The old man was in the account room with the shutters half closed against the glare, and a stranger with him: a thin clerk from the solicitor's up the street, upright, a case on his knees. The Towkay was seventy-one. He had a stick now, which he used as a pointer and not as a stick.

"You will write," he said. "Your hand is legible. His" — the stick indicated the clerk without looking at him — "is a lawyer's."

She sat down at the black desk where she had sat for nineteen years, took the green pen from her own pocket — the drawer it once lived in had been empty since she moved out — and drew a clean sheet towards her.

He dictated for an hour and twenty minutes: the estate, item by item, in the order he had acquired it. The rubber acreage at Machap. The godowns. The shares. The two lorries and the Bentley he had never learned to drive. The mansion. Then, more slowly, the dispositions: this to a nephew, this in trust, this and this to his daughter Bee Nio, with clauses about her husband's health that he had plainly been thinking about at night.

It was a schedule, not a will. But she had kept this man's books through a bankruptcy, an occupation and a peace, and she could read the shape of his mind off a schedule.

"There is nothing here for the granddaughter," she said, when he stopped.

"No," said the Towkay. He was looking at the shutters. "There is not, and I have thought about it a year, and I like none of the arrangements. The girl is ten. She is the last of me. If I leave it to her father it goes to his family; if I leave it to her mother it goes to her father; if I leave it to the girl outright, some man will marry it off her in fifteen years and I shall be dead and unable to say anything about it, which is the part I find intolerable." He turned his head then and looked at Mei Foong, once, flatly, across the desk where he had first found her useful when she was twelve; and neither of them said anything about anything.

"Take a clean sheet," he said. "We begin again, and this time we begin from the child and work outwards. Head it."

She took a clean sheet, and squared it, and dipped the pen.

"For my granddaughter," said the old man.

And Mei Foong wrote it down in her small upright hand, exactly as given, without a tremor, because thirty years in that house had trained her hand not to tremble; and the clerk's pen scratched, and the shutters ticked in the heat, and the rain began on the tiles — and four doors down the street, on the wall of a shop with her own name over the door, her daughter's last hung in the third row, second from the end, marked in ink: *Mei Lan, 1952.*
