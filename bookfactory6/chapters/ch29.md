# The Amah's Shop

The signwriter finished a little after noon and backed off the five-foot way into the road to admire himself; and the board above the door said, in gold leaf on black lacquer, HO MEI FOONG, and beneath that, in four characters, BEADED SLIPPERS, and beneath that nothing at all.

"No prosperity in it," he said sadly. He had proposed, across three visits, Kim Hoe, Guan Seng, Eng Aik — names with money folded into them the way a dispenser folds a powder. "Nobody puts a woman's own name over a door."

"Then it will be easy to find," Mei Foong said, and paid him, and went in.

It was January of 1949, and she was twenty-six. The counter had come out of a chandler's that closed after the war: twelve feet of teak, glass-topped, the glass gone green at the edges like river water, and under the glass, in trays made from cigar boxes and stiff card, the beads — rust, gold, jade, the blue that was almost night first, because those were the ones her hands went to without asking, then everything else, running colour into colour along the whole length. Behind the counter, hooks and more hooks, waiting for lasts. In the window, on a stand, one finished vamp under glass: a peony opened full, rust shading into gold, the phoenix beginning to climb out of the flowers at the edge of the frame and going nowhere, because the frame ended. She had put it there for the people who could not read the board.

Her day had a shape by then and kept that shape for eight years. Up at half past four; out of her own door at twenty to six with the street dark and the drains still cool; four doors up past the kopitiam and the goldsmith's grille to the mansion, where the side gate was unbolted for her by a kitchen girl born the year of the surrender who thought the war had been a kind of weather. She woke the child, or found her already awake reading under the sheet with a torch, which was a punishable offence in that house and had been punished twice by a woman who then bought her a better torch. She plaited the hair. She checked the shoes and the ribbon, which was blue for weekdays. She walked her daughter to the convent gate and stood on the far side of the road, as a servant stands, until the small blue-and-white figure had gone in among the others; and then she came back down the street to her own door and turned the sign and was something else until three o'clock.

The Rajus sent coffee at seven that first morning, unasked, and every working morning after, for fifty years.

"My father says it is on the house until the shop pays," said the Raju boy, who was twenty-four and newly serious, having taken the till off an uncle who drank. He set the cup down and looked round the bare room with frank interest. "He does not like to see a shop open with nothing in it but hope."

"Tell your father there are eleven thousand beads in it."

"He will still send the coffee," said the Raju boy.

---

The first apprentice was not an apprentice at all. She was Sister Kam, sixty-one, a majie of Ah Yoke's lodge, whose hands had gone at forty from twenty-two years of laundering other people's linen, and who arrived at the door in the black-and-white with her life in a rattan case because the sisterhood had told her to.

"They said you would find me something," she said, without hope.

Mei Foong looked at the hands: knuckles swollen, fingertips gone smooth and shiny. No needle would ever sit in them again.

"Can you see colour?"

"I can see colour."

"Then sit here and do not let anyone move these trays."

That was the arrangement for eleven years, and Mei Foong never once let it be called charity in her hearing. Sister Kam sorted. She counted stock, watched the door, poured tea for the mothers, learned to say the price and let the silence after it do the work — the one thing Mei Foong could never do — and built, out of nothing, a system of tobacco tins and folded paper packets and pencilled numbers that outlived her by forty years. The colour rows under the glass were hers. Sister Kam had never owned anything either.

After Kam came the girls: two, then four, then never fewer than three, sent along the majie network the way a bucket goes along a fire line. Nieces from villages the sisterhood still wrote to. Daughters of amahs whose households had gone bankrupt in the occupation and never recovered. Girls with nowhere, who could be fed on a slow apprentice's wage and taught a trade in four years. Kwai Lan came in the second year: nineteen, from a smallholding out past Jasin, hands so fast that Mei Foong's first instruction was to slow them and her second was to unpick everything done since the first. Poh Choo came in the third: fifteen, silent, so slow that Kwai Lan called her the tortoise until the day the two of them finished the same vamp within an hour of each other and Mei Foong held both up to the window and said, "That one you will sell. That one you will sell twice," and never explained which was which, because they knew.

She taught them the way she had been taught, which was badly, in the sense that nobody had taught her at all. Count the stair-step off the petal opposite: three, two, two, one. Never haul a stitch; let it settle. If the light is bad, stop — a bad hour costs a good week. And on Saturdays, wages: coins counted onto the glass, and every girl's wage tied, before it went into her pocket, with a length of red silk thread off the hank in the drawer. Everything in that drawer was Ah Yoke's — the thread, the awl, the small blunt scissors — everything but the green mottled fountain pen, which no one was permitted to touch, and which Mei Foong used every night to write the day's figures in a ledger nobody audited.

The registration came that year: the whole country queuing to be photographed and thumb-printed and made into a card, and nobody to be on a road without it. Mei Foong shut the shop for a morning and queued with them for three hours. When it was Sister Kam's turn she went stiff at the camera, and afterwards held the little card with its grey square of her own face for a long moment, and put it away inside her blouse, and said nothing.

She was sixty-one, and it was the first photograph anyone had ever taken of her.

*Dear husband. I have four women in the front room and none of them can be spared. The signwriter wanted to call the shop Prosperity, and I would not, and now I am afraid he was right and I was only proud. The child is seventh in her class in arithmetic and first in argument. Yesterday she told her teacher that a rule which cannot say why it exists is only a habit wearing a uniform, and the teacher wrote to her mother, and her mother laughed for a quarter of an hour in the pantry where nobody could hear, and then wrote a very correct letter of apology. She is yours all the way down. — Your wife, who has a name over a door.*

---

Siew Ean came back to Malacca in July of 1950 and did not say she was staying. She had taken a post — English and mathematics, at a girls' school on the far side of the padang — it was a temporary arrangement, and Ipoh was finished for her anyway, the dredges sold, the house sold, her father four years dead. She said all of it standing at the counter in a schoolteacher's white blouse without once putting down her umbrella.

"Sit," said Mei Foong.

"I am not stopping."

"Sit anyway."

She stopped for two hours, and came back on the Thursday, and after the fourth Thursday she stopped announcing that she was not stopping. It took her until September to look at the ledger, and she looked at it the way a doctor looks at a wound the patient has been dressing herself.

"Your hand is beautiful. Your figures are honest. Your prices are an insult."

"They pay them."

"They pay them because you name them apologetically." Siew Ean turned the book round. "A full pair, peony-and-phoenix, black ground, four hundred hours if it is one. You charged the Ong woman thirty-eight dollars. The tailor on Jonker Street charged her twenty-two for the kebaya and it took him nine days." She let the silence sit, which she had learned from Sister Kam. "You are not selling slippers, you fool. You are selling four months of your eyesight. Charge sixty."

"And if she goes elsewhere?"

"Then she wears somebody else's peony to her own wedding and is quietly sad about it for forty years." Siew Ean took out her own pen, the fast slanted hand, ruled a new column at the back of the ledger and headed it HOURS. "Every job. Every girl's hours separately, and yours, and I want the eyesight priced or I shall lose my temper. Give me the book on Thursdays."

"I cannot pay you."

"I have a salary and no one to spend it on, and I am extremely bored on Thursdays. You may feed me. Not kueh. Everyone in this town feeds me kueh; I am the town spinster; I am fed like a hen."

So it began and so it went on: Thursday evenings at the end of the counter with the lamp pulled over, the schoolteacher doing the shop's books with a rigour that frightened the apprentices, the two of them arguing across the glass about money, about the girls, about whether Poh Choo should be allowed to sign her own work — about everything except the one thing, which sat between them permanently and did not need mentioning. Siew Ean had a tongue like a bill of lading. She called the shop a charity for the sisterhood; she called Mei Foong a soft touch, a martyr, an idiot with a needle. She began saying we about the shop in the second year and never noticed. Mei Foong noticed.

Once, late, Siew Ean looked up from the column of hours. "Do you know what you have made here?"

"A shop."

"A household," said Siew Ean, and went back to her figures. "Which is the only kind either of us was ever going to get. You may not thank me for the observation."

"I don't."

"Good. Pass the sand."

---

The pattern got its name from a woman from Seremban who came down on the bus with her sister and stood at the counter forty minutes, unable to decide.

"That one," she said at last, pointing at the framed vamp in the window. "The Malacca one."

It was not the Malacca one. It had come to Malacca in 1934 in the head of a twelve-year-old girl off the Guangdong boat, who had it from a woman who died in a village north of Canton, who had it from her own mother in a kitchen at the top of this same street forty years before that; and the only two people who had ever ground that pattern into their hands together were two sisters, one of them upstairs in the mansion four doors away, deaf now and dead within the year, and the other ash on a Guangdong hillside with no tablet anywhere. Mei Foong took the frame down and set it on the glass and said, "Yes. That is the Malacca pattern," and quoted sixty dollars without apologising.

By 1952 they were coming from Muar and from Seremban and once, memorably, from Penang, which thinks it invented everything; and the brides' mothers said Malacca pattern in three dialects as though the town had always made it, and the town believed them inside four years, the way towns do. Every bride had her feet measured on the shop floor, Mei Foong kneeling with tape and pencil and a sheet of newspaper, and every bride got a last: a wooden foot turned by a man in Bandar Hilir at a dollar-fifty the pair, marked in ink with a name and a date, hung on the wall afterwards and never thrown away. The wall filled up, and went on filling for forty years, small to smaller, until the last rows had to go above the doorway.

They began calling her sifu in the third year. She heard it first through the doorway, Kwai Lan saying it to a stranger without emphasis, the way you say a word you have been using for months; and Mei Foong went on counting stock and did not look up, and put the moment away somewhere private with the very few others.

---

The Emergency came into the shop the way it came into everything on that street: sideways, as paperwork and hours.

Curfew meant the lamp went out at a set time, and the girls who lived out went home before it. Roadblocks meant stock from Singapore took nine days instead of four, and the interior bus travelled in convoy in the middle of the day, and everybody knew the constables at the Bukit Katil block by sight and the constables knew them, and everyone was polite, because everyone was afraid, and politeness was the cheapest protection available.

In April of 1951 Kwai Lan's brother was taken. He was a lorry attendant on the Jasin run, twenty-two; he was taken at a block on a Tuesday and not seen for five weeks. There was no charge and no news; there was a form, and an office, and a queue outside the office of women exactly like Kwai Lan's mother, who went in every Monday with a tiffin carrier that was never accepted. Nobody in the shop asked what he had done, because nobody knew, and because the question had an ugly shape to it. Mei Foong put Kwai Lan on plain black ground for five weeks — no counting, no colour, the kind of stitching hands can do while the head is elsewhere — kept her wages coming, and said nothing about any of it beyond, once, at the end of a long evening: "Eat something. He will need you fed."

He came back in May, thinner, and went back on the lorries, and never told his sister one word about where he had been, and his sister never asked. That was the whole of it. There were men in the jungle and men in the police and men who were simply on the road at the wrong hour, and the street held no opinions out loud; the street had a curfew and a ration and a queue, and got on.

The wedding at the new village was in the March after. The bride's family had been moved off their smallholding the year before along with everyone in that valley, resettled behind wire up past Jasin; the wedding was to be inside the fence, and the slippers — ordered eight months earlier, paid in two instalments — had to get there.

It took eleven days to arrange, and Siew Ean did most of it: a pass from the District Office for one Ho Mei Foong, beadworker, to enter the resettlement area between eight and five on a stated day; a seat on the mid-morning bus that went with the convoy; a permit for the goods, because you may not carry an unexplained parcel through a gate like that. At the gate the constable turned the basket out onto a trestle table in the sun: two slippers, tissue, a card of spare beads, a needle case, a cold bun. He picked up the left slipper and turned it over twice, entirely at a loss — a boy of about twenty, rifle slung, collar dark with sweat.

"What is this for?"

"A wedding, tuan."

He turned it once more, and something moved in his face — an aunt, a sister, some other kitchen a long way off — and he set it down on the tissue far more carefully than he had picked it up, and waved her through, and shouted at the next person in the queue about rice.

Inside the wire the lanes were new and raw and laid out straight, and there was a wedding: a house of unseasoned planks with a red cloth over the door, forty people, a gramophone, and a girl of nineteen who put the slippers on in the back room with three women holding her elbows, and stood up, and looked down at her own feet for a long time without saying anything at all.

Mei Foong caught the four o'clock bus back to make the curfew. At the Bukit Katil block everyone got out and showed their cards and got in again.

*Dear husband. A girl was married today behind a wire fence, in shoes I made, and she looked at her feet the way your sister did in 'thirty-nine, and it is the same look in every year and no government has yet found a way to tax it. They have made the whole country carry papers proving who it is. I carry mine. It is entirely accurate and completely false and nobody will ever check it, and that is the joke, and there is nobody left to tell it to but you. — Your wife, licensed to enter and depart between eight and five.*

---

Mei Lan began coming to the shop after school in the year she turned nine, and by ten it had stopped being a thing anyone announced.

The convent let out at one. The mansion was four doors up and had a cook, a piano, a grandfather who kept sweets in a drawer and handed them out one at a time like receipts, and a mother at home most afternoons. The shop had beads. She came down the five-foot way with her satchel banging her knee, put her head round the door, said "Auntie Foong, I have finished everything," which was true perhaps half the time, and was at the end of the counter with a tray in front of her before anyone answered.

She was ten and she was, everyone agreed, a trial. She wanted to know why Poh Choo was paid less than Kwai Lan when Poh Choo's work was better, and would not accept years as an answer, and was still saying so at the end of an argument in which Mei Foong found herself genuinely on the losing side and had to say so; the following Saturday Poh Choo's wage went up eighty cents and nobody was told why. She read the ledger upside down, which is a skill, and corrected an addition in it, and was right. She could not be got to hold the needle at the correct angle for three months, and then, one Tuesday, held it correctly forever.

And she beaded. That was the thing. She wanted the tray from the day she was tall enough to reach it; she was quick and she was neat and she settled her stitches instead of hauling them, without ever having been told — and when she leaned over the frame she put her left hand flat on the black cloth with the thumb tucked under, which is not a thing anybody teaches, and which the apprentices saw, and which Sister Kam saw, and about which none of the three women in that room ever said one word to each other in eleven years.

"She has good hands," a bride's mother said once, comfortably, from the customer's side of the glass.

"She has been taught," said Mei Foong.

It was a Thursday in the November she turned ten. The lamp was on early because the sky had gone the low iron colour it goes before the year's last rains; Siew Ean was at the far end of the counter with the hours column; Kwai Lan and Poh Choo were at the frames by the window; and Mei Lan had the small tray with a practice vamp on it, a peony in its second row, worked in the cheap opaque beads that were for mistakes.

She had been quiet a long while. Then she sat back on her stool and looked at the two rows on the cloth, and at Mei Foong's hands on the larger frame beside her, and said:

"Auntie Foong. Why do I bead like you?"

Behind her, in the doorway, with the sky going iron over Heeren Street, Bee Nio stopped.

She had come down for the child, as she came down two or three times a week, in her good sarong with her umbrella and her key basket, thirty years old, a doctor's wife, a woman with a household; and she stopped one step inside the door with the light behind her and did not come further, and did not speak, and did not go. Kwai Lan glanced up and went back to her frame. Siew Ean did not look up at all; her pen stopped, and stayed stopped, over the column.

Mei Foong finished the stitch she was in. She let it settle. Then she laid the needle along the top of the frame, which she had never once done in the middle of a row in her life, and turned on her stool to face the child.

"Because I taught you," she said. "That is what teachers are for."

Mei Lan considered it. She had her father's way of taking an answer apart in her hands before deciding whether to keep it, and Mei Foong sat and let her do it, and did not breathe.

"Chai Hoon's mother taught her the piano," Mei Lan said at last, "and Chai Hoon plays nothing like her mother."

"Then her mother is a worse teacher than I am."

The child laughed — the loud unlovely honk the convent was working on, and that nobody in the shop had ever wanted corrected — and picked up her needle and went back into the second row of the peony, and the moment closed over like water.

Mei Foong looked up. Across eleven feet of shop floor and ten years, Bee Nio was looking at her, and had been looking at her the whole time, and her face was doing something complicated and mostly hidden and not at all vain; and neither of them moved. Then Bee Nio came the rest of the way in and set her basket on the glass and said, in an ordinary voice:

"Sifu. My daughter's shoes are through at the toe again. What can be done with a child who walks on the front of her feet?"

"Nothing," said Mei Foong. "It runs in the family."

Siew Ean's pen started up again.

---

They stood together on the five-foot way afterwards while the child collected her satchel, in the smell of rain arriving over the sea.

"I nearly spoke," Bee Nio said.

"I know."

"For four years I have been —" she stopped, and did the thing she had learned in the occupation, which was to say the worst figure first. "Crowding you. The school. The dresses. The temple, the church, the piano, everything. Because while I am deciding it I am her mother in my own head for the length of the deciding." She looked out at the road. "Cheng Hoe told me a year ago and I said he was talking rubbish, and then did not speak to him for two days, which is how he knew he was right."

Mei Foong said nothing, which was an answer.

"Tuesdays and Thursdays she is yours," Bee Nio said. "Not sent. Yours. And the beading is yours entirely and I will not have an opinion about it; and if my husband's aunt asks again why the doctor's daughter sits in a shop, I shall tell her the truth, which is that the child is being taught something by somebody who knows it, and let her make of that what she likes." She picked up her basket. "And you will tell me when I am doing it again. Out loud. In words. You have a bad habit of forgiving people silently and it is very expensive to live with."

"Yes," said Mei Foong.

"Say one now."

"You are doing it about her hair. She is ten. She can plait her own hair. You do it because it is four minutes in the morning when she cannot go anywhere."

Bee Nio stood very still for a second. Then she made a small sound that was almost the old laugh, the amusement-park laugh, and looked at Mei Foong with her eyes bright and hard. "Four minutes. That is exactly what it is. Nobody in this town sees anything except you." And then, briskly, to the child coming out of the door with her satchel: "Say thank you to Auntie Foong."

"Thank you, Auntie Foong."

"Go carefully," said Mei Foong, to both of them, and stood in her own doorway under her own name and watched them up the street, the woman and the girl, until the goldsmith's grille took them out of sight.

---

Dr Lim's heart went in the second week of December, in the surgery, at a quarter past four, between a boil and a vaccination certificate.

Mei Foong was there because she had brought the child up from the shop early — the rain had come in and the light had gone — and because he had asked her in passing to hold the arm of a frightened old man while he lanced something. She saw the colour leave his face, and saw his left hand go flat and open on the desk instead of gripping, and she had him on the floor with his collar open before he had finished saying that it was nothing, it was the fish at lunch.

Grey. Sweating. The pulse under her fingers doing something disorderly and fast. She had learned what she knew of this from him, in an occupation dispensary, at night, when he had needed a second pair of hands and had had only hers; and she used it on him now, flat on his own linoleum, and sent the compounder running for the doctor two streets down and Kwai Lan for Bee Nio, and knelt there talking to him steadily about nothing at all — the stock, the Seremban order, the abominable price of glass beads — because his eyes kept coming to her face and going away and coming back.

He did not die. He was fifty-three; it was, the specialist said in January, a warning, and warnings are given to be used. He was to give up the night calls, the cigarettes, and eleven of his committees, and he gave up the cigarettes.

Mei Lan came into the shop the following evening and stood in the middle of the floor with her satchel still on her shoulder.

"He is not to walk upstairs for a fortnight," she said. Her voice was perfectly level, which frightened Mei Foong more than crying would have; the child had her father's habit of going quiet and orderly at the top of a fall. "Mother says it is nothing. When people say it is nothing they mean they have counted something."

"They mean they are frightened," said Mei Foong. "Come here."

The girl came, and stood, and after a moment leaned her weight against Mei Foong's side at the end of the counter, in front of forty thousand beads; and Mei Foong put her arm round her daughter in her own shop, under her own roof, which she was permitted to do, and which she did.

---

Ten days later she was sent for at the mansion.

The old man was in the account room with the shutters half closed against the glare, and there was a stranger with him: a thin clerk in a white shirt from the solicitor's up the street, sitting very upright with a case on his knees. The Towkay was seventy-one. He had a stick now, which he used as a pointer and not as a stick; the print of the share pages had begun to swim on him; he had not put his own figures on paper in two years.

"You will write," he said. "Your hand is legible. His" — the stick indicated the clerk without looking at him — "is a lawyer's."

She sat down at the black desk where she had sat for nineteen years, and took the green pen from her own pocket, because the pen in that desk's drawer had gone with her to her own house, and drew a clean sheet towards her.

He dictated for an hour and twenty minutes: the estate, item by item, in the order he had acquired it. The rubber acreage at Machap and the smaller piece at Durian Tunggal. The godowns. The shares — tin, rubber, one unwise parcel of a coconut concern in Johore. The two lorries and the Bentley he had never learned to drive. The mansion. Then, more slowly, the dispositions: this to a nephew, this in trust, this and this to his daughter Bee Nio, with clauses about her husband's health that he had plainly been thinking about at night.

The clerk wrote nothing; the clerk was there to carry the sheets away and put them into the proper language. It was a schedule, not a will. But she had kept this man's books through a bankruptcy, an occupation and a peace, and she could read the shape of his mind off a schedule.

"There is nothing here for the granddaughter," she said, when he stopped.

"No," said the Towkay. He was looking at the shutters. "There is not, and I have thought about it for a year, and I like none of the arrangements." His fingers moved on the head of the stick. "The girl is ten. She is the last of me. If I leave it to her father it goes to his family; if I leave it to her mother it goes to her father; if I leave it to the girl outright, some man will marry it off her in fifteen years and I shall be dead and unable to say anything about it, which is the part I find intolerable." He turned his head then and looked at Mei Foong, once, flatly, across the desk where he had first found her useful when she was twelve; and neither of them said anything about anything.

"Take a clean sheet," he said. "We begin again, and this time we begin from the child and work outwards. Head it."

She took a clean sheet, and squared it, and dipped the pen.

"For my granddaughter," said the old man.

And Mei Foong wrote it down in her small upright hand, exactly as given, without a tremor, because thirty years in that house had trained her hand not to tremble; and the clerk's pen scratched, and the shutters ticked in the heat, and the rain began on the tiles — and four doors down the street, on the wall of a shop with her own name over the door, her daughter's last hung in the third row, second from the end, marked in ink: *Mei Lan, 1952.*
