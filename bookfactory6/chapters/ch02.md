# Two Girls at the Gate

The sea let go of them on a Tuesday morning in June, and by noon Mei Foong was standing in a street called Heeren Street with the ground still tilting gently under her feet, the way the deck had tilted for a month. The heat here was different from the heat at home — thicker, sweeter, carrying the smell of the river they had come up at dawn: mud and salt and drying fish and something burnt-sugar underneath it, charcoal and coconut, the smell of a town cooking all day.

She was twelve years old and everything she owned was inside a rattan case that Ah Yoke would not let her carry.

Ah Yoke walked ahead of her down the middle of the five-foot way, the covered walk that ran along the faces of the houses like a tunnel of shade, and rickshaw pullers dozing between their poles opened one eye at the sound of her wooden clogs and closed it again. There was no mistaking what Ah Yoke was. She wore the uniform of her kind, the white Chinese blouse and wide black trousers, ironed on the ship's last morning until the creases could have cut paper, and her hair was pulled back into the single long plait of the sworn spinster, the women called majie, who took a vow before their goddess never to marry and hired out their lives to other people's households. Mei Foong had watched her comb and oil and braid that plait every morning for a month, on a heaving deck, in a crowd of seasick strangers, with the calm of a woman sweeping a floor only she could see.

"Walk properly," Ah Yoke said, in Cantonese, without turning around. It was the language they shared, the language of home; Mei Foong did not know yet how few people in this town would speak it to her. "You are not a duckling. When we arrive you will stand straight, you will keep your eyes down, and you will say nothing, in any language, until I tell you. If anyone asks, you are my sister's girl. My sister is dead. That is the whole of the story and it does not need your help."

"Yes, Aunt."

"And do not call me Aunt in that house. I am Ah Yoke there, the same as I am Ah Yoke everywhere." A pause, the length of three houses. "You may call me Aunt on ships."

The houses of Heeren Street stood shoulder to shoulder like a row of people who had been told to hold still, narrow faces, shuttered eyes, going back much further than any house had a right to go. And then the row gathered itself up into one house that was grander than the rest, two faces wide where its neighbours had one, its upper shutters green as beetle wings, its front doors carved and gilded and shut, and before them a second pair of doors only waist high, swinging half-doors through which the dim gold inside showed like fire through a grate. The pintu pagar, Ah Yoke said later, the fence-door; it let the air of the street in and kept the eyes of the street out.

Mei Foong slowed, because anyone would have.

"Not that door," said Ah Yoke, and did not slow at all.

---

They went in from the back, down a side lane barely wider than a carrying-pole, that ran through from Heeren Street towards the noise of the next street over — Jonker, Ah Yoke called it, the street of shops, in the tone other people used for the sea in a storm. The lane smelled of starch and drains. Halfway along it, in a high whitewashed wall, was a plain door the blue-green of old copper, and Ah Yoke knocked on it in a pattern, two and one, that she had clearly knocked many hundreds of times before.

That was the first door.

It opened into a back court of wet stone and bright sun, where laundry hung in ranks like flags and a charcoal stove was smoking under a blackened pot the size of a bath. Women looked up. There were more of them than Mei Foong could sort in one glance — an old one plucking a chicken, a broad one at the stove, a young one pounding something green in a mortar with a sound like a slow heart — and every one of them looked at Ah Yoke first with relief, and then at Mei Foong with open arithmetic.

"So," said the broad one at the stove, in Cantonese with the accent of another province worn smooth over it. "It lived. On that ship, in June. Sister Yoke, you could keep a candle lit underwater."

"This is the girl," said Ah Yoke. "She works."

That was, Mei Foong would learn, the entire ceremony of her arrival, as far as the kitchen court was concerned. The women had a hierarchy she could feel before she could name it, aunts in ranks like the laundry; the broad one was called Third Aunt and ruled the stoves, and the old one plucking the chicken was Great Aunt Lan and ruled Third Aunt, and above them all, in some upstairs country Mei Foong had not yet seen, was a person the whole court referred to in half-lowered voices as the Bibik, the way you refer to weather.

They gave her tea with the leaves still in it and a stool by the door, and then a shallow basket of rice to pick the grit from, because in that house, Third Aunt said, even the newly drowned worked while they dried. Mei Foong picked grit out of rice with the ground still swaying under her, grain by grain, stone by stone, and it steadied her the way the plait-combing had seemed to steady Ah Yoke; and she was still doing it an hour later when a bell rang somewhere deep in the house, twice, and every woman in the court straightened as if pulled on one string.

"Now," said Ah Yoke, taking the basket out of her hands. "The Bibik will look at you. Stand straight. Eyes down. Say nothing."

"What do I do?"

"Nothing. Being looked at is not a task." She flicked an invisible speck from Mei Foong's shoulder, and her hand stayed there a moment longer than the speck required. "Breathe," she said, in the voice she used for orders, and turned and led her into the house.

---

Afterwards, Mei Foong could have drawn the house with her eyes shut, because she counted the doors, the way she had counted hatches on the ship. It was a habit from her mother: *count the ways out before you need one.*

From the kitchen court, a door into the kitchen proper, dim and hot, hung with baskets: that was the second. From the kitchen, a third door into a passage, and the passage opened without warning into open sky — a well of light dropped down through the middle of the house, rain-scarred stone below, green ferns in pots climbing the walls, and far above a square of hard blue noon. She stopped as if she had walked into water. She had never seen a house that kept a piece of the sky indoors.

"Air well," said Ah Yoke, not stopping. "The second one. There are two. Rain falls into the house and the house does not mind. It is their way here. Come."

At the side of that second air well, going up into shadow, was a plain steep staircase of dark wood, and on the stair post hung a birdcage under a cloth. Ah Yoke jerked her chin at the staircase in passing. "The back stair. Ours. The amahs' stair. You go up to sleep and down to work and you are never on the front stairs, never, unless you are carrying something too big for this one. Remember that before you remember your own name."

Fourth door: out of the air well into the ancestral hall, and here Ah Yoke did stop, and bowed her head, and made Mei Foong stand still a moment in the smell of sandalwood ash. It was the tallest room Mei Foong had ever been inside, dark and cool as the inside of a jar. Against the far wall stood an altar table with tiers of small wooden tablets, gold characters climbing them like bees on a comb, and above the tablets a portrait of an old man in Ching-dynasty robes looking down through the gloom with an expression of mild permanent disappointment. Fresh ash stood in the urn. Somebody tended this room the way Ah Yoke tended her plait.

"The family's ancestors," said Ah Yoke quietly. "Not yours. You bow to the room, not the tablets. There is a difference and the difference is your whole life here, so learn it." She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but she stood a moment longer than the lesson needed, looking up at the climbing gold names, and her thumb moved once across the knuckles of her other hand.

Fifth door — double doors of carved screenwork, fretted so fine the far side showed through them in coins of light — into the first air well, grander than the second, with a fish jar the size of a well-head and a rack of orchids and the floor tiled in green and white like a cool chessboard. Sixth, past a heavy curtain, into the front hall itself, where the furniture was blackwood inlaid with mother-of-pearl that glimmered like oil on water, and the seventh door stood at the far end: the great carved street doors she had seen from outside, shut, with the waist-high pintu pagar swinging gently in front of them and the bright street beyond showing over its top, close enough to hear a rickshaw bell, a world away.

Seven doors, back lane to street. She had them all by heart before she had met a single member of the family, and she was standing in the last and grandest room of the count, eyes down, when she heard the small hard click of beaded slippers on tile, and the Bibik came to look at her.

---

She was not tall. That was the first surprise; the kitchen's half-lowered voices had built someone tall. The Bibik was a small round woman of perhaps fifty in a fitted kebaya blouse the colour of cream, fastened not with buttons but with three linked gold brooches, and a brown-and-gold sarong, and slippers beaded so densely they flashed like a beetle's back when she walked. Her hair was in a heavy bun stuck with gold pins. She had brought her own light into the dim hall the way a lamp does.

She spoke to Ah Yoke and Mei Foong understood not one word of it.

She had known this would happen — Ah Yoke had warned her on the ship: the family were Baba, Straits people, Chinese older than the oldest ships but Malacca-born so many generations that their daily tongue was a Malay of their own, stitched through with Hokkien; there would be Hokkien too, and English for the men's offices, and her Cantonese would be worth exactly as much as it was worth in the kitchen court and not one step further. Knowing it did not help. The Bibik's voice ran up and down like a fiddle, quick and certain, asking things; Ah Yoke's Malay answered slow and square, laying words like bricks; and Mei Foong stood in the space between the two of them, twelve years old, in her best blouse gone limp at the collar, understanding only the shapes: a question about the ship. A question about money, because money has a sound in every language. A question with her own name in the middle of it, *Mei Foong* bent almost past recognizing by the different mouth it came out of.

Then the Bibik walked around her.

She did it slowly, once, the way Mei Foong had seen buyers walk around fish at the morning market, and Mei Foong kept her eyes on the tiles and counted beads on the passing slippers. The Bibik's hand — small, plump, ringed — took her chin and tipped her face up to the light of the air well, turned it a little left, a little right. Strong teeth, good skin, no fever in the eyes: Mei Foong knew what a buyer checked, and stood for it.

But then the checking stopped, and the hand stayed.

The Bibik looked at her face, and it went on one beat too long. It was nothing — the space between two swings of the pintu pagar — but Mei Foong had spent a month learning the rhythms of a crowded ship, where you know your neighbours by their breathing, and she knew the rhythm of an inspection, and this had stopped being one. Something crossed the Bibik's eyes, quick as a bird's shadow crossing a court. Her thumb, without permission from the rest of her face, moved once along Mei Foong's jaw, the way you would touch something to see whether it was really there.

Then it was over. The hand withdrew. The fiddle-voice resumed, brisker than before, a shade too brisk, addressed to Ah Yoke over Mei Foong's head, and whatever question had opened in the room closed itself without being asked.

The Bibik said one more thing, with an upward flick of the chin at Mei Foong, and turned, and clicked away over her cool chessboard floor towards the stairs, and the hall let out its breath.

"What did she say?" Mei Foong whispered, in the second air well, when they were nearly back to the kitchen and the noise of the mortar covered them.

"She said you may stay." Ah Yoke was walking ahead, and her voice came back level as a carrying-pole. "While you earn your rice. Every day you earn it, there is rice. The day you do not, there is a ship, and ships go both ways." She paused at the kitchen door, the second door, and looked back, and for a moment the flat voice had something under it that Mei Foong could not read, pride or fear or both wearing the same starched face. "It is a fair rule. It is the same rule the sea has. You have already lived under it for a month, so eat, and start earning."

Neither of them said anything about the beat too long. Mei Foong put it away whole, the way she put away everything in those first days, in the place where she kept things that would need thinking about when there was time.

---

She met the daughter of the house before the rice was even cooked.

Mei Foong was back on the stool with a second basket — earning, grain by grain — when the court, which had been loud, went suddenly half-loud, and she looked up into the face of a girl a head shorter than her, standing much too close, wearing a European-style frock with a sailor collar and an expression of total command.

The girl said something in the fiddle-language, in the tone of a small empress.

Mei Foong looked at her.

The girl said it again, louder, because volume is the first translation anyone tries, and pointed at the basket, then at Mei Foong, then at herself, then — with a sweep of the whole arm — at the house in general. From the stove, Third Aunt said in her worn-smooth Cantonese, without turning around: "That is Bee Nio. The daughter. Eleven years old and the emperor of everything between here and the front door. She is asking if you are the new girl, and whether you belong to her."

"What do I say?"

"Nothing. She has already decided."

Bee Nio circled the stool exactly, unmistakably the way her mother had circled — the same market walk in miniature — finished her inspection, and delivered a verdict to the court at large, which made small sounds of agreement. Then she picked up Mei Foong's left hand from the basket rim, examined the palm, put it back where she had found it, said one final word with tremendous finality — Mei Foong caught *esok*, which by nightfall she would know meant tomorrow — and marched away through the third door with her nose leading.

"You carry her school basket tomorrow," Third Aunt translated. "Congratulations. You have been claimed."

"Is that good?"

Third Aunt turned a fish over in the pan and considered the question with more care than Mei Foong expected. "She came down herself," she said at last. "Within the hour. Usually she sends word." And that was all Mei Foong got, except that twice more before dark the sailor collar appeared in the court doorway on invented errands — once for hot water that was not wanted, once with a question about tomorrow's basket that had already been answered — and both times the small empress looked at the new girl sideways, quickly, the way you check that a present is still where you left it, and both times went away frowning with what even a foreigner could see was delight.

---

The son of the house she met last, and by accident, at the hour when the swallows come out.

She had been sent up the back stair at dusk with a folded stack of ironed things for the amahs' room, up the steep dark wood into the smell of starch and camphor, and coming down again — carefully; the treads were tall — she heard, in the second air well below her, a bird begin to sing.

She stopped on the stair without deciding to.

It was a soft, looping, bubbling call, three notes rolled over and over like a stone smoothed in a stream, and it was coming from the cage she had seen hanging shrouded on the stair post that noon. The cloth was off now. In the last light from the well of sky, the cage was a little bamboo palace, domed, with an ivory ring at its crown, and on the perch inside sat a small dove, barred grey and brown, neat as a folded letter, singing as if the day had been made on purpose.

A boy stood at the cage with his back to her, feeding it.

He was perhaps fourteen, in a white school shirt, his fingers still stained with school ink, up at the cage bars breaking off crumbs of banana no bigger than rice grains and passing them through with the terrible patience of an expert. He heard her on the stair — her foot, or her stillness — and turned, and did the last thing anyone in that house had done yet, which was smile as if she were good news.

He said something in the fiddle-language. She stood on the stair with the ironing-smell still on her hands and gave him the look she had been giving the whole world all day, and he understood it at once — she watched him understand — and tried again in a different language, rounder, that she guessed was Malay proper, and then in a third, clipped and strange, which she would learn later was English. Three languages, and her face answering none of them.

So he stopped talking. He held up the banana, what was left of it, half a banana peeled and going brown at the edge, bird's dinner, boy's teatime, and raised his eyebrows: a question with no country.

She was hungry. She had eaten rice and one dried fish at the servants' sitting, first shift, standing up. She was also proud, with the particular hoarded pride of a girl who owned one rattan case and not even the case, and she had been claimed once already today, and looked at like a fish twice, and she did not want the first thing between her and anyone in this family to be charity.

The boy saw the hesitation, the way he had seen the language problem, and solved it the same way, without a word. He broke the half banana into two quarters, put one on the flat of his palm, and passed it not to her but through the rails of the stair, laying it on the tread beside her feet, the exact way he had passed crumbs through the bars of the cage. Then he took a bite of his own quarter and turned back to the bird, leaving her to it, the way you leave food near something wild that you hope will stay.

The dove sang. Somewhere towards the front of the house, the Bibik's voice was climbing and falling at some other servant. The square of sky at the top of the air well had gone the colour of a mangosteen shell.

Mei Foong sat down on the stair, on the tall tread, and ate the banana.

It was the first thing anyone in Malacca had given her that she had not earned, and she was twelve and had been at sea a month, and she ate it in three bites and could have cried over it, and didn't, because the boy was watching the bird and pretending not to watch her, badly. When she finished he passed a second piece through the rails, the last piece, his own, and this time she took it from his hand.

He pointed at the cage. "Merbok," he said, and made the bird's three-note roll with his own mouth, so well that the dove cocked its head and answered him, and Mei Foong laughed for the first time on that side of the sea — one short syllable of laugh, out before she could stop it.

"Merbok," she said.

He pointed at himself. "Boon Siew."

She pointed at herself, and gave him her name, her real name in her mother's tongue, the first time she had said it aloud in this country. He said it back. He bent it, of course — his mouth was made of other languages — but he bent it carefully, twice, checking with his eyebrows whether the second try was better, and it was, and she nodded, and that was all. A bell rang towards the front of the house; he went up his stairs and she went down hers.

The merbok sang in the dark a while, and then the cloth must have gone back over the cage, because it stopped.

---

The amahs' room was at the top of the back stair, under the roof, where the day's heat gathered to spend the night. Six women slept there in a row, on low wooden beds with woven rattan tops, and Mei Foong had been given a pallet at the foot of Ah Yoke's bed, in the corner, beneath a small high window through which she could hear the town — dogs, a late rickshaw bell, water somewhere always — and, if she turned her head, see one handspan of foreign stars.

She was so tired that sleep came up at her like the floor of the sea. But she had spent a month learning to sleep in a room full of strangers, which is a skill made mostly of pretending, and so when Ah Yoke rose quietly much later, past midnight by the depth of the dark, Mei Foong surfaced without moving, breathed long and slow, and watched through her lashes.

Ah Yoke lit no lamp. She worked by the handspan of starlight and forty years of knowing where her own hands were. She lifted the rattan case onto her bed and opened it without a creak — she had oiled the hinges, Mei Foong thought; she oils everything, she plans everything — and she unpacked it, though it had already been unpacked once that evening, in daylight, publicly.

Then, from inside her own bedding, where it must have travelled all day against her body, she took out a bundle.

It was small, the size of two fists, wrapped in dark cloth, and it was tied — Mei Foong's eyes had the dark now — with thread that showed, when Ah Yoke's turning hands brought it through the little window's light, a colour. Red. Wound many times, knotted with the deliberate beauty of knots that are not meant to be convenient, that are tied the way vows are tied. Ah Yoke held the bundle in both hands. She was still for a long time, a woman kneeling at a case of folded clothes as if it were an altar; and once her thumb moved across the red thread the way it had moved across her own knuckles in the ancestral hall, among the gold names that were not hers.

Then she laid the bundle flat in the bottom of the case, under the paper lining, and put every folded thing back on top of it, and closed the lid, and slid the case beneath her bed to the wall, where a case lives in a servants' room, invisible because it is exactly where everyone's case is.

She lay down. Her breathing evened with a discipline that Mei Foong, a professional now in the field of pretended sleep, recognized at once and filed without judgment: so we are both doing it.

Mei Foong lay in the heat under the roof of the house of seven doors and set out the day's accounts in rows, the way she would all her life. A rule about rice, which was fair. A small empress, who would be work, and possibly — the frowning delight, the invented errands — something better than work. A red bundle at the bottom of a case, put away in the dark by a woman who did nothing in the dark that could be done in daylight. A look that had lasted one beat too long, and a thumb along her jaw, checking that she was real.

And a banana passed through the rails of a stair, to a wild thing they hoped would stay.

The house ticked and settled around her, long and narrow, going back and back from its gilded street face into the dark, room after room after room. Somewhere below, a clock she had not yet found struck a single soft note. She matched her breathing to Ah Yoke's, one beat behind, and let the tide take her out.
