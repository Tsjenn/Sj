# What the Water Took

The river came for the godown in the last week of December, on the fourth night of a rain that had stopped being weather and become a condition of the world, like darkness. Mei Foong woke before dawn to lantern light crossing the kitchen court and the Towkay's voice pitched in a way she had never heard from him — not loud, but stripped, the lacquer gone off it, a man counting losses out loud to nobody.

The Malacca River had left its bed. It did so a little every monsoon, a polite trespass of inches the town had long ago agreed to forgive; but that year the rain had sat over the hills for a week and the tide had come up the river mouth to meet it, and the water had nowhere to be except everywhere. The firm's godown stood in the row along the river behind the bridge, a long dim shed Mei Foong knew by smell before she ever saw it flooded: smoked rubber, which is a smell like burnt sugar and creosote quarrelling, and under it river mud and hessian and the paraffin of the lamps. The rubber was in bales, sheet upon ribbed sheet pressed and wrapped for Singapore, stacked on bearers a foot off the floor because a foot had always been enough.

The water that night rose four.

She was in the second boat across, wedged between Ah Yoke's umbrella and a coil of rope, because Boon Siew had come running back through the rain at first light with his shirt stuck to him like paper on a lantern and said the word that unlocked every door in the house: books. The stock was insured against fire, riot and the act of God now occurring; the account books were insured against nothing. They lived in the godown office at the shed's river end, in a camphorwood cupboard that had been proof against ants, damp and forty years of clerks, and that was now standing to its waist in the river.

"The girl checks the invoices," Boon Siew said to his father, in the front hall, in front of the head clerk and the drivers and heaven. "She knows the books in her hands. Send her too."

The Towkay had looked at Mei Foong the way he looked at a lorry: as a question of capacity. Then he had nodded once and turned away, and that was how she came to be fifteen years old in a sampan on a street she had walked a hundred times, poling past the tops of doorways, watching a town look up at its own water lines.

They worked three days.

Afterwards, when the story had been polished by telling into something with a shape — the flood year, the year the girl saved the lawsuit — Mei Foong would find she had kept none of the shape and all of the pieces. The cold coming up through her feet from water that had no business being cold in Malacca. Kerosene pressure lamps hissing on the mezzanine while the river slid by under the floorboards with a sound like a great animal turning over. The head clerk, old Yap, sixty and dignified, wading with his ledgers held over his head in a hammock of his own shirt, weeping without noise and without stopping work, because forty years of his handwriting was dissolving under his hands. And the pages: river water is not clean water, and where it had reached, the ink went out like a lamp — figures blurring to smoke, columns bleeding into columns, a year of the firm's memory becoming one brown watermark.

The men hauled the cupboards and chests up the ladder to the smoke mezzanine, where in better times the sheets were hung to cure; and there, under the lamps, in air so thick with old smoke it made a second dusk, Mei Foong and two junior clerks unbound the wet volumes, interleaved the pages with newsprint and rice paper, weighted them, changed the papers, moved them again, hour upon hour, like nursing a fever through a night. What could be dried was dried. What was going — and some pages you could watch going, the numbers fading as the damp crept up the fibres — she copied. She sat on a rubber bale with a board across her knees and a fresh ledger from the stationer's, and she copied the dying pages before they died: date, buyer, grade, weight, price, tally, her figures marching down the new page in the small upright hand Boon Siew had taught her at the kitchen table, each entry checked twice against the original while the original could still be read at all.

Boon Siew worked at her shoulder the first night, calling the blurred figures aloud where her eye doubted them, holding the lamp so its light fell on the page and not in her eyes, and they found — both of them, without a word spent on it — that they worked well in harness, his voice and her hand, the way two people carrying a table learn each other's steps. Around midnight of the second night he fell asleep mid-sentence, upright, an invoice in his hand, and she took it from him gently as one takes a bowl from a sleeping child, finished the column, and let him sleep against the bale until Yap woke him out of decency. She did not stop. Somewhere in the middle of that second night the work stopped being duty and became something with an appetite of its own. These were the family's numbers. She had checked them line by line for two years in the ordinary way of her evenings; she knew the good months and the bad, the buyers who paid to the day and those who needed dunning like children need calling twice; she knew this firm on paper better than anyone alive except possibly old Yap, and she felt — hidden, the way she kept all her pride hidden, folded small — that the water had come for something that was hers.

On the third day, the water went down and the trouble came up.

The trouble was rubber. A third of the stock had stood too deep too long: sheets meant to go to Singapore as first grade came out of their soaked wrappings stained, stinking of the river, mould already ghosting the ribs. That was loss, but loss was arithmetic. The lawsuit was character. Among the bales were ninety that did not belong to the Tans at all but were held in store for Chop Guan Seng, a dealer's firm off Bunga Raya that lodged smallholders' rubber in the Tan godown against shipment, paying rent by the month. Within a week of the water going down, Guan Seng's lawyer sent a letter in beautiful English claiming that the Tans' negligence had ruined not ninety bales but a hundred and forty, of the highest grade, and naming a figure with enough zeros in it to shorten the Towkay's temper for a month.

"A hundred and forty," said old Yap, and put the letter down as if it were a fishbone found in rice.

The men went to and fro for a fortnight — lawyers, assessors, an insurance surveyor with a clipped moustache and a way of saying *in the ordinary course* that made everyone in the room feel accused of something. What Guan Seng was counting on, Mei Foong understood, listening from the account room with her ears open, was the flood itself: the river had eaten the evidence. The godown book was a brown ruin; who could now say what had been in store, and of what grade? A flood is a broad back. It can carry any number a man cares to load on it.

Except that the pages had not died. They had been copied while they were dying, twice-checked, in a small upright hand.

She was not taken to the assessor's rooms — girls did not go where lawsuits went — but her ledger was, and the water-stained originals with it, still interleaved with her rice papers; and Boon Siew came back that evening and told it to the account room at large, too loud, too happy, seventeen and lit like a lamp. The recopied book showed ninety bales lodged by Guan Seng, with dates, cart numbers and grades; showed, better still, that thirty of the ninety had been graded down on arrival months before — mixed sheet, not first — the downgrading initialled by Guan Seng's own carter, in an entry that survived in the original just legibly enough to match the copy word for word. The copy proved the book; the book unmade the claim. Guan Seng's lawyer had looked at the young lady's figures — his phrase — for a long minute, said Boon Siew, and then begun to speak of settlement in the voice of a man leaving by a small door.

Mei Foong saw the other firm exactly once. Sent to the stationer's for more ledger paper, she passed below the lawyer's office a knot of men from Guan Seng waiting in the five-foot way, and on its edge a clerk — a narrow-shouldered man of thirty-odd in a shirt gone grey at the collar, holding his firm's duplicate book against his chest the way a schoolboy holds the work that has failed him. It was his figures, she understood later, that had not matched; his book that the girl's book had shamed; and though no one in the five-foot way could have told him which passing girl had done it, he was looking out at the wet street with a patience that was not resignation, the look of a man taking down in some inner ledger of his own everything the world owed him. Their eyes crossed for half a step, the way strangers' do. She had forgotten him by the corner. She would have cause, in another life, in another Malacca, to remember that she had forgotten him.

The settlement was signed before the New Year, for the true ninety bales at their true grades, and the Towkay, who had faced a claim of some thousands and paid a fraction, was moved to a public act.

He did it at Sunday dinner, the whole household assembled, a rubber broker from Singapore at his right hand as witness, which was the point. He called Mei Foong from the doorway where the serving was marshalled and had her stand by the table, and he made a speech of nearly a minute, which for him was an oration. He spoke of the flood, of the books, of the claim; he took from his waistcoat two silver dollars and put them in her hand, closing her fingers over them with his own dry ones, a gesture he must have believed to be kindness and which was in fact possession.

"You see how it is," he said to the broker, over her head, in the voice of a man displaying a lorry's engine. "People talk of blood — good family, clever family. Rubbish. It is training, nothing else. This one came to us an amah's girl, could not write her own name. Give any child figures young enough and drill them, and there is your result. Anyone can be made useful. It is all training and no blood at all."

The table made the sound tables make. Bee Nio clapped, briefly, sincerely. Boon Siew did not clap; she felt him not clapping from across the room. And Mei Foong stood with two dollars growing warm in her fist and discovered that a wound and a prize can arrive in the same closed hand. *An amah's girl.* She had not known until that minute that she had been carrying, somewhere unexamined, a hope of being — not praised; she could not have said what. Seen, perhaps. Found. And here was the finding: she was proof of a proposition. She was what you could make of anyone. The room admired her the way it would admire a well-trained merbok, and the bird, as Boon Siew had said once on the dark stair, sings because it cannot do anything else.

She bowed, and thanked the Towkay in Baba Malay with every ornament of respect Ah Yoke had drilled into her, and went back to the doorway, and served the soup with steady hands. It was the Bibik, strangely, whose eye she felt as she served — the Bibik, who had said not one word throughout, watching her over the dishes with the still, sifting expression of a woman listening to a far-off sound she cannot place. *No blood at all,* the Towkay had said. The Bibik looked at Mei Foong's wrists as she set down the tureen, and looked away.

In the kitchen court afterwards Ah Yoke took the two dollars, examined them as if coins could lie, and gave them back.

"Keep them knotted," she said. "Money that is given in front of people is a rope with two ends. But keep it." And then, tying the coins herself into the corner of Mei Foong's handkerchief with a majie's flat hard knots: "He is wrong, what he said. It is not all training." She pulled the knot tight. "Go and wash the rice."

It was two evenings later, in the account room, that Boon Siew tried to give her the pen.

The account room was the smallest room in the mansion, off the second air well, hardly more than a cupboard that had won promotion: one window, one shelf of dead years, and the black desk with the abacus and the brass lamp, where Mei Foong now sat most evenings with the invoices, because after the flood no one, not even the Towkay, pretended any longer that she was checking Yap's work rather than Yap checking hers. Boon Siew came in with his hair still wet from the bath and put the pen down on the open ledger without preamble, the way he did everything that mattered to him, as if speed could get a thing past the sentries.

She knew the pen. Everyone knew the pen: his prize from the High School, given before the whole hall — an English fountain pen, dark green, mottled like river water under trees, with a gold nib he tended like livestock. He carried it clipped inside his shirt pocket where the sun could not fade it.

"For the accounts," he said. "A copying hand like yours shouldn't be dipping and dipping like a fowl drinking. It slows the work."

Mei Foong looked at the pen lying on her figures and understood it completely — the flood nights, the voice-and-hand of their working, the two dollars closed into her fist, everything the pen was arriving to answer — and picked it up, and held it out to him.

"It is your prize," she said. "It has your name on the case. What is the amah's girl doing with the young master's prize? You know what the house would make of it. There is nothing wrong with my dipping."

He did not argue, which frightened her a little, because he argued about everything; fairness was the one subject on which he had no manners at all. He took the pen back and looked at it, then at her, with an expression she filed away and did not examine because examining it would have been a door opening onto a corridor of doors. He said only, "The work shouldn't be slower because the house is foolish," and said good night, and went.

In the morning, when she opened the shallow left-hand drawer of the black desk — her drawer, the drawer of nibs and blotting sand and red thread for tying the year's bundles, the drawer no other hand in the household had reason to open from one Cheng Beng to the next — the pen was lying in it. On a folded square of blotting paper, squared to the corners. Without its case, so that it had no name on it at all.

She sat looking at it for the length of a stick of incense. Then she took out her steel dip pen, and did the evening's invoices with the dip pen, and put the green pen back exactly where it lay, and closed the drawer.

The next evening it was still there. And the evening after. She did not use it and she did not return it; he did not ask and she did not answer; and by such a treaty, never drafted and never signed, the pen stayed in the account drawer through everything that came after — used, in the end, and used, and used, until her hand knew its weight the way a hand knows its own — but that came later. That January it simply lay in the drawer like money in the corner of a handkerchief: knotted, kept, not spent, and not, whatever anyone might ever ask, accepted.

The matchmakers began coming for Bee Nio while the godown was still drying.

It began, as the great campaigns of the Bibik's life always began, with kueh. For a week the kitchen court was a factory of pineapple tarts and kueh bangkit, the Bibik moving through the steam like an admiral through gun smoke, tasting, condemning, decreeing. Then the visits: matchmaking aunties of a particular glossy kind, come at the polite hour, admiring the altar, the tiles, and most especially, at strategic length, the daughter of the house, produced in her second-best kebaya to carry in the tea tray with her eyes lowered — Bee Nio, who had never lowered her eyes at anything since birth, performing modesty like a role learned overnight, and performing it, Mei Foong observed from the doorway, alarmingly well.

There were photographs. Bee Nio was taken by trishaw to the studio on Jonker Street, hair oiled and pinned by the Bibik's own hands, the good kerosang brooches brought out of the iron box; and the result, a week later, was passed around the front hall in its stiff card folder: Bee Nio seated beside a potted fern, chin at the angle the photographer had placed it with one finger, looking like a stranger of good family, fifteen years old and offered to the world at large. Copies went out with the matchmakers the way a firm's price list goes out with its travellers. Other photographs came back: sons. Sons in high collars, sons in spectacles, one son standing beside a motorcycle with an air of having recently been ordered off it. The Bibik dealt them out on the marble table like a fortune-teller and read pedigrees aloud from memory — this one's grandfather, that one's mother's people, shop, sickness, temper, gambling, the whole submerged ledger of the Straits kept in that iron head — and Mei Foong, refilling the teapot, thought: this is also a kind of accounts. Grade, weight, provenance. Bales in store.

Bee Nio in daylight was insufferable about it all, comparing photographs with the cruelty of a girl who has never yet been refused anything, giving the merbok in its cage her opinion of the motorcycle son. That was daylight.

Past midnight, three nights after the first photographs went out, Mei Foong woke on her pallet in the back room she shared with Ah Yoke's ironing to find a shape sitting at the foot of it, hugging its knees. Bee Nio, in her sleeping clothes, her unpinned hair making her small, her face — when the moon through the airwell found it — undone in a way Mei Foong had never once seen it in four years under the same roof.

"I can't sleep in my room," Bee Nio said, in a voice with all the lacquer gone off it, her father's voice from the flood night. "Don't light anything. Don't call anyone."

She did not speak for a while. Then it came out sideways, in pieces, in whispers, the way water finds seams: that she had heard the aunties totting up her points as if she were a beast at Tampin market — good hips, fair skin, temper doubtful; that the motorcycle son was twenty-nine, which was nearly dead; that once married she would belong to another woman's kitchen, another woman's altar, a mother-in-law who could be anything at all, and no one would ask her, because no one had yet asked her a single thing in the whole grand machinery except whether she preferred the fern on her left or her right for the photograph. "I said left," Bee Nio said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked through into the truth of it: "Mei Foong, they will send me away from this house to strangers, and everyone keeps smiling, and I am supposed to smile, and I am so frightened I can't breathe lying down."

There were answers a servant could make, in the smooth Baba Malay of comfort, and Mei Foong knew them all. She put them aside. She moved against the wall instead, turning back the thin blanket, and said what one girl says to another at two in the morning: "Then breathe sitting up. Stay here."

Bee Nio slept in her bed that night the way children settle in a shared bed the whole world over, top-to-toe, her cold feet by Mei Foong's shoulder, one ankle in Mei Foong's loose grip — because at the last edge of waking Bee Nio had reached down and put it there, as though a held ankle were an anchor, and muttered into the dark: "When I marry, wherever they send me, you'll come. I'll make Father put it in the agreement. You're mine anyway."

It was the old claiming, the *my girl* of the first year, half friendship and half ownership, never yet resolved into either. But a thing said in daylight and the same thing said by a frightened girl holding your ankle in the dark are not the same thing. Mei Foong lay awake a while, feeling the grip slacken as sleep took her — sister, mistress, neither, both — and found that her own account of Bee Nio, kept four years in a hidden column, would not balance any longer, and let it stand unbalanced, and slept.

In the last week of January the newspaper came.

The Chinese paper came to the house every day; the war in China came with it, and had for months — a coastline of names, subscriptions raised, speeches at the association hall, the Towkay's conspicuous donation and the kitchen court's hidden ones. The house had learned to live with the war the way one lives with thunder in the hills: grave, distant, discussed over tea. Nanking had fallen in December; that had been a heavy day, but a day like the others, and the tea had still been drunk.

This was different, and Mei Foong knew it was different before she knew anything else, because she was crossing the front hall with the mended lamp shade and the hall was full of men and the hall was silent.

They stood around the marble table — the Towkay, Boon Siew, old Yap, two visiting towkays, the paper open between them at a photograph from Nanking. From the doorway she could not see the photograph, only its position on the page, and the men. She never did see it close, then or after; and long after, when she had come to know exactly what the years meant to do with all of them, she understood that she had not needed to. What the photograph was, was in the men's faces; what it meant was in their stillness — the particular stillness of men who have been talking about a war for months, fluently, with figures, and have just been shown what one of their words looks like. The visiting towkay who had laughed loudest at dinner had his hand flat on the table as if the table might move. Boon Siew was white to the mouth. He had argued Malaya into this war at his father's table for a year, seventeen years old and certain; he looked, in that silence, like a boy who has won an argument with the sea.

Nobody said anything while she was in the hall. That was the thing she carried out with her, the lamp shade still in her hands: in a house that had a saying for everything, that had rules for grief and forms for disaster and a proverb ready before the rice cooled — nobody had said anything at all.

She went back through the second air well, past the account room where the pen lay in the drawer, to the kitchen court, where the ordinary noon was going on: charcoal smoke, the chop of Third Aunt's cleaver, laundry steam. And there was Ah Yoke at the lane door, counting coins from her own knotted purse — her own, not the household's, Mei Foong saw that at once and never forgot it — into the hand of the rice merchant's boy, while behind him on the handcart sat a full bag of rice. The house's monthly bag had come only the week before. It stood in the back store, still fat.

The boy shouldered the second bag in and set it where Ah Yoke pointed: not with the house rice, but behind it, in the dim corner under the shelf, against the wall. Ah Yoke covered it with an empty gunny sack, which hid it not at all, and stood back and looked at it the way she looked at a hem she might yet unpick.

"The bin is still half full," Mei Foong said. "And the house bag not yet opened. Why a second?"

Ah Yoke straightened the sacking with one hand, a small, exact, unnecessary motion. In the front hall the men had found their voices at last; you could hear the murmur starting up, argument returning like circulation. Ah Yoke did not look toward it. She had not been in the hall; she had not seen the paper. She had lived forty more years than Mei Foong, and had crossed one sea already ahead of one catastrophe, and needed no photograph.

"For later," she said.

She said nothing else. She took up the account of the day's marketing and read out the vegetables for Mei Foong to enter, price by price, in the small upright hand, as on any other afternoon; and the second bag sat in its corner under the sacking, the first stone of a wall no one in the house yet knew was being built.
