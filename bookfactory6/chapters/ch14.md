# The Red Thread

On the fourth morning of the occupation, Third Aunt lined the kitchen court up between the laundry poles and taught it to bow, because she had seen a man knocked flat at the river bridge for doing it badly. She stood in for the sentry herself, arms folded, while the household bent from the waist one by one — the second amah, the kitchen girls, the Taiping cousins, Mei Foong — and she corrected each of them the way she corrected seasoning, without apology and twice.

"Lower," she said. "Lower than that. You are not greeting him. You are showing him the top of your head. He wants to know you understand whose street it is." She made even Great Aunt Lan do it, eighty years old, holding her elbow through the bend. "Good. Now it is a thing your back knows, and your back can do it while the rest of you is elsewhere. That is the whole trick of this year. Be elsewhere."

That week the notices went up where the auction bills used to go, pasted in two languages over the old paper, and the town learned the shape of its new life, which was a queue. Heads of households were to report with the particulars of every soul under their roofs. The Towkay went in his second-best suit and stood four hours in the sun outside the Stadthuys, a man who had not queued for anything in thirty years, and came home with a paper bearing a red stamp and said nothing at all at dinner. Mei Foong had copied the household list out fair for him the night before — twenty-two souls, name, age, sex, occupation — and had ruled her own line among the rest: *Ho Mei Foong, female, nineteen, servant.* Four facts. Before the month was out she would learn that one of them was a loan and one was not a fact at all.

The clocks were put forward to the hour of Tokyo, so that the sun now rose officially in the middle of the morning, and the notices were dated in a year called two thousand six hundred and two, which nobody had been living in until told. The checkpoints kept the new hour strictly. Crossing to Jonker Street for the marketing meant the sentry at the corner, and the bow, held to the count of two, and Mei Foong did it the way Third Aunt had drilled it, her back present, the rest of her elsewhere, and was not once knocked down.

The billeting officer came on the ninth day. He was three men: an officer who looked at things, an interpreter who said what the officer had not troubled to say, and a clerk who wrote everything down. They walked the house of seven doors from the street face to the kitchen court with the Bibik walking one pace behind in her plainest kebaya, offering tea at correct intervals and being correctly refused, and the clerk measured the rooms with his eye and entered them in his book. The ancestral hall, dark and cool as the inside of a jar, with its tiers of gold names climbing the altar wall, went into the book as one large room, south-facing, contents: shrine. The officer stood in it for a moment, looked at a hundred years of ancestors the way a man looks at weather that does not concern him, and moved on.

In the shed he walked around the Bentley the way the Bibik had once walked around a new servant. It was the only thing in the house he wanted. The syce had never once let the battery die in the car nobody could drive, and so it started at the first press, beautifully, and rolled out of the yard for the first true journey of its life with a soldier at the wheel; and Mei Foong entered it in her narrow column that evening without comment, because the fever chart had taught her that some entries make their own comment: *under, 9. Inspected. Tea refused. The Bentley drives at last.*

---

Ah Yoke kept the side door open the first week of the occupation on the argument that the scoop was the scoop under any flag. The queue in the lane was longer now and quieter, and she stood at the door each dawn levelling the measure against her palm, coughing between households — the small tight cough with the click in it that had come out of the flooded trench and the wet nights under the altar table and never gone home again.

On the eleventh day the scoop shook. Mei Foong saw it from the bin: the level palm coming across, and the rice trembling off it, six grains, seven, onto the ground, and Ah Yoke looking down at the spilled grains with an expression of pure professional insult. By noon she could not climb the back stair. By dark she was burning.

They made a bed for her in the dry storeroom behind the kitchen, off the court, because heat gathered in the amahs' room under the roof and because sick women should not have stairs between them and the people nursing them; Third Aunt said this as procedure and then sat outside the storeroom door on a stool all evening like a temple lion. Boon Siew went out at first light with his old armband and his new papers and brought Dr. Lim through two checkpoints, and the doctor knelt in the storeroom a long time with the stethoscope, moving it, listening, moving it, the way Mei Foong had watched him listen all through the bombing weeks, and then he sat back on his heels.

"The left lung," he said. "Pneumonia. I would send her to the hospital if the hospital were a place I could still send anyone." He left what medicine he had, which did not fill a hand, and gave the nursing instructions to Mei Foong twice over — fluids, the fever sponged, propped upright always, the counting of breaths. "It will build for some days and then it will break," he said at the door, quietly. "Around the seventh day. It breaks, or it does not. I am sorry to say it to you like a timetable."

"Say everything to me like a timetable," Mei Foong said. "It is how I hear best."

Inside, Ah Yoke had listened to all of it through the wall, because she had never in her life failed to hear anything said near a door. When Mei Foong came in with the broth she was propped against the sacks with her plait over her shoulder, her eyes bright with fever and perfectly steady, doing sums.

"Seven days," she said. "Then there is time, if the nights are not wasted. Tonight, when the house sleeps, come back with the lamp. No book. What I have for you goes in no book ever kept." She took the broth in both hands, drank it level as a measure, and gave back the bowl. "I am going to pay a debt, girl. It is a long account and I will have to pay it in instalments, so do not be late."

---

The house slept. The blackout paper made the storeroom a box of lamplight; the wick was down to a bead; outside in the court the new hour of Tokyo said midnight while the old sky of Malacca said half past ten. Mei Foong sat on the floor by the bed with her hands in her lap, and Ah Yoke lay propped high on the sacks, and began — not weakly, but economically, spending words the way she spent everything, nothing twice, nothing spilled.

"You asked me once for the end of a story, and I told you the oil was finished. The oil is not finished tonight." She breathed, carefully, around the thing in her chest. "Two sisters. You have kept the words, I know you; say them back to me."

"In Kwangtung, in a house bigger than this one, there were two sisters," Mei Foong said. "Close the way stairs are close. One step above, one step below, made from the same wood. The elder had the obedient wrist. The younger had the clever one."

"Word for word," Ah Yoke said. "Good. Now I take one word back. The house was not in Kwangtung. I moved it. Old women may move houses; it is our one power." Her eyes did not leave Mei Foong's face. "The house was in this town, girl. At the other end of this street. You have swept past its door carrying marketing."

The lamp burned. Somewhere in the walls the house ticked, settling, the way it had settled every night for eight years over her sleep.

"Say the other name you have been carrying," Ah Yoke said. "Since the temple. Since the night under the table. Say it and I will finish the story properly, in order."

"Guat Kim," Mei Foong said. Her own voice came out very quiet and very level, and she understood that she had been rehearsing this word in the dark of herself for three years without once permitting the rehearsal to be seen.

"Neo Guat Kim," said Ah Yoke. "There. Now it has its first stone back." She looked at the lamp a moment. "The elder was Neo Guat Eng. She married the son of the Tan house when the younger was still a child, and kept the name, and kept the recipes, and kept the door. You call her the Bibik. Under the altar table, in the bombing, she prayed to your mother by name and told her to watch the roof. She is the only soul in this house besides me who remembers that your mother was ever alive, and she does not know that your mother ever had you. Hold all of that still. We go in order."

And in order she went, in the counting-rice cadence, a measure at a time, resting between measures.

Nineteen twenty-one. The younger sister nineteen years old, the elder long married up the street. A clerk in one of the English trading houses on the river; a Eurasian, a Malacca Portuguese name; Pereira. "I saw him one time only," Ah Yoke said. "A thin man with ink on his cuff, who stood up when a servant came into the room and then looked sorry for making her notice. That is everything I ever knew of him and it was enough to sink two lives." The families found out in the third month. The word was no. "And she looked at the word the way you would look at a door, deciding. Her father said the door had one side. Her sister —" the first pause that was not for breathing — "her sister said nothing. She was the elder. Elder sisters are the wall the family saves money on."

The fifth month, before the kitchen fires were lit: one bundle, and her mother's ring off the altar table — took back, she would have said, what was promised her — and she ran. To him; to Singapore; to a church that was willing and a registry that was slow. "They had fifty-one days," Ah Yoke said. "I have counted them for her many times. The count does not improve. The fever went through the coolie lines and the clerks' quarters alike that year, and it was quicker than the registry. He died on a Tuesday. They were not yet married. You will want a better word than that for it someday. There is no better word. The paper was not finished, so the world says there was nothing to finish. The world keeps books the way the Towkay keeps them: what is not entered did not occur."

"And at home," Mei Foong said, because Ah Yoke's breath had shortened and the next part was owed, "her father—"

"Her father took down the register of the family and ground ink," Ah Yoke said, "and drew one line. I was told by those who were in the room that it took less time than boiling water for tea. A name is years in the making, girl, and one stroke in the unmaking. I have seen chickens killed with more ceremony." She lay back against the sacks. "That is the whole of what they did to her. No beating, no curse, nothing you could bandage. A line of ink. Remember that when you go looking for the monster in this story. There is no monster. There is a book, and a brush, and people who kept the book. It is the tidiest way the world has ever found to kill somebody and leave them walking."

She closed her eyes then, and Mei Foong thought the instalment was ended, but the old woman spoke once more from behind her closed lids, at the border of sleep.

"She wrote home once from Singapore, before she sailed. No answer came. The whole sea goes one way, girl — everyone in it going south, to the gold, to the rubber, to the good years. She rode it backwards. Alone, in the sixth month, carrying you." A long breath, with the click in it. "Enough. Count what I have given you. Tomorrow the sisterhood."

---

The next day the house was a house under occupation, which is to say a house pretending with all its strength to be only a house. The Bibik sent down her own chicken essence for the sick amah, in the blue-and-white cup with the lid, and Mei Foong carried it in and did not spill it, and stood a moment watching Ah Yoke drink her employer's kindness — her employer of twenty years, the elder sister, the wall the family saved money on — and said nothing, and took the cup back to the kitchen, and washed it, and was elsewhere the whole time, the way Third Aunt had taught the street to bow.

On the second night the lamp went down to its bead again, and Ah Yoke, hotter now, her sentences shorter, told the middle of the account.

A district in Kwangtung; the county the Neo family had come from four generations back, whose name was still spoken over their incense. "She ran the whole map backwards. To the ancestors' own country. If the living would not keep her, she would go and live near the dead, who cannot refuse." A vegetarian hall of the sisterhood stood in the market town there — women who had combed up their hair and said no to marriage, keeping, at the end of a corridor, a woman marriage had said no to. "My own hall," Ah Yoke said. "I was home that year; my mother had chosen it to die in, and heaven does its scheduling without consulting the servants. I saw them bring her in off the road. A nyonya, in a kebaya the colour of cream gone grey with the sea, speaking her fever in Baba Malay that not one soul in the town could understand. I understood the shape of it. I had served in Malacca. I knew what she was before I knew who she was, and when she told me the street — girl, when she told me the street, I said: I have swept it."

You were born in the third month of nineteen twenty-two, early, small, loud. A child must enter somebody's book, and her mother's book was closed, and her father's name would have sunk the two of them in that town before she could walk. "I said: take mine. Ho. A wide name; half of Kwangtung shelters under it; nobody counts who comes in." The pause here was not for breath. "It cost me nothing to lend. It is the only thing I ever lent in my life that I hope is never given back."

Her mother chose the given name herself. "Mei for the plum, that flowers in the cold. And Foong—" Ah Yoke's mouth made something that on any other face would have been a smile — "Foong for the phoenix. Out of the pattern, girl. The peony and the phoenix, her mother's pattern, the one the two sisters ground into their hands at that end of this street the way flour is ground. She had no land, no gold, no name of her own to will you. She put the phoenix in your name and called the estate settled."

She fed you eleven years on beads. Wedding work, funeral work, the fine slippers for the towns downriver, her wrist the clever one to the last. And once a year she wrote to her sister — eleven letters in eleven years, posted with her own hand — and once a year I wrote to her what this house ate at the New Year, the kueh, the whole menu, because it was all I had to send; and she read menus the way other women read scripture. Eleven letters south. No answer in eleven years. Whether they were read, or burned, or never reached the hand — the house never showed a mark. A door is not obliged to tell you who knocks.

"Then the cough came to her. The one with the click in it." Ah Yoke opened her eyes and looked at Mei Foong with the fever bright in them and the irony brighter. "Yes. I know the sound I make. Why do you think I am keeping accounts at this speed?"

---

On the third night she said: "Bring the case."

Mei Foong went up the amahs' stair in the dark and drew the rattan case from under the bed, from against the wall, where a case lives in a servants' room, and carried it down through the sleeping house; and in the box of lamplight she lifted out the folded blouses and the winter things and the comb in its cloth, and worked up the paper lining at the bottom, and took out the bundle she had watched buried there when she was twelve years old and pretending to sleep.

It was the size of two fists, wrapped in dark cloth, wound many times about with red thread, knotted with the deliberate beauty of knots that are not meant to be convenient.

"Untie it," Ah Yoke said. "You have waited eight years. I have waited nine. Everything in it is in order; take it out in order."

The knots gave slowly, remembering the fingers that had tied them. Inside, in order:

A ring of green jade on a cord, warm-toned, a woman's size. Mei Foong lifted it and the lamplight went round it once, and Ah Yoke said, "Put that back. It came off an altar, and its business is not finished. When its day comes you will know the day," and would say nothing else, and Mei Foong laid it back in the cloth like a sleeping thing.

A twist of red paper, soft with age. Inside, a curl of black hair, fine as smoke. "Your first month's shaving. She carried it to her dying and I have carried it since. You weighed nothing," Ah Yoke said, "and that is what it weighs."

A letter, one only, folded small, addressed in the small upright hand Mei Foong had seen once on a red slip in a temple: two characters, no surname. Never posted. "The twelfth," Ah Yoke said. "She wrote it in her last winter and did not send it. She said: the eleven were knocking. This one is for whoever opens the door."

And at the bottom, wrapped alone in a square of faded indigo: a slipper.

A child's slipper. One only, the length of Mei Foong's palm, beaded — and her breath went out of her as if she had been struck, because she knew the pattern the way she knew her own wrist. Peony and phoenix, rust and gold and jade on black, the full-headed flowers, the tail carried like a promise: the pattern the Bibik had set down before her in careful ink; the pattern she had beaded through the whole of nineteen thirty-nine, ten thousand small decisions, another bride's first steps; the pattern of which the Bibik had said, under the lamp, in a voice from an unvisited country, *my sister beaded like this* — all of it here first, years before her, worked in miniature by a dying woman's hands onto a shoe the size of a guava. The beads were smaller than any Mei Foong had ever worked. The sole was unmarked. It had never once touched a floor.

"She made it in the last winter, when her hands could still be trusted by lamplight," Ah Yoke said. "You were eleven. Long past the size of it. I told her so. She said she knew." A rest, the longest yet. "Sick women's hands go back down the years, girl; they make things for the child that was, because the woman that will be is not theirs to sew for. She knew she would not raise you the rest of the way. She knew another house would see your first proper steps, and your bridal ones, if you ever had them, and that she would not be at either. So she made the first step she had missed the least — you walked at ten months, in Kwangtung, on a mud floor, and nobody beaded anything — and she made it perfect, and never showed it to you, and put it under the thread. A bride walks her whole life in her first steps. Somebody in your family used to say that. Now you know which family."

Mei Foong sat on the storeroom floor with her mother's work in her two hands, and did not cry, because the crying was somewhere below the place she had reached, like weather under a mountain.

"The oath," she said. "Finish the account, Ah Yee."

"Three instructions," Ah Yoke said. "She gave them like measures. Level, no seconds. One: take her to my blood. Raise her near it, in reach of it, whether or not it ever knows her — blood is blood, it keeps even in the dark, like gold. Two: never tell. Not the child, not the family, not the sisterhood, no one, ever. My shame is mine; it goes in the ground with me; it does not go onto her. Told, she is the scandal walking, and they will cast her out to bury it. Untold, she is a girl who eats. Choose eating." The old woman's voice had gone down almost to the wick's bead, but the cadence never broke. "Three: a name-paper, once a year, two characters, as written — she wrote the slip herself, that same winter. I asked her: why not the whole name; the dead cannot be disowned twice. And she said: they took the first stone from me while I lived. I will not steal it back for a prayer. Write me as I am."

"Some names are prayers," Mei Foong said.

"And some are debts. Now you have the whole of the sentence, and you know why I could not give it to you on your birthday over noodles." Ah Yoke turned her head on the sacks and looked at her, and the fever and the discipline sat in her eyes together like two old women sharing a bench. "I am breaking her second measure. Look at me doing it; I want a witness who knows what it costs. I kept it thirteen years. But a secret must have a living keeper, girl, or it is not a secret — it is only a thing that never happened. If this lung finishes me and you do not know, then she has no daughter, and you have no mother, and you will sweep your own family's floor to the end of your days with your back present and the rest of you nowhere — nowhere at all, because I let the ledger burn. I will not. Sworn to her or not, I will not let you be a thing that never happened."

She closed her eyes. "The secret has two keepers now. When there is one again, and the one is you, you will decide what I have had to decide, and you will be alone in it, as I have been. That is your inheritance, girl. I am sorry it is not money."

Mei Foong read the letter alone, later, by the bead of the lamp, while Ah Yoke slept her shallow clicking sleep. It was short. *Elder sister. I do not write to be forgiven; eleven letters taught me the price of that stamp. I write so that you will know the child grinds flour with the clever wrist, and beads what we beaded, and that if heaven is ever careless enough to put her where you can see her, you will know whose she is without being told, and be kind by accident, if you cannot be kind on purpose.* It was signed with two characters, no surname.

The letter had never been sent. And the Bibik had dropped a bowl at the sight of a wrist-turn all the same, and looked one beat too long at a girl at a gate, and been kind, by accident, in her own coin: rice for work, a roof, a rule. Every prophecy in the letter had arrived without its envelope. Mei Foong folded it small along its old creases and tied the bundle whole again, the ring inside it, and wound the red thread the way she had seen it wound, knot for knot.

She did not sleep that night. She lay in the amahs' room doing what she did with everything, setting the account in rows, and the rows kept coming out the same. Eight years a servant in her own family's house. Her grandmother's altar two streets away; her aunt upstairs; her aunt's rule — *the girl may stay while she earns her rice* — spoken over her own sister's daughter at the gate. The two dollars the Towkay had paid her for saving his firm, the praise for the amah's girl as proof that anyone can be trained. *You bow to the room, not the tablets. There is a difference and the difference is your whole life here.* Ah Yoke had said that on the first day, flatly, with her thumb moving over her own knuckles, and it had been true, and now it was true the other way, like a seam turned.

And Boon Siew was her cousin.

The thought stood up in her in the dark, unasked, with everything it carried, and she made herself look at it once, squarely. Her cousin: the Bibik's son, her mother's sister's boy. In the Baba houses cousins married — different surnames, the mothers' sides; it was liked; it kept the gold in the family; there were three such marriages within five doors of this one, blessed and feasted and ordinary. If she could be told, she was not a servant reaching over a wall. She was kin, and a match a matchmaker would have carried up the street with a lacquered fan. The door she and he had agreed, in the rain of the godown, could not be walked through — the door had been unlocked her whole life.

And to open it she would have to produce her mother; and her mother could be produced only as scandal, the struck-off name, the Eurasian clerk, the unfinished paper — the very flood the oath had built its wall against, that would land not on the dead but on the living: on Ah Yoke, forsworn; on the Bibik, shamed in her own hall; on herself, cast out to bury it. The one fact that made him possible was the same fact that could never be said. She flinched from the thought the way you take your hand off iron, and folded it, and put it away in the place where she kept things that could not be used — the fullest shelf in her, that place, and the best swept.

---

The crisis came on the seventh night, as the timetable said.

The fever climbed all evening and would not be sponged down, and the click in the breathing grew until every breath was a small latch lifting and failing, and Ah Yoke went away from them into some burning distance — Canton, by the words that came; she scolded novices forty years gone; she counted rice, endlessly, the measures level, no seconds; and once, near the worst of it, her hand came up off the blanket and took Mei Foong's wrist with all its old strength, and she looked straight into her face and said, in a voice of great tenderness and reproach, "Kim. You came back for her. I kept the count. Look how I kept it," and Mei Foong said, steadily, "You kept it, elder sister. Every grain. Sleep now," and the hand let go.

Curfew held Dr. Lim across the town until the pass could be arranged; he came at grey dawn with Boon Siew, and knelt, and listened a long time, moving the stethoscope, and Third Aunt stood in the doorway with her arms folded like a woman prepared to argue with heaven through an interpreter.

The fever broke at noon. It went out of Ah Yoke the way the monsoon goes out of a sky, all at once, leaving everything rinsed and lighter than its own weight; she slept eleven hours, breathing wet but breathing, and woke in the dark asking, in a thread of her own voice, whether the side door had been kept and at what measure. Then, when the others had gone out, her eyes found Mei Foong.

"What did I say?"

"Everything."

"In order?"

"In order. All three nights, and the bundle, and the letter."

Ah Yoke lay looking at the ceiling for a while, doing the audit. "Good," she said at last. "Then heaven has left me standing to teach you the other half. Knowing is one skill, girl. Keeping is a different trade entirely, and I have been thirteen years at it, and you will apprentice properly. Nothing changes in daylight. Not a look. Not a syllable. You will bow where they point and sweep what they show you, and you will know what you know, and knowing is the whole of the work for now." She turned her head. "The bundle goes back to the bottom of the case. The ring stays in it. Everything in its place until its day. Now bring me rice. Congee is for the dying and I have been refused."

*under, 21,* the chart said that evening. *A.Y. took rice.* And under the entry Mei Foong ruled no line at all, because the account was open, and would stay open, and was the only account she kept now in which the credit column held more than the debit, though not one figure of it could ever be shown.

---

By early February Ah Yoke was at the side door again, thinner, slower, propped on a stool that Third Aunt placed and pretended not to have placed, levelling the scoop against her palm with a hand that no longer shook. The queue moved past her like a rosary. The town bowed at its corners and set its clocks to Tokyo and stood in its queues, and the loudspeaker vans came through twice a week now, announcing in three languages the progress of the war in the south: the island invested, the island entered, the island's fall a matter of days. The vans announced many things, and the town had learned what weight to give them; but this one it believed, because the sky said it too — southward, at night, a low orange stain on the world's hem that no wireless was needed to read. Somewhere down that darkness, on the island where the greatest naval base under heaven had been built for exactly this hour, the last of the sixty-nine days was running out; and there was nothing to do about it in Malacca but queue, and bow, and count.

On the second Friday of February, past midnight, Mei Foong rose from her pallet, and took no lamp.

She went down the amahs' stair by memory, the way all the back of the house went, past the shrouded cage where the merbok slept, through the second air well where the blackout made the shaft of sky above her only a deeper black, and through the fourth door, into the ancestral hall.

The dark in there was total, and it did not matter. She had counted this room when she was twelve and it had never left her: the altar against the far wall, the tiers of tablets climbing it like bees on a comb, the urn with its hundred years of ash, the portrait looking down through the blindness with its mild permanent disappointment. The air was sandalwood and old smoke. Under this table she had held her cousin's hand in the bombing while her aunt called the roll of the household's dead — the founders, the grandmothers, her own grandmother among them, named aloud in this room by the daughter who resembled her; and once, between one name and the next, a struck-off name, spoken anyway, because the dead keep even in the dark, like gold.

She lit nothing. Incense leaves ash, and ash is an entry, and this was to go in no book ever kept.

She crossed the cold tiles to the place where the family stood at the rites — the exact flags of the floor where, every festival for eight years, the blood of the house had arranged itself by rank while she stood back among the servants by the door. She stood on that place in the dark, a servant girl in a sleeping occupied house, before the tablets of the ancestors of her cousin, of her aunt, of the grandmother whose ring lay knotted in red thread at the bottom of a rattan case above her head — her people, at one remove she could now count on two fingers; the family that did not know it was hers; the names that would never carry hers.

*You bow to the room, not the tablets. There is a difference, and the difference is your whole life.*

Mei Foong put her feet together the way Ah Yoke stood before Guan Yin, like a schoolgirl, and bowed to the tablets.

She did it three times, slowly, from the waist, lower than any sentry had ever required of her, her back present and the whole of her present with it, claiming nothing — no name spoken, no light lit, no ash left, no entry made; a debt acknowledged and not called in; a daughter of the house, in the house, in the dark, known to no one but the dead and herself. Then she straightened, and stood a moment in the smell of a hundred years of other people's incense that was also, at one remove, hers.

She went back the way she had come, past the two staircases of the house of seven doors — both of them hers now, by an arithmetic no register would ever show — and took the back one.

Three streets away, at the Stadthuys, the loudspeaker vans stood parked and silent in the dark, with tomorrow's announcement already in them.
