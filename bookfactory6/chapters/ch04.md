# Lessons Through a Wall

The primer had Bee Nio's name written inside the cover in a tutor's severe hand, and under it, in pencil, a cat that Bee Nio had drawn at seven and failed to rub out. Mei Foong sat with the book square in front of her on the scrubbed kitchen table, her hands in her lap because she had not yet been told she could touch it, while Boon Siew turned up the kerosene lamp and Ah Yoke, in the corner by the water jars, took out her sewing and said nothing at all.

It was a Tuesday in the wet season of 1936, and the lesson had been arranged the way everything in the house of seven doors was arranged: through walls.

Mei Foong was fourteen and had been two years in the house, long enough to know its grammar. The front hall spoke English and Baba Malay and money; the kitchen court spoke Cantonese and Hokkien and work. Between them ran a wall with a lattice transom near the ceiling, through which the smells of the kitchen were permitted to pass forward, and nothing was supposed to pass back. But sound is not smoke. In her first year, when a tutor still came on Saturdays for Bee Nio — a dry old gentleman who recited the Three-Character Classic as if each line owed him rent — Mei Foong had discovered that the passage beside the middle hall took a very long time to sweep. She swept it every Saturday for a year, holding the floor-brush so it never stopped whispering while her whole mind stood on the other side of the wall, and by the time the tutor was dismissed in favour of the convent school she carried perhaps two hundred characters in her head with nowhere to put them down.

Nowhere but butcher paper. The provision shop wrapped pork bones in sheets of coarse grey paper, and the margins of butcher paper belong to no one. She wrote with a burnt matchstick, at first, then with the stub of a pencil Bee Nio had thrown at a spider and not picked up. She wrote the characters small, in the strip of margin where the grease had not reached, and when the paper had done its work she fed it to the stove and watched her writing go up the chimney, which felt less like loss than like sending letters.

It was one of these sheets that Boon Siew found on a Thursday in October, lying beside the chopping block where she had been called away from it mid-stroke.

He was sixteen that year, in his second-to-last year at the High School, and he came home each afternoon with his satchel strap across his chest, smelling of chalk and bicycle. He had a habit the household indulged because he was the son: he came in through the kitchen court instead of the front door, because the kitchen was where things were true. He put a banana from his tiffin through the bars of the merbok's cage on the way — the bird knew his step and began its bubbling call at the gate — and then he stood at the chopping block for a long time with the butcher paper in his hand.

Mei Foong came back from the well with the water and saw what he was holding, and her stomach dropped the way it had dropped the day she fell off the delivery bicycle in the back lane.

"Who wrote this?"

There were people in the house it would have been safe to name. She was not quick enough to name one. He looked at her silence, at her hands white on the bucket handle, and did the arithmetic — even then his face thought in the open, so that watching him was like watching a man count money in good light.

"These are in the wrong order," he said at last, turning the sheet. "You've put the heart radical down before the top. Who taught you?"

"The wall," she said, because she was frightened, and the truth comes out of the frightened either not at all or all at once.

He did not laugh. That she remembered all her life: he did not laugh, and he did not look pleased in the way people look pleased at a clever animal. He looked at the greasy margin of a sheet of pork-bone paper covered in characters the size of rice grains, and he went a slow, furious red, and he said, "Two years. You've been in this house two years," and walked into the front of the house still holding the paper.

What was said in the front hall she learned only in pieces, through the usual masonry. His mother said the convent took day girls for four dollars a month and was he mad. His father was not home. It was Ah Yoke, summoned to the middle hall and questioned — was the girl idle, had her work suffered, what business had a kitchen girl with a pencil — who settled it, in the flat voice of a woman stating the price of fish: "Her work is done and well done, Bibik. What she does after, at the kitchen table where I can see her, harms no one. I will sit with them."

And the Bibik — this too came through the wall, and Mei Foong turned it over for years — was silent a long moment and then said, "At the kitchen table, then. Not in the front rooms," as if the wall itself had been written into the judgment, and something in her voice had gone away to another room of itself, the way it did sometimes when she looked at Mei Foong a beat too long.

So: the kitchen table, scrubbed after dinner; the kerosene lamp with its wick trimmed by Ah Yoke, who counted lamp oil the way she counted everything; the cast-off primers in a stack — the Three-Character Classic with the cat in it, a copybook of squares, a convent reader with a lighthouse on the cover called *The Coral Island Primer, Book Two*; and Boon Siew on the far side of the table with his sleeves turned back, sixteen years old and deadly serious, teaching because unfairness sat on his chest like a weight and this was the only way he knew to shift it.

"Begin with your name," he said, the first evening. "Everything after belongs to whoever learns it. Your name belongs to you already."

He wrote *Ho Mei Foong* in the first three squares of the copybook, in the up-and-down student hand the High School had put on him, and turned the book around. And Mei Foong, taking the pencil, had a strange half-second in which the kitchen went thin around her — because she had seen her name written once before, in Guangdong, at the bottom of a letter her mother was writing by a window, the brush held in a way she had never seen a village woman hold a brush; and her mother had covered the page with her sleeve, not quickly, but completely, the way you cover a well.

She wrote her name. The strokes came out in the wrong order and the right shape.

"Again," said Boon Siew.

From the corner, the small sounds of Ah Yoke's needle going through cloth, steady as a clock that had decided, for its own reasons, to permit this hour to exist.

---

She was faster than both of them. That was the trouble, and she understood it as trouble almost at once.

Not faster at everything — Boon Siew had eight years of schooling stacked in him, English and geometry and the names of English kings — but faster along the ground, faster from not-knowing to knowing. He would set her ten characters for the week and she would have them by the second evening; the five-times table in a night; the shape of a sum in the time it took him to state it. He taught the way the High School had taught him, in stairs, each step level and sensible, and she went up his stairs two at a time and then stood at the top trying not to look as though she had arrived early.

Because pride was not a thing a kitchen girl could wear. She had watched, in two years, exactly what the house did to servants who were seen to rise — the cook's boy who mimicked the Towkay's cough and was gone by Friday — and she had no illusions that cleverness was different from mimicry to anyone but the clever. So she learned to bank her quickness the way Ah Yoke banked the stove at night: covered, banked, alive under the ash. When Bee Nio joined the lessons and stumbled on a character, Mei Foong stumbled on the next one. When Boon Siew looked from her page to his sister's and back with the comparison arriving on his open face, Mei Foong asked a question she knew the answer to, to give the comparison somewhere else to go.

But the hunger she could not always cover. It had a body of its own. It was in the butcher paper, still — more butcher paper than ever, the margins full of English words copied twice, *lorry, invoice, harbour, examination* — and in the way she did sums against the world all day without meaning to: eggs into ang koo for the feast day, the seventeen steps of the amahs' back stair times the eleven trips she made before noon. It was in her habit — Ah Yoke caught her at it and said nothing, which from Ah Yoke was a whole conversation — of writing characters with a wet fingertip on the zinc of the meat-safe, where they lived for a breath and dried into nothing. The stove got the butcher paper; the air got the rest. She was fourteen, eating knowledge out of the margins of a rich family's life, and she would have been ashamed of the hunger if there had been anyone to see the whole of it. There was no one. She made sure.

Twice a week became three evenings, then four. The abacus came down from the account room in November — Boon Siew carried it in grinning, because here at last was a thing the High School had *not* taught him; he had learned it small from the firm's chief clerk and loved it the way you love what you learn before you learn that learning is work. He set it on the table with a clatter of rods.

"Soft hand," he said. "The clerk says the beads should sound like rain, not like quarrelling."

Her hand was soft in three evenings. It was kitchen work, when she thought about it — the same as flicking water off washed greens, the same wrist Ah Yoke was always after her about with the flour. By the New Year she could carry a chain of figures up and down the rods faster than Boon Siew could call them out of the newspaper, rubber at so many cents a pound, tin at so many dollars a picul, and he would put the paper down and stare at her, and she would say the total again slowly, as if unsure, to give the moment a way to be about the number instead of about her.

"You're doing it again," he said once.

"Doing what?"

"Arriving first and waiting for me around the corner." He said it without heat, looking at the beads. "You do it for Bee Nio. You needn't do it for me."

She looked at Ah Yoke's needle, going in and out. "It isn't for you," she said, which was as near the truth as the kitchen table permitted, and he thought about that and let it be.

---

Bee Nio at thirteen was two people, and Mei Foong, who had been fond of her since the first week — you could not help it; her weather changed hourly but it was always genuine weather — never knew which one would come down the stair.

The first Bee Nio arrived with her convent exercise book and her voice pitched high for company, joined the lessons as if conferring a favour on the table, and was, in her own territory, a real teacher. The convent had put things in her that Boon Siew's school had not: romanized Malay in a running hand, English hymn-words, a way of holding a pen like a wand. "Not like that, Foong, you write English like it's Chinese, it *slides*, look—" and she would cover Mei Foong's hand with her own and drag it through *the quick brown fox*, the two girls' heads nearly touching over the copybook, and for half an hour the kitchen was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the stove. Bee Nio taught her *selamat pagi* and *terima kasih* in the roman letters, and the English months, and a hymn about eternal fathers strong to save which Mei Foong found beautiful and baffling in equal parts. Teaching, Bee Nio was at her best; being at her best, she knew it; knowing it, she came again.

The second Bee Nio came down on the evenings when the first had been wounded somewhere above — a cold word from her mother, a poor mark, the discovery, always fresh, that her brother's attention was a country with other citizens in it. This one did not join. This one perched on the end of the table swinging her slippered foot and found the lesson stupid, the lamp smoky, the kitchen hot; this one asked Boon Siew to help with her needlework map of the Peninsula *now*, tonight, it was due, Sister said; this one, once, tipped the water cup across Mei Foong's finished copybook page with a movement that was almost, almost an accident, and watched the characters drown with her chin up and her eyes bright and miserable.

Boon Siew rose fast that night with his mouth open, and it was Mei Foong who got there first, under the table, quick and level: "The page was full. I needed the next one anyway." And to Bee Nio, holding out the wet cloth from the rack, ordinary as Monday: "Wipe it, then test me on the months. I keep losing August."

Because she had understood the thing Boon Siew, who had never shared anyone, had not: that Bee Nio was not cruel; Bee Nio was *outnumbered*. She had claimed Mei Foong two years ago — *my girl*, half friendship and half ownership and never settled which — and had assumed, the way you assume the floor, that her brother was hers too; and now her girl and her brother had built a small bright country at the kitchen table with a border she could not find, and everything in her that was thirteen and lonely and third in every line threw water at it. Mei Foong knew what it was to stand at a wall and hear the life on the other side. Bee Nio knew a version of it, standing in her own house; and so Mei Foong dried the page without complaint and lost August on purpose, twice.

Later, on the back stair, Bee Nio caught her wrist in the dark, and said in a rush, furious with herself, "I'll test you on all the months. And the days. Sister makes us do the days till we cry," which was, in the currency of the house's daughters, an apology of nearly a dollar, and Mei Foong banked it with the rest.

---

The newspaper began in the dry season, and no one ever said one word about it, which is how she knew it meant something.

The Towkay took two papers: the Chinese paper for the front hall and the *Straits Echo* for himself, and when the house had finished with them they came to the kitchen court to line shelves and wrap fish, seniority running downhill like water. A paper might be three days dead before the kitchen got it. That was the order of things.

Then in March, or thereabouts, the order changed by four inches. Boon Siew read the *Echo* in the evening on the bench by the merbok cage, and where he had used to leave it folded on the hall table for the boy to clear, he began to leave it on the third step of the amahs' back stair — the step where the lamp from the kitchen door fell, the step she passed eleven times before noon — folded not anyhow but to a page, sometimes to a column: shipping movements, the rubber quotations, once a long report of a debate in the Legislative Council with the hard words underlined in soft pencil, *tariff, expenditure, constituency*, like stones set in a stream where a person might want to cross.

Nothing was said. That was the whole of it: nothing was ever said. She read standing in the kitchen doorway in the ten minutes between the washing-up and the lamps, refolded the paper along its own creases so exactly that it could deny everything, and set it back on the step; and the next evening it was there again, folded to a new place. Once, being careless, she left it refolded to a column of her own choosing — a notice about a night class at the Chinese school, which she had read four times with her heart going strangely — and the next day the paper did not appear, and she scrubbed the whole staircase in a cold fright at her own boldness; and the day after that it was back, folded small and hard to the shipping page, one line under the arrivals in soft pencil: a steamer in from Hong Kong and Swatow, the ports of home. As if to say: *seen*. As if to say: nothing at all. It was nothing at all — a folded newspaper on a step — and she knew it with total clarity, and she began coming down the stair each evening the way you come down a stair when the step might be bare: braced, and ashamed of being braced, and lighter than the stair had ever made her when it wasn't.

Ah Yoke, who missed nothing that happened on that staircase, said only, once, without looking up from the sewing: "Learn everything. Say nothing." And then, as if it were part of the same sentence, bit the thread.

---

The Towkay discovered the lessons on an evening in June, and the strange thing, Mei Foong thought afterwards, was that they had been going on for eight months in his own house, four evenings a week, one wall from his newspaper chair, and he had not known. But that was the wall doing what the wall was for. Nothing was supposed to pass back.

He came down the passage at nine at night with a chit in his hand, hunting the cook — a dispute with the chandler over kerosene, the kind of forty-cent dispute he pursued the way other men pursued tigers, because he had been poor at ten and had never told anyone what it was like, only itemized it — and he stopped in the kitchen doorway, and the lesson looked up.

The lamp. The primers. His son with his sleeves turned back, mid-word. His daughter, tongue between her teeth, halfway through a sum she would not have finished. The amah in the corner with the sewing gone still. And the kitchen girl with the pencil in her hand and the abacus at her elbow, in the light.

The silence went on long enough for Mei Foong to hear the merbok shift on its perch in the dark of the court.

"Whose doing is this," said the Towkay, not loudly. He was a heavy man who wore even his singlet like a man expecting a photographer, and his displeasures were public property; the whole kitchen had priced this one before he finished the sentence.

"Mine," said Boon Siew, standing. He had gone red along the cheekbones, but his voice came out level, and he did a thing Mei Foong had never seen anyone do to the Towkay: he did not explain. He stood there, sixteen, with the fact, and let his father be the one to want more.

The Towkay looked at the table for some time. Then he crossed to it, put down the chit he was carrying, and turned it with two fingers so that it faced Mei Foong.

"Read it."

The chit was from the chandler, in the mixed scrawl such papers came in, Chinese and figures and a line of Malay. Her mouth went dry as flour. She read it. Kerosene, two tins; wick, one packet; the figures; the total. Her voice did what she made it do, which was walk slowly and not fall.

"She reads it," said the Towkay, to no one, in the tone of a man confirming a lorry part. Then, because he was who he was: "The total. Check it."

She did not need the abacus, but she used it, because the beads gave her somewhere to look. The rods clicked in the quiet like rain beginning. She came up the last column and stopped, and understood — in one long second in which she saw, very clearly, the cook's boy who had been gone by Friday — that there was no safe direction. Wrong was useless; right was visible. The only choice left her was what to be visible *as*.

"The total is right," she said. "The chit is wrong."

The Towkay's face did not change. "The figures add."

"The figures add. But we took delivery of one tin, not two." She kept her eyes on the paper. "The second was short-shipped; the chandler's boy said it would follow with Thursday's cart. It didn't. I carried the tin in myself; we are burning it now." She turned the chit slightly, the way he had turned it to her. "He has billed for both."

The silence this time had a different texture. Bee Nio was looking from face to face like a spectator at badminton. Ah Yoke's needle had stopped altogether. And the Towkay stood looking down at the greasy chit, at the forty avoidable cents that his own front office had initialled and passed for payment, and Mei Foong watched something happen behind his heavy face that she would spend years learning to price: not pleasure, nothing so warm; a shifting of weights; the sound, almost audible, of a man moving an item from one column of the world to another.

The front hall believed paper. The kitchen knew what came off the cart. And here, apparently, drawn up neat at his own kitchen table, was the place where the two could be made to agree — for the cost of the lamp oil she was already burning.

"Who checks the chits before payment," he said, in the direction of the passage, of the absent clerk, of the house itself.

No one answered. It had, as a question, never existed before.

He picked up the chit, folded it once, and looked at Mei Foong for what she realized was the first time in two years — looked at her, not at the work in her hands.

"From the first of the month, the kitchen chits come to you before they go to the front," he said. "Provision, chandler, fishmonger, all of it. You will mark what was delivered against what is billed, and you will mark it in writing." A pause, itemized. "The lamp oil for these evenings goes to the household account. Not to hers." A tilt of his head at Ah Yoke, who bowed hers, expressionless, over the sewing. And, at the door, without turning, to his son, in the tone of a man approving an invoice: "Teach her the abacus properly. The clerk is getting old."

Then he was gone down the passage, and the kitchen let out its breath, and Bee Nio said, into the ringing quiet, "Well, *I'm* not checking any chits," with such perfect thirteen-year-old dignity that Boon Siew sat down and laughed until the lamp shook, and after a moment, cautiously, in the corner, even Ah Yoke's shoulders moved once, like a roof settling.

Mei Foong did not laugh. She sat with her hand flat on the abacus to still it and felt the house rearrange itself around her by one small, enormous room.

---

The chits began coming on the first of the month, as decreed: a wooden tray on the shelf by the meat-safe, and the kitchen accounts of the house of seven doors passing through the hands of the amah's girl on their way to the money. She checked each figure twice, once forward and once backward, a habit she invented that first week out of pure fright and kept for sixty years. She caught the fishmonger's rounding in the first fortnight and the provision shop's thumb on the scale in the second, and she recorded both in the copybook Boon Siew ruled into columns for her — date, item, billed, received, difference — in characters that had steadied now into a small upright disciplined hand.

By August the tray held godown dockets too, sent down without comment: rubber sheet tallies, lorry mileage, the price of kerosene by the drum. She sat with them in the evenings after the lesson, the primers pushed to one side and the family's money spread where the Three-Character Classic had been, and learned it the way she learned everything, through the wall of it, from the margins in: what the house spent, what the house feared, where the house bled forty cents at a time and never felt it. The Towkay had opened the door for the same reason he did everything, because it added; he had no idea — she barely had an idea herself, bent to the lamp, fifteen that autumn, entering the difference column in her small clean figures — what he had let through it.

On the third step of the back stair, the newspaper went on appearing, folded to the quotations now, rubber and tin, as if to say: *these too*. As if to say nothing at all. She read it standing in the doorway with the lamp behind her, and refolded it along its own creases, exact to the corner, so that it could deny everything; and carried the numbers up the seventeen steps in her head, where no one could see how much she owned.
