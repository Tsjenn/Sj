# Two Rooms in Malacca

The express from Puduraya came in at nine minutes past nine, and Su-Lin watched her grandmother come down its steps the way a surveyor comes onto a site: handbag across the body, case in the left hand, eyes going over the ground for trip hazards before they went anywhere else.

"You did not need to meet me," Mei Lan said. She did not surrender the case. "I have travelled by bus before."

She was sixty-two and stood like a woman who had run a household by rota for forty years: beige trousers that did not crease, hair set on the Thursday, a cardigan over her arm in thirty-two degrees because buses were cold.

"I have a ticket for the six-thirty," she said. "I have told your mother I am at Auntie Poh's in Klang, and I lie badly, so we will use the day. You will show me this house, I will hear whatever you have arranged to say to me, and I will go home tonight and we will have sorted this nonsense out."

"Yes, Ah Ma."

"Good." She settled the strap on her shoulder. "Which way is the river?"

---

The telephone call from the airwell had lasted eleven minutes and gone nowhere. Her grandmother had said no in three different ways, each of them correct — she did not travel now, the drive tired her, whatever was in a dead woman's cupboards was a matter for a lawyer — and when Su-Lin said, *I am asking you to come and look at something*, the line had held a silence with weight in it, the way a beam holds before it shows anything; and then her grandmother had said, "Lock the house properly," and put the receiver down.

So Su-Lin had drafted a letter, four times, and every version came out either a legal opinion or an accusation; and on the fifth night she stopped writing and photographed the child's slipper instead. Black cloth on white card, the peony no bigger than a five-sen coin, her steel rule laid alongside so that the size could not be argued with. Twelve centimetres. A foot that had barely learned to stand. On the back of the print she copied, stroke by stroke from the back of the wedding photograph, the line that had waited there sixty years, and posted it to the tidy house off the Ipoh road with no letter in the envelope.

Nine days, nothing. Then on the Thursday night the Nokia lit up in the airwell at ten past eight, in the middle of the evening programmes, and her grandmother's voice said, perfectly level:

"What time does the first bus get in."

---

They came into the old town on foot, over the bridge, because Mei Lan said the taxi men were robbers, and she said nothing for six streets except, once, "There was a cinema there," in the tone of a woman correcting a document.

At the top of Heeren Street she paused. The terrace ran away in its long unequal line, shutters and drying poles and tour groups in file down the five-foot way; and halfway along, the mansion stood behind its gate with the state's blue plaque bolted beside the door and a laminated notice about admission prices.

Mei Lan looked at it for perhaps two seconds. "They have painted it wrong," she said, and walked on.

Two doors before the shop, Uncle Raju was in the good chair by his door with the crossword. He looked up as they came level, put the paper down, and stood — a man of eighty who stood up for nobody.

"Miss Mei Lan," he said.

Her grandmother did not break stride, but her chin came round. "The coffee shop had a marble counter."

"It still has."

"Yes," said Mei Lan, and went on; and Su-Lin, following, saw her grandmother's hand go to the strap of the handbag and take hold of it, the way you take hold of a rail.

---

The shop door stood open on its hook, and the morning came in over the sill and laid its long rectangle down the boards.

Mei Lan stopped in the doorway and did not go in.

Su-Lin, four feet inside, understood after a moment that it was not hesitation. Her grandmother stood with her head slightly up, the way a person stands in the first second of a room, and the room was giving her what it gave everyone: camphor, then dust, then starch, then that faint metal-and-floral smell of many thousand glass beads — which Su-Lin had stopped smelling in her third week and smelled again now, borrowed, through somebody else's face.

Then Mei Lan came in and began, at speed, to be efficient.

She wanted the frontage: fifteen feet, Su-Lin said. The depth: eighty. Whether the party wall was shared, what the assessment rates were, whether they were paid up. Told about the roof, she said that in this town the roof was never the problem, the drains were the problem, and went the whole eighty feet to look at them with her handbag on her arm.

She kept it there for an hour and ten minutes. She said the pots in the airwell wanted repotting, and that one cup was a sad thing in a kitchen; she tested the third tread of the stair with her foot and did not go up. And she touched nothing — not the counter, not the lasts on the wall, not the trays under the green-edged glass with the beads running rust to rose to the blue that was almost night. She stood in the middle of that floor like a woman who had been told there was wet paint on everything.

"The wiring is original," Su-Lin said. "Devan warned me about the fuses."

"Then it is not original, it is dangerous, and you will get it done first, and not by anybody's cousin." Mei Lan turned round once more on the boards. "What was in the tin, then. You said on the telephone there were letters. Whose?"

And Su-Lin, who had rehearsed a sentence in the car for three weeks — *Ah Ma, the woman who kept this shop was your mother* — opened her mouth and found that it would not do it. What came out instead was:

"There's a pen on the counter."

Her grandmother's eyes went to it: the fountain pen lying on its square of blotting paper by the ledger stand, black, worn silver at the cap band, where it had lain since before Su-Lin ever put a key in the door.

"I keep meaning to have the sac replaced," Su-Lin said. "It doesn't write."

Mei Lan set her handbag down on the counter glass.

She picked the pen up, turned it to the light from the door, unscrewed the cap with a small practised movement, and looked at the nib the way a person looks at a face across a station platform. Su-Lin did not move at all.

"Nineteen thirty-eight," her grandmother said. "English composition."

"Ah Ma?"

"There was a certificate for it in the front hall of that house up the street. Under glass, with the school crest." Mei Lan screwed the cap back on and set the pen down on its blotting paper, squared to the edge, and stood with both hands flat on the counter glass over the trays of beads. "I asked her about it. I was twelve. I said, why is my dead uncle's prize pen in your shop, and she said —" and here her voice did something Su-Lin had never heard it do, went thin at one edge like paper "— she said, because a pen goes with a ledger."

The street made its morning noise outside. A trishaw went past with its sound system playing.

"I had an amah in this town," Mei Lan said. "I loved her."

---

It came out of her standing up, in the flat correcting voice she used for everything; and Su-Lin did not sit down either, because sitting down would have made it an occasion.

She had woken her every morning, her grandmother said. Ten to six, the side gate, the plaits — two, always two, the ribbon blue for weekdays — the shoes checked, the walk to the convent gate; and then the amah crossed to the far side of the road and stood there, as a servant stands, until the girls had all gone in. Every day for eleven years. Mei Lan had not thought about that road-crossing in half a century and could still see the woman standing on the other side of it.

"When I was fourteen my grandfather moved the family to Kuala Lumpur. Business follows government — that was the sentence; we all learned it. My mother told me Auntie Foong had her own business now and could not be expected to leave it." She turned one hand over on the glass. "The family let her go. That is what I understood, and I was fourteen, and I understood it completely. I cried for a month, in a house with nine rooms and nobody in it who had ever had to be quiet about crying."

"Did you write to her?"

"Three times. She answered twice." Mei Lan's mouth did something dry. "Half a page. *The shop is well. Study hard. Do not walk in the sun without a hat.* Like a receipt. I stopped after the third."

She had come back to Malacca twice in forty-eight years, she said, both times for funerals, and both times the woman from this shop had been at the back of the hall in a grey coat, and had left before the food.

"I was at the front doing the thanking. That is where the daughter of the house stands." Mei Lan looked down the shop at the airwell light. "I looked for her afterwards, both times, and both times she had gone; and I said to myself: that is how the old servants are, they come out of respect and they do not stay. I have said that sentence to myself for twenty-six years. It is a very comfortable sentence."

"Nobody talked about her at home," Su-Lin said. It was not a question.

"Nobody would speak of her at all. My mother changed the subject the way she changed a plate — completely, with both hands, and you did not get it back." She turned from the counter and faced her granddaughter. "That whole house was loose tiles, Su-Lin. There was no photograph of my uncle's face anywhere in it. There was a tablet in the hall with a woman's name on it — Neo Guat Kim, no dates — and I asked my grandfather who she was, and he said, *your grandmother had a sister*, and that is the entire answer I got in his lifetime. I asked my father where I was born and he said Ayer Keroh; I asked my mother and she said the town. I was fourteen and I could do subtraction, and I knew somebody in that family was lying to me about me." She put the handbag back on her arm. "Then we moved, and there was a new country and a new school and boys, and I put it down. You put things down. Forty-seven years later you cannot remember where the thing is, only that you are not going to look."

Su-Lin said, carefully, "And when I telephoned you in March—"

"When you telephoned me in March," said her grandmother, "I knew you had found it."

---

They ate at the kopitiam, where Mei Lan asked three questions about the price of the coffee and none about anything else, and Uncle Raju did not once look over, which Su-Lin took for the greatest act of tact she had ever seen on that street. Then they went back, and Su-Lin cleared the counter.

She did it the way she cleared a table for a client, because it was the only competence she had left, and she set the papers out along the glass, left to right, a hand apart, each square to the counter edge, talking while she did it in the flat voice she used for a presentation, because the flat voice would carry her further than any other.

The deed first: the 1948 conveyance, Devan's strongroom copy beside the original from the tin, the recital read to the end — *in recognition of long and faithful service to the grantor and to the firm, and in particular of the preservation of the accounts in the flood of 1938* — and the name of the grantee: Ho Mei Foong, spinster, of Heeren Street.

Then the lacquered box, open, with the child's slipper on its silk.

Then the registration paper on its foam cradle, Dr Sim's report, and Puan Aini's photographs of three occupation-era registrations laid alongside it: same form series, same ruling, same chop at the same habitual tilt. *I cannot certify that a document tells the truth. I can tell you this one is consistent in every material respect with late 1942, and that nothing about it is imitating anything.*

Then the photograph, both halves up, the crease running between the young man in the high collar and the girl of nineteen in the borrowed kebaya. Then the letters in their rank down the glass, oldest at the left, fifty envelopes with two brushed characters and no street and no stamp, and beside them the exercise books in which Su-Lin had built them back into English one character at a time.

Her grandmother touched none of it. She went down the rank slowly, the way people go along a wall in a museum, and stopped at the lacquered box, and opened her handbag.

Out of it came a bank's plastic envelope, and out of that the print; and Mei Lan laid the photograph of the slipper on the glass beside the slipper itself and moved it a half-inch until the two were square, and looked at them together — the thing and its picture, checked off against one another by a woman who had checked deliveries against invoices all her life.

"You wrote on the back of this," she said.

"I copied it. From the wedding photograph. My characters are bad."

"They are terrible." Mei Lan turned the print over, and read it, and put it face down, and left her hand on it. "Say the thing you have brought me here to say."

And Su-Lin could not.

She had the whole of it — three months of nights, a wall opened at ten past midnight, a count closed twice with a pencil point — and she stood in her great-grandmother's shop with the documents squared to the counter edge and could not get the sentence up out of herself. Spoken, it would take something off this woman and never give it back, and Su-Lin's mouth had worked out before the rest of her did that she was not entitled to be the one who said it.

"I'm going to read you something instead," she said. "The first letter. It's from 1941 — the only one from before the war."

Her grandmother put both hands on the counter edge and said, "Read it."

*Tonight I write what I cannot say aloud in this house. You gave me a pen to keep your father's figures with, and for nine months I have used it on his figures only, which is one use for a pen and not the best of them. You are to be married in the dry season to a girl in Ipoh with a good name and gramophone records, and I have entered the date in the household book with the other engagements, in my own hand, correctly. I have no quarrel with the arrangement. Correct things have never once required my permission.*

*I set it down only because a thing written down stops walking about the room at night.*

*I am the amah's girl and you are the son of the house, and there is no year yet made in which those two sentences will let a third one stand between them. So I will not say it. I have not said it. I have said it here.*

*Paper is patient. It can wait for a braver year.*

The house ticked. Out at the back the airwell was taking the first of the afternoon rain, coming down the shaft in its fine mist.

"Who is she writing to?" said Mei Lan.

"The envelopes all say the same two characters. Every one of them, for fifty years."

Her grandmother turned the topmost envelope towards her with one finger and looked at the two characters a long moment; and Su-Lin remembered that this was a woman who had given up Chinese at fifteen and then spent four years of Saturday mornings trying to make her granddaughter keep it.

"Boon Siew," Mei Lan said. "That is my uncle. He died in the war. Before I was born."

"Yes."

"He was nineteen or twenty. There is a tablet in that hall." Her hand stayed on the envelope. "Read me another one."

Su-Lin turned to the page she had spent four nights on, and read the letter from September 1945.

*Dear husband. The flags have changed again. I am still your amah. Our daughter called another woman Mother today, and I said nothing, and the saying-nothing is the loudest thing I have ever done. Your name has gone up on a tablet in the front hall of your father's house, and I clean it, and nobody wonders why the amah cleans it so well — cleaning is what I am for. The rice has come back to the town. The truth has not. I have checked the accounts twice: it is not coming. I will keep them anyway. — Your wife, in the only office that never changed flags.*

Su-Lin finished, and put the exercise book down on the glass, and did not look up.

Her grandmother did not move.

She stood exactly as she had been standing, both hands on the counter edge, her head a little forward, her face in the lamp's light doing nothing whatever; and the rain came down the airwell and got louder, and somewhere in the long dark of the house a shutter moved on its hook. Su-Lin counted, because she counted things now. It was nearer two minutes than one.

Then Mei Lan said, in a voice with the sand taken out of it:

"Read the first one again. The one from 1941."

Su-Lin read it again.

Halfway through, her grandmother's hand went, without her seeming to notice, to the little cloth slipper on its silk, and rested there, one finger against the sole; and Su-Lin, reading, saw her lips move very slightly and knew what she was doing, because it was what Su-Lin had done on graph paper with a pencil point, twice, until the count closed.

Nineteen forty-one, before the war. February 1942, an altar, a photograph with a fold in it. The eleventh month of 1942, a registration paper with a mother's name in it and an empty column where a father should be. And the eleventh month of 1942, in a family that had told the story a thousand times: the war baby, the miracle, heaven's repayment. *Your great-great-grandmother lit joss sticks for a year.*

"The month on that paper," Mei Lan said. "Say it."

"The eleventh month. November 1942."

"And nobody ever told me the day of the week." Her grandmother's finger moved once on the little sole and stopped. "Forty-seven years I have not asked anybody a question about myself. I want you to understand it was not stupidity."

"I know."

"I do not think you do." Mei Lan straightened up. Her face had not changed at all, which was the worst of it: it was the face she wore for hospitals and other people's grief. "Su-Lin. I would like the night in this house."

---

Su-Lin argued, and lost, and heard herself losing: the fuses were forty years old, the mattress upstairs was a site mattress, there were mosquitoes off the drain and a perfectly good hotel four minutes away with two rooms in it held in her name for a fortnight. She said, at the very end, in a voice that came out younger than she wanted, "Ah Ma, you have a ticket for the six-thirty."

Her grandmother took the bus ticket out of the handbag and did not tear it. She laid it flat on the counter glass and squared it to the edge with two fingers, and Su-Lin watched that small movement and said nothing about it at all.

"Show me the fuse box," said Mei Lan.

So Su-Lin showed her the fuse box, and the kettle, and the tap that had to be turned hard and then back a quarter, and Hafiz's number on the wall by the telephone in carpenter's pencil, and how the bolt on the front door went across.

"It's the first door she ever locked from the inside," Su-Lin said, before she could stop herself. "It's in one of the letters."

Her grandmother took the keys.

At the door Su-Lin turned round with the whole of it rising in her, and got as far as "Ah Ma, if you want me to stay, I can—"

"Leave everything on the counter," said Mei Lan. "And go."

---

Hafiz came at seven with a tiffin carrier, because his mother had heard from the coffee shop that there was a lady down from Kuala Lumpur, and the street's intelligence service ran, as usual, six hours ahead of anyone's intentions.

"Kuih. Two kinds. I'm only the postman." Then he looked down the five-foot way at the shop, shutter three-quarters down and the lamp burning behind it, and at Su-Lin in the road with nowhere at all to be.

"She's staying in the house," Su-Lin said. "Alone. She's sixty-two and she asked and I said yes."

Hafiz considered the shutter a moment.

"Then we walk," he said.

They walked. Jonker was shut to traffic and doing its Friday business, stalls the whole length of it, karaoke out of the association hall at a volume that had defeated three generations of complaint. They ate the kuih standing by the bridge. By eleven the trestles were coming down and the street was a street again, wet and littered and ordinary.

He did not ask her one question about any of it. He talked about the house instead — the purlin, the lime mix, a shophouse two streets over with a 1920s tile floor that somebody would skip if the trust did not move — and Su-Lin understood somewhere around midnight that he had chosen a subject that could be talked about all night, and was talking about it all night on purpose.

They passed the top of Heeren Street at twelve, and at one, and at half past two, and each time she looked down the terrace at the one shopfront with light behind its shutter, lying out in a bar across the five-foot way. At two they sat under a fan three streets over.

"You keep looking at your watch," he said.

"Working out where she's got to. Fifty letters. At my speed that was three months." Su-Lin turned the cup on its saucer, caught herself doing it, and put it down. "She reads better than I do. She gave it up at fifteen."

At ten to three the light was still on, and the town had emptied out to the drains and one lorry unloading past the bridge.

"Go to the hotel," Hafiz said. "You'll be no use to her at six standing in a road."

He waited until she went. Su-Lin let herself into her room and put the second room's key on the desk beside her own, both in their paper folders, both kept since the Friday she had first asked for them, neither slept in that night by anybody at all. At twenty past five she got up and washed her face and went out into a town that smelled of rain that had not yet arrived.

---

The five-foot way opposite the shophouse had a step in it, and Su-Lin sat down on it at ten to six with her back against a shut shutter.

The shop's light was still on. The sky over the roofline went from nothing to grey and then to the colour of the inside of a shell; a shutter went up four doors along; the kopitiam's charcoal caught and put its smoke straight up into air with no wind in it.

At twelve minutes past six the light in the shop went out.

Su-Lin stood up.

The shutter went up from the inside, hauled unevenly by somebody who had not done it in forty-eight years, and stuck, and came the rest of the way; and the door was pulled back on its hook, and her grandmother stood in the opening in yesterday's trousers, sleeves pushed to the elbow, hair not set at all, and the whole night behind her in the room: the letters back in their rank, the lamp cooling, the tin standing open on the glass with its lid beside it.

Mei Lan had been crying, and had stopped, and had washed her face at the tap that had to be turned hard and then back a quarter; and she stood holding the door frame with one hand, and looked across the road at her granddaughter in the first of the light.

"I remember her hands," she said.

Su-Lin came off the kerb as far as the middle of the road and stopped, because her grandmother had put up the other hand, flat, the way she stopped waiters and traffic and grief.

"Sixty-two years, and I could not have told you her face without a photograph," Mei Lan said. "But I know her hands. Doing my plaits. Six o'clock in the morning, every morning, both of them, here—" she brought her own hands up briefly beside her head and put them down again "—and the shoes, and the ribbon. I have known those hands my whole life and never once asked myself why I knew them."

The kopitiam's radio came on two doors down, low, the six-thirty news in Malay.

"Take me to where she is buried," said Mei Lan.
