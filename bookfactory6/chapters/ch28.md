# The Deed

The houseboy came for her at half past ten at night and said only that the Towkay wanted the amah in the office, which was a sentence with no precedent in it; nobody wanted the amah at half past ten. Mei Foong dried her hands on the cloth that hung for the purpose and went along the back passage under the white paper still pasted over the lintels, three months old now and curling at the corners, the way mourning does when a house has resumed its meals.

He was at the desk with the lamp pulled close, and he had the drawer in front of him — not a drawer's contents; the drawer itself, lifted out of the carcass of his dead wife's blackwood chest and set on the blotter like a thing brought in for identification.

"Shut the door," Tan Hock Lye said. "Sit."

She shut the door. She did not sit; then she saw that he intended to wait for it, and sat on the edge of the chair with her hands in her lap. In fourteen years she had sat down twice in that room, both times on the clerk's stool by the wall, which was where the amah's part of the furniture began and ended.

The drawer held letters. She knew them across a desk's width the way one knows a face in a crowd before knowing it: eleven envelopes gone the colour of weak tea, each addressed in two characters and no surname, each cancelled at a Kwangtung post office in a year when she had been small enough to be carried. Beside them lay a twelfth sheet, unfolded, soft at the creases from a night of being read.

"I am sorting my wife's papers," he said. "One drawer was locked and the key was in her sewing box under the thread, which is where she kept every key she had, including the ones to my house." He squared the unfolded sheet with one finger and did not look at it. "Read the fourth line."

"I can read it from here, Towkay."

"Then read it aloud."

"'The child grinds flour with the clever wrist,'" said Mei Foong, "'and beads what we beaded.'"

"Not that one. The line after. The one with a birth date in it."

She let a breath go and gave it to him level, no seconds, the way a measure is given. "'Her name is Ho Mei Foong. She was born in the fifth month of the year of the dog, in the village, in the rain.'"

The lamp made its small circle. Somewhere in the house a shutter was being closed by someone with no idea what room she was standing outside of.

"I have been lied to by men with lawyers and sons in my own clan hall," he said, "and never once by anyone who was not surprised to find me counting. So do not trouble. It is a family letter from a woman to her elder sister, and the elder sister kept it thirty years under a lock, and there is only one thing in this world it can be."

"It is true," said Mei Foong.

He had been braced forward on both forearms, and the bracing went out of him so completely that she understood he had spent the night rehearsing an argument, and that the argument had been his handrail, and she had just taken it away.

"Say it in words."

"Neo Guat Kim was my mother. Neo Guat Eng was my mother's elder sister. Your wife was my aunt. Your son and your daughter were my cousins." Her hands stayed still in her lap. "I have never taken anything out of this house that was not given to me."

"I did not ask you that."

"No. You would have, in a week. I have kept your books eleven years, Towkay. I know the order the questions come in."

He got up, which took longer than it had before the war, and stood at the window with his back to her. When he spoke again the voice had gone flat, and she recognised the flatness: it was the account-room voice, the one that read a column aloud to see whether it would confess.

"How long?"

"Six years. Ah Yoke told me in the second month of 'forty-two, in the storeroom, when she was dying that week and got better. She had sworn to my mother never to tell me. She broke the oath because she held that a secret with no living keeper is only a thing that never happened."

"And before that?"

"Before that I was a girl at your gate who was allowed to stay while she earned her rice."

His shoulders did not move. "Did my wife know?"

There it was — the question she had turned in her hands a hundred times since January, in the sickroom, in the procession, in the kitchen where the recipes now belonged to her. She found she could answer it exactly, which was a mercy she had not expected.

"At the end she called me Kim," said Mei Foong. "Nobody in the house corrected her. I answered to it."

He was quiet a long moment. "She never said your name to me. Not in forty years." He came back and sat down heavily. "Understand me, because you are the only person left in Malacca who will understand it correctly. I am not angry with my wife. My wife did what her mother did to her sister and her grandmother before that: she shut a door and then stood against it for forty years so that nobody would see it was only a door. That is not wickedness. That is the trade we are all in." He put two fingers on the drawer's edge. "I am angry that I have been kept out of my own accounts by people who believed they were protecting me. All my life I am the last man in the room to be told anything, because I am the one who pays, and nobody tells the payer."

---

He did not send for her again for nine days, and she was afterwards grateful for the noise they made.

In the middle of June the papers carried the killing of planters at their desks up in Perak, and by the end of that week the government had declared an emergency — first in two states and then across the whole Federation, the word reaching the coffee shops well ahead of the proclamation on the wall. Police stood on the trunk road with rifles and a trestle table. The estate lorries came in late and came in searched. And with the emergency came, as Dr. Lim had promised on a night of wet mortar three years before, the forms: every person of twelve years and upward to be registered, photographed, thumb-printed, and to carry the card of it on the body, and to produce the card at a table when a man with a rifle asked.

Mei Foong read the notices twice, as she read everything. A nation of documents, she thought; and under it, permitted once and then put away on the swept shelf: I am a document too. In circulation since 1934, and no clerk has ever held me to the light.

On the tenth day the houseboy came in daylight, and the desk upstairs was clear of everything but one sheet of the firm's ruled paper covered in the Towkay's own hand — the square, slow, self-taught hand of a man who had learned his figures in a shed and never learned to trust anyone else to enter them.

"Sit. I have been doing an audit. Read it."

Three entries, dated, in the form he used for the firm.

*1938. Godown, flood. Sheets ruined, buyer refused, buyer sued. Books brought out of the water and the tally re-made from the weighbridge slips in three nights. Case abandoned. Exposure: eleven thousand four hundred.*

*1941–45. Inventory of the goods and metal behind the false wall and in the mattresses. Kept, corrected, produced entire in 'forty-six. Firm restarted upon it. Value: not calculable; without it there is no firm to restart.*

*1947. Claims. The tribunal takes contemporaneous records only. We had contemporaneous records. Awarded in full, where every other Chinese house on this street was cut or refused.*

"Three times," said Tan Hock Lye. "I have been through it twice and I cannot make it fewer. A firm saved three times in fourteen years by one person, and the person is not on the list of persons. Then I looked for what I paid. An amah's wage; then a clerk's wage I set myself in 'thirty-nine because you were doing a clerk's work, and I remember being told in the clan hall that I was spoiling the servants." He turned the sheet over. On the back, alone: *1938. Bonus, godown clerk's girl: $2.*

Mei Foong looked at the figure. Fourteen years old in the front hall with the whole household assembled, the coins going warm in her fist and the words *the amah's girl* travelling round the room like a plate of kueh. She had carried that two dollars a long way. It was strange to meet it again in an old man's handwriting, entered correctly.

"You were not being ungenerous," she said. "You paid the going rate for what you thought I was."

"Do not manage me." There was no heat in it. "What I thought you were is the whole of the offence. I have run this firm forty years on one principle: everything has its price, and if you know the price you have understood the thing. It bought two lorries and a house of seven doors and a motor car I cannot drive." He stopped. A bicycle bell went along the five-foot way below. "And when the war came I could not buy the one thing, not for eleven taels or eleven thousand, and I sat by that wireless three years finding out my principle was a small principle. And all that time my wife's sister's daughter was scrubbing my floors and saving my firm, and I priced her at two dollars, and I would have gone into the ground not knowing the price was wrong."

He laid both hands flat on the desk.

"My wife could not say the word. It is not in the family. I have listened to myself for nine days and I cannot say it either; I have tried it aloud in this room and it comes out sounding like a man asking for a discount. So I will not insult you with it. I will do the other thing. I am going to pay you. It is what I have."

---

They walked down the street together on the last Tuesday of June, in the flat hour after four when the shutters are still down and the town is a corridor of shade. At the gate he had put out his hand in a small correcting gesture, as if straightening a picture, and she had come up level with him; and they went the length of four houses that way, the Towkay of the house of seven doors and the amah beside him, not behind, and she felt the street's eyes on that side of her like sun.

Two doors past the kopitiam he stopped and took the key off his own ring.

It was a key she had carried for six years. It had lived on the firm's board and the firm's board answered to her; she had let herself in with a ledger under her arm in the paper-burning week of 'forty-five, and in a curfew in 'forty-four with an oilcloth parcel, and in the dry month of 'forty-three with a lacquered box under the greens in a market basket. She watched him fit it to the lock of his own property and thought, with the part of her that never stopped keeping accounts: he does not know this house has been mine for five years. He knows only that it is about to be.

Inside was the flat dead air of a shut house — dust, old cloth-size, the ghost of a tenant's incense. The front room ran fifteen feet across and the long teak counter ran down the side of it, glass-topped, too heavy to have been worth carting away by a man leaving ahead of his debts. The rooms went back and back to the air well, where a fine dust of light came down, and then the kitchen, and the yard, and the well. He walked it once through, quickly, the way he walked everything, taking the signature on faith.

"The roof wants two rafters. The firm's carpenter will do it."

"I will pay the carpenter."

"You will not; it is my roof until the deed is registered." He came back to the middle hall and stood in front of the tenants' abandoned altar table with its bare recess, and the two small arched lamp niches on either side with the soot of old wicks in their throats. He rapped the wall between them with two knuckles, out of a landlord's habit, and it gave back the flat sound of a wall.

"Damp gets in on that side. It always did in these houses. Have it seen to."

"Yes, Towkay," said Mei Foong.

Behind the plaster and the good English brick, at the height of her heart, lay a lacquered box the size of two spread hands: a square of silk, a child's beaded slipper that had never touched a floor, and under the silk a district registration from the eleventh month of 1942 with her own name in the mother's column and the father's column empty. She stood in the room and let him tell her about damp. It cost her nothing. She had been standing one step back and one to the left in rooms like this since she was twelve, and the whole art of it was to let a man finish his sentence.

He unbarred one leaf of the shutters and pushed it open, and the street came in, hot and loud, charcoal and a trishaw bell and two women arguing about fish.

"Now the terms. Attend, because I have thought about this the way I think about a contract that will be attacked. The house is the firm's, and the firm is not only mine — there are my nephews, there is the estate after me, and any one of them looking at a conveyance to a servant will see the beginning of a claim. So. First I buy this property out of the firm at a valuation, a real one, from a real valuer, and pay it with my own cheque, and the sale is minuted with two signatures and entered in the firm's book like any other sale. Then it is mine, bought and paid for, and what is mine I may do as I like with. Then the deed."

"In whose name?"

"Ho Mei Foong, spinster, of Heeren Street." He said it without a flicker — the legal word for a woman who had never married in the sight of any office in the world — and she took it as she took everything of that kind, on the chin and into the book. "Not a gift. Gifts are challenged; a gift is what a family calls a thing when it wishes to say the old man had gone soft. A conveyance for consideration, and the consideration recited on the face of it: in recognition of long service to the grantor and to the firm, particularised — the flood, the inventory, the claims. Three lines. Devan will draw it. And I will swear a declaration before him, in my own words, that I am of sound mind and acting freely, and it will be sealed up with the deed, and a copy will lie in his strongroom when you and I are both bones. Let them attack it. They will be attacking a document, and the document will be correct."

Mei Foong stood in the shuttered shop of a cloth merchant who had gone upcountry ahead of his debts, and understood that she was being given a house.

She had planned nothing to say. What arrived, from somewhere below the ledger, was: "Why not money?"

"Because money is spent," said the Towkay, "and then it has never been. And because a house cannot be dismissed."

---

Devan's rooms were above a chandler's near the bridge, up a stair with a brass plate at the bottom: *Advocate & Solicitor*. He was a spare, deliberate man with his reading glasses pushed up into his hair and a son of about fourteen who brought the tea and stayed to watch; he drew the deed in a fortnight and read every clause of it aloud to Mei Foong at his table, twice, at his own insistence, before he would let anybody touch a pen.

"You are not the party who signs," he said. "But you are the party who will be alive in forty years, and I have found that the one who will be alive in forty years should be the one who has heard it read."

The registration took a morning, and the entry, when she was shown it, was four lines long. She had expected the paper to feel like something; she examined the expectation afterwards and did not entirely respect it. It felt like a receipt. That was correct, and she was a woman who valued correctness, and still she stood on the steps in the sun with the stiff board folder under her arm and had to wait a moment before she could remember which way the street ran.

It was on the walk back that he made the other offer, and he made it badly, which was how she knew it was real.

"There is the New Year," he said at the corner where he would turn up towards the mansion and she would go on. "The clan hall. Three hundred people, my nephews, the committee, the whole street. I can stand you up in that hall and say whose daughter you are. My wife's sister's child. It would be said once and be true for ever after, and nobody in Malacca would open his mouth about it in my lifetime, and after my lifetime it would be on paper, because I would have it minuted." A pause. "You would eat at the table. Not near the door."

Heeren Street went on with its eleven o'clock. A goldsmith's boy passed with a tray under a cloth; in a doorway a woman combed out her daughter's hair with the roughness that means love.

Mei Foong looked at the offer for longer than she let him see. The table, not near the door. To be a Neo of the Neos, acknowledged, set among the cousins at last, her mother's erasure hauled up out of the ground and reversed in one sentence in a hall full of people who had spat on the memory of a girl who ran off with a Eurasian clerk in 1921. To be in daylight, in her own name, what she had been in the dark since she was twelve.

And in the same instant the ledger did what the ledger always did, and turned the page and showed her the other column.

A servant is furniture, and nobody audits furniture. But let the amah of the Lim household stand up at the New Year and be revealed as the Bibik's blood, and the street would go back over her lovingly, item by item, the way this town went over anything it had not been told at the time. Where was she in the war? At the country house, in the counting year — with whose child? A dead northern clerk's, they said. And when was the child born? The eleventh month of 'forty-two. And what was she doing in the February of that year, in a house whose young master was taken off a padang and did not come back? And how does the doctor's daughter come to have the amah's exact hands, and the two fast strokes of the young master's brow? Nobody had asked those questions in six years because there had been no reason to look at the amah at all. Give the town a scandal it liked and it would count backwards for entertainment, and some clerk, some aunt, some cousin with a grievance and a free afternoon would arrive at a date in February and a date in November, and pull, and the thread would come away in his hand.

She had bought her daughter's clean name once, with everything she had, in a lamplit house among the rubber. She was not going to spend the payment twice for the pleasure of sitting nearer the middle of a table.

"No," said Mei Foong.

He turned his head. "Say your reasons. I will hear them."

"Three." She gave them as Ah Yoke had given measures: level, no seconds. "One. My mother's name is a prayer, not a lever. She wrote it herself without the surname, because they took the first stone from her while she lived and she would not steal it back for a prayer. I will not make her a story told at a dinner to prove that her sister's husband is a just man. Two. Your nephews. You have spent a month making a deed they cannot break — stand me up in that hall and you hand them the one thing they lack, which is a motive: the servant who turned out to be family and got a house in the same year. Then the deed is a scandal instead of a receipt, and it goes to court, and every paper in this family is read aloud by strangers." She stopped one beat, because the third reason was the only one that mattered, and she was about to hand him a copy of it with the true page removed, exactly as she had once handed a man back his own ledger. "Three. There is a child in your house being raised as a doctor's daughter, and she is five. This town does arithmetic for entertainment. Whatever you say in that hall about me will be counted backwards over her, because I am her amah and I hold her hand in the street. I have watched that counting done to other people. I will not have it aimed anywhere near her."

The Towkay stood in the sun with his hat in his hand and did the sum she had given him.

She watched him arrive — she saw the moment; it went over his face like the shadow of a bird — at the conclusion his own knowledge permitted: that the kitchen widow's fatherless child, given to the Lims in the counting year, was Guat Kim's granddaughter; that his wife's blood was in the small girl who had been spoiling herself on his knee for four years; that heaven had repaid the house of seven doors more literally than anyone in it had understood. He put out a hand and rested it on the wall of the corner house, an old man steadying himself in bright light.

"Then she is my wife's blood," he said, "and my wife went into the ground not knowing it."

"Yes," said Mei Foong, and let it stand there, whole and wrong, and added not one syllable in either direction.

He was one door from the other room. She could feel the draught of it. All he had to do was ask the smallest question a man can ask — *and this northern clerk; what was his name, what year did you marry, who stood witness* — and she had the answers dry and in order, as she had kept them for six years; and she watched him not ask. He did not ask because his hand was on a wall in the sun and his son had been dead six years and asking had once cost him everything that asking costs; and because the answer would not alter one line of what he had already decided to do; and because he was a man who closed an entry when it balanced.

"Do not spit into the wind," he said at last, which was his family's proverb for exactly this, and settled his hat, and went up his own street to his own house; and he never learned it, ever, in the eleven years remaining to him.

---

She moved on the fourth day of July, and it took one trip: a wooden trunk of clothes and work, the bead trays and cards of thread and paper packets of seed glass that weighed more than the clothes, the ledgers of her correspondence course, a mattress — and a black lacquered box wrapped in a cloth, carried in her own two hands on top of everything, which she set on the shelf of the left-hand niche in the middle hall, square to the edge, four feet from where its twin lay behind the brick. The lorry boy would have carried the box. She said it was only sewing.

Bee Nio came to the amahs' room while she was tying the trunk and stood in the doorway with her arms folded, and for a moment neither of them said anything about what was happening, because the terms had been settled in an hour on the day the Towkay announced his intention, and it had been made clear by everybody that nothing was changing.

"Five in the morning," said Bee Nio at last. "You will be here at five, and stay till she is asleep, and then you will walk four doors down and sleep in your own house. That is what was agreed. I would like to say aloud that I agreed to it and did not have to be persuaded."

"It is what I asked for."

"I know what you asked for. I was in the room." Bee Nio looked at the trunk. "In 'forty-three I swore something to you beside a cradle, and one part of it was that no economy and no quarrel and no peacetime would ever send you away. This is not me sending you away."

"No," Mei Foong said. "This is me having somewhere to go. There is a difference, and I have waited fourteen years to be on this side of it."

Mei Lan was five and a half and took the whole business as a personal development. She came along the five-foot way at the end of the afternoon holding Bee Nio's hand, let go of it at the door, went straight past everybody down the length of the empty shop to the air well, and stopped there looking up at the square of sky as though something ought to be arranged in it, and announced that the house was too long.

"It is fifteen feet across and eighty feet deep," said Mei Foong. "They taxed the front, so we grew backwards."

"Who is we?"

"Everyone on this street."

The child considered the sky further. "Will you sleep here?"

"Every night."

"Who will sleep with you?"

"The house," said Mei Foong.

"That is nobody."

"That is a great deal," said Mei Foong, and picked her up — five and a half and getting to be a weight, all elbows, smelling of the Lims' soap — and carried her back down the long dark rooms to her mother at the door.

At six the lorry returned with one more thing on it, unlabelled: the merbok cage from the mansion's air well, the round brass-hooped one that had hung there since a boy of fourteen was given a bird for the New Year. The cage was empty. The bird had died in the hungry year and nobody had replaced it, and nobody in that house had ever quite been able to take the cage down either. The lorry boy set it on the five-foot way, said it had been in the way of the sweeping, and was gone before she could send it back. She hung it in the air well where the light came down, and did not shut its little door.

At half past six she walked the two doors to the kopitiam for a candle and a tin of milk, the electricity not being connected until Thursday, and the old man behind the marble counter — whose son, a serious young fellow of twenty-three with a bicycle and a great deal to say about the new curfew, was stacking crates at the back — took her money and gave her three cents too many.

"Uncle. This is wrong."

"New house," he said. "Never mind, never mind."

She looked at the coins in her palm, then folded them into her handkerchief and said, "I will owe you." The old man laughed at her. She went home and entered it in the back of her marketing book — *kopitiam, three cents, to be repaid* — and it was still entered there, and still uncancelled, when she died in the room above that shop at the age of eighty-two.

---

She lit the candle in the middle hall at eight o'clock, and the house came forward out of the dark a little at a time.

Nobody had eaten in it for six years. There was no rice bin, no oil, no god in the recess, no chair. There was the tenants' altar table, and the counter, and her trunk, and the trays, and the mattress rolled against the wall; and behind and above and around all of it eighty feet of rooms she did not have to leave at five in the morning, and a stair, and a roof wanting two rafters, and a well, and a door with a bolt on the inside of it.

She sat down on the trunk with the candle beside her and took out the biscuit tin.

It was English, printed with roses and a hunting scene already going soft at the corners with handling, and it had crossed a sea in a majie's basket before it was ever hers. She lifted the lid and set the things out one at a time on the trunk, the way she checked stock: the bundles of letters under their red thread; the fountain pen with the fine nib, given to her over a column of her own figures in a flooded godown by a boy who thought fairness was a thing you could simply do; the beaded slipper, left foot, peony and phoenix, worn to the shape of a foot that had walked in it one morning in February 1942 and never since. The jade ring was not in the tin. The jade ring was at her neck on its cord, inside the collar, where hands are seen and collars are not.

Then she untied the tape of the folder and unfolded the deed and read it through once more by candlelight, all the way to the recital: *in recognition of long and faithful service to the grantor and to the firm, and in particular of the preservation of the accounts in the flood of 1938, of the inventory of 1941 to 1945, and of the records upon which the claims of 1947 were founded.*

Fourteen years, three lines, one house. No mother in it anywhere. No husband. No daughter. Every true thing about her folded back out of the frame, and the paper correct, and registered, and hers.

She folded it along its creases and laid it in the bottom of the tin, under the letters, over the folded photograph; and the tin, which had been a post office of the dead for six years, took it without comment, the way it took everything.

Then she unscrewed the pen.

*Dear husband. I have an address now. You may send word.*

*It is the cloth merchant's house, two doors up from the kopitiam — the one you said had good bones and a bad landlord. The landlord has improved. Your father gave it to me in recognition of service, which is the only sentence he has; he said it correctly, and I have decided to be paid in it, because a house cannot be dismissed. He knows whose daughter I am. He offered to stand me up in the clan hall at the New Year and say it in front of three hundred people, and I refused, and he does not know why, and he will not, ever. I have been offered my name twice in my life. The first time I gave it up to keep our daughter fed, and it was taken out of my hands. This time I handed it back myself, across a deed, in the sun, from a house of my own. Tell me the difference is not everything, and I will not believe you.*

*The roof wants two rafters. There is a bird cage in the air well with nothing in it, and I have left the door open. Our daughter says the house is too long. — Your wife, who owns eighty feet of it.*

She folded the sheet small, put it into the tin among the others, and pressed the lid home.

Then she went the whole length of the house with the candle, room by room, all the way to the back, because it was hers and there was nobody in it to think her strange for looking; and at the front again she stood a moment with her hand on the bar of the door, and heard the last trishaw go by, and a wireless two doors up talking in Malay, and the curfew hour beginning out in the districts where men with rifles were stopping lorries in the dark.

She drew the bolt. It was the first door she had ever locked from the inside.
