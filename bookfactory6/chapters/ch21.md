# Amah

The doctor's house stood four doors up Heeren Street from the house of seven doors, and in it the child slept in the east room and the amah slept on a mat in the passage outside its door, and the distance between them was eleven feet. Mei Foong measured it on the first night, lying along it in the dark, because she had spent her life turning things into figures and there was no reason to stop now.

Eleven feet, one closed door, and the rest of her life.

The office had its uniform: white blouse, black trousers, hair in one plait. It had its wage — nine dollars a month in the new money, which by the year's end bought two eggs if the egg woman was generous; she took it and entered it, because a servant who refuses wages is a story, and stories walk. And it had its name. In that house she was Foong to the doctor, Ah Foong to the kitchen, and to the child, when the child had words, she would be Amah, which is not a name but a description of a pair of hands.

The days built themselves fast, the way days do when they are made of other people's needs. Before light, the charcoal and the water and the child's cloths. Six o'clock, the child, who woke like her father, all at once and full of opinions. At eight she was carried down to her grandmother's, which was four doors, which was two minutes, which was the distance from one country to another. Afternoons belonged to the firm's account room. Evenings, the child again, then the pots, then whatever hour was left, which was hers, and which she spent under one lamp with a needle.

She had thought, in the long night at Ayer Keroh, that the hard part would be the handing over. It was not. The handing over was two minutes, and she could hold her face for two minutes; she had held it nine years under sharper eyes. The hard part was the arithmetic that ran underneath everything without permission: that the child had been eleven hours out of her sight that week and forty minutes in her arms; that she was accumulating a debt owed her by nobody.

She did not keep that column. She kept the child's — feeds, weights, teeth, the small red book of a baby's year — and wrote it up nightly and left it on the shelf in the east room where a mother would find it; and Bee Nio read it every night and never once thanked her for it, which was the arrangement.

---

The arrangement had been made in the first week, without a word of it spoken above the level of a lamp.

Bee Nio came into the east room on the third night, closed the door, put the child straight into Mei Foong's arms and sat down on the floor with her back against the cot, in her house clothes, like a girl at a picnic.

"This is the hour. Every night, this hour. In the day I will not give her to you unless there is a reason a stranger could see, and you will not ask for her, and neither of us will look at the other while she is handed about like a dish. That is the day. This is not the day."

Mei Foong stood in the middle of the floor with her daughter asleep on her shoulder and could not immediately speak.

"And there is a second thing." Bee Nio looked up. "I will tell you her. All of it, every day, whatever I have that you have not — with nothing left out to spare you and nothing put in to comfort you. I am not a good woman, Foong. I was vain, and careless, and unkind to you for years without noticing. But I can keep an account. I watched my father do it my whole life."

She kept it. Tan Bee Nio, who had never reconciled so much as a laundry list, rendered a daily statement for two and a half years and did not miss a night. Some nights she fell asleep in the middle of it against the cot, and Mei Foong put a folded sarong under her head and sat on with the child.

In daylight it held, because they built a machinery for it: no order given in front of the kitchen that had not first been asked for out of sight, no eyes meeting across a room where a third person stood. And there was the tray.

The aunts came, and the Taiping cousins, and neighbours who came to see a war baby the way people come to see rain in a dry month, and they asked the mother the questions that are asked of mothers: whether she was weaned, whether she slept the night, what was given for the wind, did she take the yolk mashed or beaten. And Bee Nio, who knew the answers to none of it, would say, "She is very particular," in a mother's voice, and turn her head half an inch toward the tray coming round; and the amah would set down the cups and lay two fingers flat on the tray's edge for yes, one for no; and the answer arrived a moment later in Bee Nio's own words, correct in every particular. It was the longest conversation of either of their lives.

---

The street outside the two houses frayed that year the way rope frays: not all at once, and always in the same places. The offices had begun writing 2603 on their papers, and the town went on counting in goats and monkeys and waiting. Rice came down by a measure and then by another.

In the eighth month the notices went up at the market, and the recruiters came to the doors with a clerk and a list: work in the north, on the railway, good wages, rice at the site, three months. The tinsmith three doors up had two sons and nothing to pay with. The second boy was seventeen; one morning he went up to the padang with a bundle and a hat, and the lane stood in its doorways and did not wave, because the years had made waving frightening.

Three letters came in the first months, in a boy's careful school hand, each saying that the work was hard and the food adequate and that his father was not to worry. Then nothing came. The tinsmith set the boy's bowl at the table every meal for two years, and the lane let him have it.

And on Tuesdays, in the account room, there was Nagata.

He had a supervisor's brush and a fine touch for the exact weight a household would surrender without complaining afterwards. He never took. He admired. He admired the pre-war brandy, and it went to lunch with him; he admired the Towkay's second lorry, and the lorry was lent on no stated date; and in the eleventh month he admired the Bibik's slippers, the black pair with the peonies, and mentioned a sister-in-law in Osaka who had been shown such things once, in a shop in Singapore.

So a pattern was drawn on paper in the account room, and the Bibik came down to the kitchen court that evening with her second-best pair in her hand and unpicked the vamps herself with a bodkin, bead by bead, into a bowl — because no beads were to be had in Malaya that year, and there were, in that house, four pairs.

"Peonies for the peonies," she said. "Not the phoenix. He gets no phoenix." She set the bowl in Mei Foong's hands. "Do it well. It goes out of this house with our name on it, and if it is done badly he will come back and admire something else."

It took eleven nights. It was the best work she had done since the wedding slippers of 1940, and it left the house in a box lined with the Bibik's own tissue paper, and no money changed hands, and nobody thanked anyone. It was the only piece of her life she did not afterwards carry in her memory the way you carry a tune, because she had put nothing into it.

---

The first word came in the twelfth month, in a doorway, and went where everyone had agreed it would go.

The child was thirteen months and had been furnishing the household with sounds for weeks, long declarative sounds on subjects nobody could identify. Then on a wet afternoon Bee Nio came in from the five-foot way shaking out her umbrella, and the child, on the floor of the middle hall with a wooden spoon, sat up straight and said, "Mah."

She said it twice, and the second time she put her arms up.

The hall went off like a firecracker. Third Aunt came out of the court with flour to her elbows; the Bibik was fetched and had it repeated twice; and the news went up and down the street before the rain stopped, because that year a house needed things to say that were not about rice. The Bibik ruled on it: the word was a word, addressed and not accidental, and no child of this family had ever spoken later than fourteen months.

Bee Nio, kneeling on the tiles with the child's fists in her hair, looked up once. Half a second, across the hall to the doorway where the amah stood holding the wet umbrella. Mei Foong laid one finger flat on the handle, twice, for yes — take it properly, or it is worth nothing — and Bee Nio turned back and picked up her daughter and answered to the name.

Afterwards Mei Foong carried the umbrella out to the back court and stood it open to dry, and stayed a while in the smell of wet brick, entering the thing whole and carrying nothing forward. It was a good word, and it had gone to the right woman, and every part of that had been arranged on purpose by people who planned it, herself among them.

She thought she had priced it correctly. She found out, two years later, in the same hall on an afternoon when the word came out whole and formal at last, that the first one had not inoculated her at all.

---

The fever began on a Thursday night in the first week of January and did not break until Sunday.

It came out of nothing, the way they do in the tropics: the child hot at bath time, refusing food at seven, by ten burning in the way that frightens the hand that touches it. Dr. Lim was at the hospital that night and the next, and there was no going out after dark for anyone who valued their papers. So there was the east room, one lamp behind a screen, and two women.

Mei Foong stripped her to a cloth and sponged her — wrists, neck, the small feet — with water taken off the boil and cooled, because Ah Yoke's rule was that cold water on a burning child makes the fever dig in like a cat. She got fluid in by spoon at ten minutes and then at twenty, entering each spoon in the red book. She counted breaths against the doctor's clock four times an hour and wrote the numbers down. She sang, quietly, the two songs she had, and did not notice until the second night, when Bee Nio, eyes shut against the cot, asked what the first one was, and Mei Foong said only that her mother had sung it.

At the low hour of the third night the child's breathing changed and her eyes went up and her body stiffened and jerked, and for perhaps thirty seconds no power on earth could stop it. Mei Foong turned her on her side and cleared her mouth with a finger and held nothing but the head, and said, in a voice that came out level: "Don't hold her arms. Let it finish. It finishes." Bee Nio, on her knees, obeyed her. It finished. The child lay grey and floppy, and then wailed, which was the best sound in the world, and Bee Nio put her forehead down on the floorboards.

By dawn on Sunday the fever had broken, and the two of them sat either side of the cot in a room smelling of vinegar and lamp oil, too tired to hold their heads up.

Then the household came.

The Bibik first, at seven, with a charm from the temple on Java Lane, giving thanks aloud to Kuan Yin and to Bee Nio, telling the room, and the street after it, that a mother's own hands were worth nine doctors. The neighbours came in the afternoon, and each said to Bee Nio, in the identical words the whole town keeps for it, that she must be exhausted, poor thing, that a mother goes through it twice, once in the body and once in the mind.

Bee Nio, grey-faced, upright, holding a cup she had not drunk from, opened her mouth on the fourth of these and got as far as, "It was—"

Across the hall her amah, coming in with the water jug, looked at her. Nothing in the face; no gesture anyone in that hall could have named or repeated. Bee Nio closed her mouth. Then she said, "It was three nights," which was true, and drank her tea, and the visit went on.

Dr. Lim came home at noon, having been thirty hours in the wards, and read the red book at the window without sitting down: the spoons, the count of the breaths, the fit at the low hour of the third night with its duration in seconds, all in a small upright hand with the numbers ruled straight.

He looked at it a long time. Then he closed it and said, to the book rather than to anyone, "Well nursed."

That was the whole of it, and there was nothing else that could be said in that house on that morning by that man; and Mei Foong took it standing in the doorway with the empty basin against her hip, and found it enough to be going on with.

---

Nine days afterwards he opened the dispensary door and said, "Grind that."

He gave no speech. There was a mortar with quinine bark in it and a pair of scales; he named the weight he wanted and went back to his own work, and when she had done it he looked at the powder and said "Finer" and went away again. It went on for two years in that manner, with nothing ever said about what it was: how to fold a powder into a paper so it will not spill in a pocket; the difference between a child who is thirsty and a child who is drying out, which is in the eyes and the skin of the belly, and which he made her recite back in order, months apart, without warning, as a schoolmaster does.

He taught her to give an injection on an orange and then on his own forearm, and — it took an afternoon, and cost him something, she saw it — the exact words with which you tell a family standing in a doorway that there is nothing in the house that will help, and the necessity of saying it once and not decorating it.

She understood at the time that he was short-handed and that the war had eaten his nurses. She understood, considerably later, that a decent man had spent two years building a trade into a woman he could not otherwise repay, so that whatever the peace turned out to be, she would walk into it holding something in her own hands.

---

Ah Yoke's hands went that year. It was not sudden, and nobody discussed it.

She was past seventy, the pneumonia had left its flag in her lungs, and the hands that had counted rice for a whole street had begun to refuse fine work the way an old servant refuses — without a word, by doing it slowly until the task is taken away. That season Mei Foong found her at the window with a slipper vamp, threading and rethreading; she took the piece next day and finished it and said nothing, and after that the fine work came to her without being handed over.

There was work, which surprised her at first and then did not. People married in the occupation, quietly, because a wedding is a thing you can still do when there is nothing to eat and no news. There were no new beads in Malaya; the glass had come from Europe, and Europe had other business. So the craft turned to salvage: dead women's slippers unpicked into bowls. She mended and remade, and beaded three pairs of bridal kasut manek in eighteen months for brides she never met, working the peony and leaving out the phoenix, because a phoenix is a promise and she would not put one into work she was paid for in eggs.

The price was eggs, oil, a bag of charcoal, once a live chicken carried up Heeren Street by a woman too proud to be thanked. She kept the accounts in the back of the exercise book, worst figure first; and by the end of the war the beadwork had fed the doctor's house perhaps one meal in nine.

She worked at night, after the pots, on the mat by the wall, the frame propped on her knees, one lamp turned low for the oil's sake. Four beads at a time on a needle finer than the argument for using it. The house asleep; the child asleep eleven feet away behind a door; and four doors up the street, in the amahs' room, an old woman with a click in her breathing, keeping its own books. She had, some nights, the sensation of being at the bottom of a very deep well, the only place she was permitted to be entirely herself, and of quite liking the well.

One night that season she took out the tin and unscrewed the fountain pen and sat with the paper squared in front of her, and did not write.

*Dear husband. Your daughter had a fever of three nights and I counted her breaths and she is well. Another woman was thanked for it, correctly, in front of the whole street, and I found I could stand in the doorway and hold the basin. Your mother will keep the New Year even so; she has decreed it. I am beading again, for eggs. None of this can be written down.*

She thought it in order, in her own head. Then she capped the pen and put the tin back under the boards and went to the frame; and the tin took no letter that year, nor the next, nor the one after, and the silence in it was not an absence but an entry.

---

The Year of the Monkey came in on the twenty-fifth of January, and the Bibik declared that this house would keep it.

There was no coconut worth the name, the sugar was grey, and there was no flour but what tapioca could be tortured into; Third Aunt said what she thought of all three, at volume, and made the kueh anyway. The lapis went into the steamer layer by layer, nine of them, poured and set and poured again, thinner than any lapis that court had produced in living memory and — the Bibik tasting, twice, and saying nothing, which was the highest grade she awarded — correct.

"Anyone can be rich," she said to the room in general, laying the ninth layer. "Few can be careful."

On the second morning of the making she sent for the child.

The kitchen court had a practice about this, older than anyone in it and never written anywhere: every child of the house puts its hand to the mould once before it can walk properly, so that the hands should remember what the head will not. The Bibik sat on the low stool with her sleeves pinned and had Mei Lan set on the table before her, fourteen months old and outraged at being held still; and she closed the small fist round the ang ku mould and pressed it down into the red dough — hand over hand, the old over the small — until a tortoise-back came up out of the wood with a fringe of fingerprints along one edge that no cook would have allowed on any other tray.

"There," said the Bibik. "Now she is ours."

Mei Foong stood at the stove four feet away with the steamer lid in her hand and kept the lid where it was.

She was the only person on the earth who could see the whole line of it: that the hand doing the pressing had once closed the same mould over the fist of a younger sister; that the sister had been struck off a family register and sent to Kwangtung, and had made this kueh there once a year out of the wrong sugar and a menu written out by an amah and posted north, because a struck-off woman will build the whole festival out of a list of dishes if a list of dishes is all she is sent; and that Mei Foong had eaten it at five and six and seven years old and thought it was simply what people ate; and that here was the mould, and here was the same hand, and here was the next fist inside it, pressed into the dough by a grandmother who did not know she was a great-aunt, in front of a mother who was not permitted to be one.

There was no ledger anywhere with a column for it. She put the lid back on the steamer, because the steam was going, and the steam was the one thing in that room she was still responsible for.

---

The photographer came on the fourth day of the New Year, in the afternoon, with a plate camera and a boy to carry it.

It was the Bibik's order, and she gave no reason for it except once, to nobody: "Because who knows." The town had had three New Years now in which the words *next year* had quietly gone out of use.

They took it on the front steps, because the light in the hall was no good and the front steps were what a family had.

The arranging took forty minutes and was done entirely by the Bibik, in Baba Malay, with her stick. It was not taste; it was a diagram. The Towkay in the centre in the carved chair, thinner than his suit; the Bibik beside him in her best kebaya with the three gold brooches that would go visiting before the year was out. Dr. Lim at the Towkay's right, because a doctor son-in-law seats high; Bee Nio at her mother's left; the Taiping cousins ranked behind by generation and, within generation, by age. The senior servants at the ends of the back row, Ah Yoke on the left in her black-and-white, standing like a plumb line. The kitchen girls and the syce below, on the steps. And in front, dead centre, on a low rattan chair set on a cushion: the child.

The child would not sit in it.

She was fourteen months old, it was three in the afternoon, and she had been dressed in a red jacket by four separate people. She got out of the chair twice and was fetched back from the drain; the third time she stood up in it and roared; and the photographer, who had a plate he could not afford to waste, said over the roaring that the small ones need to see whoever ordinarily has them, and where was the amah.

"At the back," said the Bibik. "Foong. Come where she can see you."

So it was settled in ten seconds, by a photographer's convenience: the amah brought up from the far end of the row and stood at the edge behind the little chair, one hand on the chairback where the child could reach up and take hold of two of her fingers, which she did at once, and stopped roaring, and sat.

Nobody looked at anybody. Ah Yoke, at the other end of the row, did not turn her head. Bee Nio arranged her hands in her lap and looked straight ahead at the camera as her mother had told her to. Dr. Lim polished nothing.

Mei Foong stood where she had been put and looked out over the family at the black box on its tripod, and saw exactly what she was standing in. Rank, front to back, near to far: the house at the centre in its chair, the blood of the house seated, the servants at the ends and the hired at the feet. Any stranger who ever picked the photograph up would read it correctly at a glance.

They would also be looking, and would never know it, at a family portrait correctly arranged in the only order ever available to it: the great-aunt in the middle chair with her brooches; the woman who had been given the child; the child; and the mother, standing at the edge, one step back and one to the left, holding the chair her daughter sat in.

"Still," said the photographer. "Still, still — good."

The shutter fell, and the boy drew the plate.

Then the Bibik, who had gone to the trouble of a photographer in a year with no trouble left over, said they would have a second, because plates spoil and the man had come all this way. So the household stood on in the four o'clock light while the plate was changed; and Mei Lan, secure now, tipped her head back to look up at whoever was holding the chair.

Mei Foong let her eyes go down, to the top of her daughter's head, and left them there.

The shutter fell the second time.
