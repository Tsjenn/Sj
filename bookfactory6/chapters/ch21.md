# Amah

The doctor's house stood four doors up Heeren Street from the house of seven doors, and in it the child slept in the east room and the amah slept on a mat in the passage outside its door, and the distance between them was eleven feet. Mei Foong measured it on the first night, lying along it in the dark with her arms above her head the way you measure cloth when the tape is lost, because she had spent her life turning things into figures and there was no reason to stop now.

Eleven feet, one closed door, and the rest of her life.

The office had its uniform, and she put it on: white blouse, black trousers, hair in one plait, nothing at the throat. It had its wage, and she took it — nine dollars a month in the new money, paid on the first, and by the end of that year the month's wage bought two eggs if the egg woman was in a generous mood. She took it anyway, and entered it, because a servant who refuses wages is a story, and stories walk. It had its name. In that house she was Foong to the doctor, Ah Foong to the kitchen, and to the child, when the child had words, she would be Amah, which is not a name but a description of a pair of hands.

The days built themselves fast, the way days do when they are made of other people's needs. Before light, the charcoal and the water and the child's cloths. Six o'clock, the child, who woke like her father, all at once and full of opinions. At eight she was carried down to her grandmother's, which was four doors, which was two minutes, which was the distance from one country to another. Afternoons belonged to the firm, three days a week in the account room with the shrinking columns, because the Towkay had never replaced her and had stopped pretending he meant to. Evenings, the child again: the bath, the rice-water, the walking of a small furious person up and down a passage until sleep took her mid-grievance. Then the pots, then the mending, then whatever hour was left, which was hers, and which she spent under one lamp with a needle.

She had thought, in the long night at Ayer Keroh, that the hard part would be the handing over. It was not. The handing over was two minutes, and she could hold her face for two minutes; she had held it nine years under sharper eyes. The hard part was the arithmetic that ran underneath everything without permission and could no more be switched off than her hearing: that the child had been eleven hours out of her sight that week and forty minutes in her arms; that the word said in the front hall at noon had gone into the child's ear and not hers; that she was accumulating, at a rate she could have entered in a column had she allowed herself one, a debt owed her by nobody, payable never.

She did not keep that column. She kept the household's and the firm's and the child's — feeds, weights, teeth, the small red book of a baby's year — and wrote it up nightly in her small upright hand, and left it on the shelf in the east room where a mother would find it; and Bee Nio read it every night and never once thanked her for it, which was the arrangement.

---

The arrangement had been made in the first week, without a word of it spoken above the level of a lamp.

Bee Nio came into the east room on the third night, closed the door, put the child straight into Mei Foong's arms and sat down on the floor with her back against the cot, in her house clothes, like a girl at a picnic.

"This is the hour. Every night, this hour. In the day I will not give her to you unless there is a reason a stranger could see, and you will not ask for her, and neither of us will look at the other while she is handed about like a dish. That is the day. This is not the day."

Mei Foong stood in the middle of the floor with her daughter asleep on her shoulder and could not immediately speak.

"And there is a second thing, which I have thought about more than anything in my life." Bee Nio looked up. "I will tell you her. All of it, every day, whatever I have that you have not. If she does something in the front hall you did not see, you will have it that night, in order, with nothing left out to spare you and nothing put in to comfort you. I am not a good woman, Foong. I was vain, and I was careless, and I was unkind to you for years without noticing, which is worse. But I can keep an account. I watched my father do it my whole life."

She kept it. That was the thing Mei Foong had not expected and never afterwards held lightly: Tan Bee Nio, who had never reconciled so much as a laundry list, rendered a daily statement for two and a half years and did not miss a night — the two new teeth, the shouting at the ancestral portraits, the exact shape of the fright the neighbour's dog gave her, the fish eaten and then reconsidered. Some nights it took four minutes. Some nights she fell asleep in the middle of it with her back against the cot, and Mei Foong put a folded sarong under her head and sat on with the child.

In daylight it held, and it held because they built a machinery for it. There was a rule that Bee Nio never gave an order in front of the kitchen unless she had first, out of sight, asked for it. There was a rule about eyes, which was that they did not meet across a room where a third person stood. And there was the tray.

The aunts came, and the Taiping cousins, and neighbours who came to see a war baby the way people come to see rain in a dry month, and they sat in the front hall and asked the mother the questions that are asked of mothers. Whether she was weaned. Whether she slept the night, and what was given for the wind, and did she take the yolk mashed or beaten. And Bee Nio, who knew the answers to none of it, would say, "She is very particular," in a mother's voice, and turn her head half an inch toward the tray coming round; and the amah would set down the cups and lay two fingers flat on the tray's edge for yes, or one for no, and take it away; and the answer arrived a moment later in Bee Nio's own words, correct in every particular. Nobody ever saw it. It went on for years. It was the longest conversation of either of their lives, conducted entirely in fingers on a lacquer tray, across rooms full of people, about a child who belonged to both of them under two laws that could never be reconciled and were never once, in private, pretended to be.

---

The street outside the two houses frayed that year the way rope frays: not all at once, and always in the same places.

The offices had begun writing 2603 on their papers, and the town went on counting in goats and monkeys and waiting. Rice came down by a measure and then by another. Cloth was mended until the mending was itself a kind of cloth. The kitchen court learned tapioca in nine forms and gave four of them names that could be said in front of the Bibik.

In the eighth month the notices went up at the market and on the shutter of the closed cinema, and the recruiters came to the shophouse doors with a clerk and a list: work in the north, on the railway, good wages, rice at the site, three months. The tinsmith three doors up had two sons. He was a widower; the second boy was seventeen, thin as a lath, famous in the lane for mending anything with a wire; and one morning he went up to the padang with a bundle and a hat, and the lane stood in its doorways and did not wave, because the years had made waving frightening.

Three letters came in the first months, in a boy's careful school hand, each saying that the work was hard and the food adequate and that his father was not to worry. Then nothing came, and nothing went on coming. The tinsmith set the boy's bowl at the table every meal for a year, and then another year, and the lane understood it to be an argument he was having with heaven and let him have it.

And on Tuesdays, in the account room, there was Nagata.

He had a supervisor's brush and a supervisor's boredom and a fine touch for the exact weight a household would surrender without complaining afterwards, which was the whole of his art. He never took. He admired. He admired the pre-war brandy in the front hall, and it went to lunch with him; he admired the Towkay's second lorry, and the lorry was lent, on no stated date; and in the eleventh month, walking through the front hall, he admired the Bibik's slippers, which that day were the black pair with the peonies, and mentioned that his wife's sister in Osaka had once been shown such things in a shop in Singapore and had spoken of them since.

So a pattern was drawn on paper in the account room, and the Bibik came down to the kitchen court that evening with her second-best pair in her hand and a face like a woman signing something, and unpicked the vamps herself with a bodkin, bead by bead, into a bowl — because there were no beads to be had in Malaya that year for love or money, and there were, in that house, four pairs.

"Peonies for the peonies," she said. "Not the phoenix. He gets no phoenix." She set the bowl in Mei Foong's hands. "Do it well. It goes out of this house with our name on it, and if it is done badly he will come back and admire something else."

It took eleven nights. It was the best work she had done since the wedding slippers of 1940, and it left the house in a box lined with the Bibik's own tissue paper, and no money changed hands, and nobody thanked anyone. Of all the beadwork of her life it was the only piece she did not afterwards carry in her memory the way you carry a tune. She had put nothing into it. That was the trick she learned that year, and she was not proud of it: that hands can be made to work while the woman stands off at a distance, and that tribute is best paid by somebody who is not there.

---

The first word came in the twelfth month, in a doorway, and went where everyone had agreed it would go.

The child was thirteen months and had been furnishing the household with sounds for weeks — long, declarative, deeply serious sounds delivered from the hip of whoever held her, on subjects nobody could identify. Then on a wet afternoon Bee Nio came in from the five-foot way shaking out her umbrella, and the child, on the floor of the middle hall with a wooden spoon, sat up straight and said, "Mah."

She said it twice, and the second time she put her arms up.

The hall went off like a firecracker. Third Aunt came out of the court with flour to her elbows; the Bibik was fetched and had it repeated for her three times, the child obliging twice and then refusing on principle; and the news went up and down the street before the rain stopped, because that year a house needed things to say that were not about rice. The Bibik ruled on it in the front hall with her chin up and her hands folded: that the word was a word, that it was addressed and not accidental, and that no child of this family had ever spoken later than fourteen months.

Bee Nio, kneeling on the tiles with the child's fists in her hair, looked up once. Half a second, no more, across the hall to the doorway where the amah stood holding the wet umbrella. Mei Foong laid one finger flat on the handle, twice, for yes — take it; take it properly, or it is worth nothing — and Bee Nio turned back and picked up her daughter and answered to the name.

Afterwards Mei Foong carried the umbrella out to the back court and stood it open to dry, and stayed a while in the smell of wet brick, doing what she had trained herself to do, which was to enter the thing whole and carry nothing forward. It was a good word, and it was the right word, and it had gone to the right woman, and every part of that had been arranged on purpose by people who had planned it, herself among them, in an argument with the dead.

She thought she had priced it correctly. She found out, two years later, in the same hall on an afternoon when the word came out whole and formal at last, that the first one had not inoculated her at all.

---

The fever began on a Thursday night in the first week of January and did not break until Sunday.

It came out of nothing, the way they do in the tropics: the child hot at bath time, refusing food at seven, and by ten burning in the way that frightens the hand that touches it. Dr. Lim was at the hospital that night and the next, on a ward with a corridor full of the swelling sickness; there was no going out after dark for anyone who valued their papers. So there was the east room, one lamp behind a screen, a basin, a woman who had grown up where the amahs nursed everything, and a woman who had not.

They did it together for three nights and neither slept above two hours in the whole of it.

Mei Foong stripped her to a cloth and sponged her — wrists, neck, the soles of the small feet — with water taken off the boil and cooled, changed as it went warm, because Ah Yoke's rule was that cold water on a burning child makes the fever dig in like a cat. She got fluid in by spoon at ten minutes and then at twenty, counting the spoons aloud, entering them in the red book with her left hand while her right held the head; when the child refused the spoon she wetted a clean cloth and let her suck it and counted that as half. She counted breaths against the doctor's clock four times an hour, in her head, the way she counted a column, and wrote the numbers down. She sang, quietly, the two songs she had, and did not notice she was doing it until the second night, when Bee Nio, sitting against the cot with her eyes shut, asked what the first one was, and Mei Foong said she did not know, only that her mother had sung it.

At the low hour of the third night the child's breathing changed and her eyes went up and her body stiffened and jerked, and for perhaps thirty seconds no power on earth could stop it. Mei Foong turned her on her side and cleared her mouth with a finger and held nothing but the head, and said, in a voice that came out of her level and strange, "Don't hold her arms. Let it finish. It finishes." Bee Nio, on her knees, obeyed her. It finished. The child lay grey and floppy, and then wailed, which was the best sound in the world, and Bee Nio put her forehead down on the floorboards and stayed there.

By dawn on Sunday the fever had broken, and the two of them sat either side of the cot in a room smelling of vinegar and lamp oil, too tired to hold their heads up.

Then the household came.

The Bibik first, at seven, with a charm from the temple on Java Lane and two eggs she had got heaven knows where, giving thanks aloud to Kuan Yin for the third time that week and to Bee Nio for the second, telling the room, and the street after it, that a mother's own hands were worth nine doctors, that she had said so at the time, that she had known the fever would turn on the Saturday because of the moon. The cousins came at eleven. The neighbours came in the afternoon, and each said to Bee Nio, in the identical words the whole town keeps for it, that she must be exhausted, poor thing, that a mother goes through it twice, once in the body and once in the mind.

Bee Nio, grey-faced, upright, holding a cup she had not drunk from, opened her mouth on the fourth of these and got as far as, "It was—"

Across the hall her amah, coming in with the water jug, looked at her. Nothing in the face; no gesture anyone in that hall could have named or repeated. Bee Nio closed her mouth. Then she said, "It was three nights," which was true, and drank her tea, and the visit went on.

Dr. Lim came home at noon, having been thirty hours in the wards, and read the red book at the table by the window without sitting down: the spoons, the four-times-an-hour count of the breaths, the fit at the low hour of Saturday with its duration in seconds, all of it in a small upright hand with the numbers ruled straight.

He looked at it a long time. Then he closed it and said, to the book rather than to anyone, "Well nursed."

That was the whole of it, and there was nothing else that could be said in that house on that morning by that man; and Mei Foong took it standing in the doorway with the empty basin against her hip, and found it was enough to be going on with, which frightened her a little, later, when she had time.

---

Nine days afterwards he opened the dispensary door and said, "Grind that."

He gave no speech. There was a mortar with quinine bark in it and a pair of scales; he named the weight he wanted and went back to his own work, and when she had done it he looked at the powder and said "Finer" and went away again. That was the beginning. It went on for two years in that manner, with nothing ever said about what it was: how to fold a powder into a paper so it will not spill in a pocket, six papers to a strip; how to boil dressings in a kerosene tin and dry them without touching them; how to find a pulse in a fat baby's wrist and an old man's ankle; how the swelling sickness prints your thumb into the flesh, and how long the print takes to fill; the difference between a child who is thirsty and a child who is drying out, which is in the eyes and the skin of the belly and the number of times a cloth is wet in a day, and which he made her recite back to him in order, twice, months apart, without warning, as a schoolmaster does.

He taught her to give an injection on an orange and then on his own forearm. He taught her — it took an afternoon, and cost him something, she saw it — the exact words with which you tell a family standing in a doorway that there is nothing in the house that will help, and the necessity of saying it once, plainly, and not decorating it.

She understood at the time that he was short-handed and that the war had eaten his nurses. She understood, considerably later, that a decent man had spent two years quietly building a trade into a woman he could not otherwise repay, so that whatever the peace turned out to be, she would walk into it holding something in her own hands. He never said so. Thanked for it, he would have polished his spectacles and asked her the signs of drying out, in order, twice.

---

Ah Yoke's hands went that year. It was not sudden, and nobody discussed it.

She was past seventy, and the pneumonia had left its flag in her lungs, and the hands that had counted rice for a whole street and caught two generations of children had begun to refuse fine work the way an old servant refuses — without a word, by doing it slowly until the task is taken away. In the third month Mei Foong found her at the window with a slipper vamp, threading and rethreading, and stood behind her and said nothing; and took the piece next day and finished it and said nothing; and after that the fine work came to her without being handed over.

So the trade moved down the room, from the pallet to the mat, from an old woman to a younger one, the way trades have always moved.

There was work, which surprised her at first and then did not. People married in the occupation — more than before, and faster, and quietly, because a wedding is a thing you can still do when there is nothing to eat and no news. There were no new beads in Malaya; the glass had come from Europe, and Europe had other business. So the whole craft turned to salvage: dead women's slippers unpicked into bowls, one burst vamp sacrificed to save two others, colours matched across three generations of somebody's grandmother's taste. She mended, and remade, and cut old fronts down to new lasts, and beaded three pairs of bridal kasut manek in eighteen months for brides she never met, working the peony and leaving out the phoenix, because a phoenix is a promise and she would not put a promise into work she was paid for in eggs.

That was the price, mostly. Eggs, oil, a bag of charcoal, a cup of sugar gone grey, once a live chicken carried up Heeren Street by a woman too proud to be thanked. Nobody wrote any of it down but Mei Foong, who kept the accounts in the back of the exercise book, worst figure first; and by the end of the war the beadwork had fed the doctor's house perhaps one meal in nine, which in that year was an empire.

She worked at night, after the pots, on the mat by the wall, the frame propped on her knees, one lamp turned low for the oil's sake. Four beads at a time on a needle finer than the argument for using it. The house asleep; the child asleep eleven feet away behind a door; the old woman asleep across the room with the click in her breathing keeping its own books. She had, some nights, the sensation of being at the bottom of a very deep well, and of the well being the only place she was permitted to be entirely herself, and of quite liking the well.

One night that month she took out the tin and unscrewed the fountain pen and sat with the paper squared in front of her, and did not write.

*Dear husband. Your daughter had a fever of three nights and I counted her breaths and she is well. Another woman was thanked for it, correctly, in front of the whole street, and I found I could stand in the doorway and hold the basin. Your mother is teaching her hands the kueh. I am beading again, for eggs. There is nothing in any of this that can be written down.*

She thought it in order, in the small upright hand of her own head. Then she capped the pen and put the tin back under the boards and went to the frame; and the tin took no letter that year, nor the next, nor the one after, and the silence in it was not an absence but an entry, and she was the only auditor it would ever have.

---

The Year of the Monkey came in on the twenty-fifth of January, and the Bibik declared that this house would keep it.

There was no coconut worth the name, the sugar was grey, and there was no flour but what tapioca could be tortured into; Third Aunt said what she thought of all three, at volume, and made the kueh anyway. The lapis went into the steamer layer by layer, nine of them, poured and set and poured again, thinner than any lapis that court had produced in living memory and — the Bibik tasting, twice, and saying nothing, which was the highest grade she awarded — correct.

"Anyone can be rich," she said to the room in general, laying the ninth layer. "Few can be careful."

On the second morning of the making she sent for the child.

The kitchen court had a practice about this, older than anyone in it and never written anywhere: that every child of the house puts its hand to the mould once before it can walk properly, so that the hands should remember what the head will not. The Bibik sat on the low stool with her sleeves pinned and her rings left upstairs and had Mei Lan set on the table before her, fourteen months old and outraged at being held still; and she took the small fist in her own hand and closed it round the ang ku mould and pressed it down into the red dough — hand over hand, the old over the small, once, twice, three times, until a tortoise-back came up out of the wood with a rounded shell and a fringe of fingerprints along one edge that no cook alive would have allowed on any other tray.

"There," said the Bibik. "Now she is ours."

Mei Foong stood at the stove four feet away with the steamer lid in her hand and kept the lid where it was.

She was the only person in that court, or in that house, or — so far as she was ever afterwards able to establish — on the earth, who could see the whole line of it: that the hand doing the pressing had once pressed the same mould over the fist of a younger sister; that the sister had grown up and been struck off a family register and gone to Kwangtung, and there, twenty years later, in a cold kitchen in a bad province, had made this kueh once a year out of the wrong sugar and the wrong rice and a menu written out by an amah and sent north with the mail, because a struck-off woman will build the whole festival out of a list of dishes if a list of dishes is all she is sent; and that Mei Foong had eaten it at five and six and seven years old and thought it was simply what people ate; and that here was the mould, and here was the hand, and here was the fourth of them being pressed into the dough by a grandmother who did not know she was a great-aunt, in front of a mother who was not permitted to be one, in a kitchen full of women, in the middle of a war.

There was no ledger anywhere with a column for it. She put the lid back on the steamer, because the steam was going, and the steam was the one thing in that room she was still responsible for.

---

The photographer came on the fourth day of the New Year, in the afternoon, with a plate camera and a boy to carry it.

It was the Bibik's order, and she gave no reason for it except once, in the front hall, to nobody: "Because who knows." Nothing else was said. The town had had three New Years now in which the words *next year* had quietly gone out of use, and half a dozen households up and down Heeren Street had the same thought in the same fortnight, so that the photographer was busier that week than at any time since 1941. He was paid in an ang pow everyone understood to be ceremonial, and a bag of rice, which was not.

They took it on the front steps, because the light in the hall was no good and the front steps were what a family had.

The arranging took forty minutes and was done entirely by the Bibik, in Baba Malay, with her stick, and it was not a matter of taste. It was a diagram. The Towkay in the centre in the carved chair, thinner than his suit; the Bibik beside him in her best kebaya with the three gold brooches, which had not yet gone visiting and would go before the year was out. Dr. Lim at the Towkay's right, because a doctor son-in-law seats high; Bee Nio at her mother's left. The Taiping cousins ranked behind by generation and, within generation, by age, two of them moved twice because the Bibik reconsidered a marriage. The senior servants at the ends of the back row — Ah Yoke on the left in her black-and-white, hair oiled, hands in front of her, seventy-two years old and standing like a plumb line. The kitchen girls and the syce below, sitting on the steps. And in the front, dead centre, on a low rattan chair carried out from the middle hall and set on a cushion so that she should be seen: the child.

The child would not sit in it.

She was fourteen months old, it was three in the afternoon, and she had been dressed in a red jacket by four separate people. She got out of the chair twice and had to be fetched back from the drain; the third time she stood up in it and roared; and the photographer, who had a family of his own and a plate he could not afford to waste, said over the roaring that in his experience the small ones needed to see whoever ordinarily has them, and where was the amah.

"At the back," said the Bibik. "Foong. Come where she can see you."

So it was settled in ten seconds, by a photographer's convenience: the amah brought up from the far end of the row and stood at the edge behind the little chair, one hand resting on the chairback where the child could reach up and take hold of two of her fingers, which she did at once, and stopped roaring, and sat.

Nobody looked at anybody. Ah Yoke, at the other end of the row, did not turn her head. Bee Nio arranged her hands in her lap and looked straight ahead at the camera as her mother had told her to. Dr. Lim polished nothing.

Mei Foong stood where she had been put and looked out over the family at the black box on its tripod and the man's arm coming out from under the cloth, and saw, with the part of her mind that had never once shut up, exactly what she was standing in. Rank, front to back, near to far: the house at the centre in its chair, the blood of the house seated, the servants at the ends and the hired at the feet. Every stranger who ever picked this photograph up, for as long as the paper lasted, would read it correctly at a glance and know at once what everybody in it was.

They would also be looking, and would never know it, at a portrait of four generations arranged in the only order ever available to them: the great-aunt in the middle chair with her brooches; the woman who had been given the child; the child; and the mother, standing at the edge, one step back and one to the left, holding the chair her daughter sat in.

"Still," said the photographer. "Still, still — good."

The shutter fell, and the boy drew the plate.

Then the Bibik, who had gone to the trouble of a photographer in a year with no trouble left over, said they would have a second one, because plates spoil and the man had come all this way. So the household stood on in the four o'clock light, holding the pose, while the plate was changed. Under the cloth the photographer counted them again. Somewhere behind the house a hawker's bell went by, and Mei Lan, secure now, tipped her head back to look up at whoever was holding the chair.

Mei Foong let her eyes go down, to the top of her daughter's head, and left them there.

The shutter fell the second time.
