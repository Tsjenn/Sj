# The Tin

The rain began at Seremban and did not let up, so that Su-Lin drove the last hour to Malacca inside a drum. It was the serious afternoon rain of the west coast, the kind that arrives like an appointment, and the wipers of her sensible Japanese car kept a beat she was too tired to find annoying. On the seat beside her, in a plastic folder from the lawyer's office, was a death certificate for a woman she had met perhaps four times in her life, and the deed to a house she had never seen.

Ho Mei Foong. Even the name felt like something found at the back of a drawer. In the family it had always been just *Foong* — Great-Aunt Foong, though nobody could say whose aunt she actually was, said in the tone reserved for obligations older than anyone present. She had appeared at a handful of funerals in Su-Lin's childhood, a small straight-backed woman at the edge of the group photographs, always in grey, always leaving before the food. Someone from the old house in Malacca, was the explanation, when there was an explanation at all. *She was staff, sayang. From your great-great-grandfather's time. An amah's daughter.* And now the amah's daughter had died at the age of eighty-two and left her shophouse on Heeren Street — the deed said Jalan Tun Tan Cheng Lock now, but the lawyer, who was also old, had said Heeren Street — to Tan Su-Lin of Kuala Lumpur, whom she had met perhaps four times.

"There must be a mistake," Su-Lin's mother had said on the phone, three times, in the voice she used when a table was badly set. "She was nobody. Why would she have a house? Why would she leave it to *you*?"

Su-Lin had no answer then and none now, at the traffic light where Jonker Street's gaudy trishaws sheltered under a bridge like wet birds. She was thirty-four years old. Eight months ago she had been an architect with a firm, a fiancé, and a five-year plan, and now she had a firm. People kept telling her the other two things were merely postponed, the way you would tell someone a cancelled flight was merely postponed, and she had stopped correcting them because the correction sounded worse out loud. When the lawyer's letter came, she had said yes to the drive mostly because it was a task with edges. Collect keys. Inspect property. Advise family. She was good at tasks with edges. It was the borderless ones — what do you want, what will you do now, who are you when the plan goes — that she had begun, quietly, to fail.

The lawyer's office was above a shop that sold funeral paper goods, which struck her as either tactless or efficient. Mr. Devan was seventy at least, with a cardigan despite the heat and a ceiling fan doing slow, doubtful work. He had the file ready. He had, he said, been Miss Ho's lawyer for forty years, and his father before that, which took some doing for a woman her family called nobody.

"The estate is simple," he said. "The house, its contents, and a small sum that will cover the transfer and the outstanding assessments. She was precise. I have never administered a cleaner estate." He slid the keys across the desk on a ring with a worn leather tab. "She left one instruction beyond the will itself. That the house be given to you with everything inside it, nothing removed, nothing tidied. She used the English word. *Untidied.* She said you would want to see it as it was."

"Why me?" Su-Lin asked. It came out blunter than she meant, the KL bluntness that Malacca made audible. "I didn't know her. I'm told she worked for my great-great-grandparents before the war. That's all anyone knows about her."

Mr. Devan looked at her for a moment over his glasses, and she had the sensation, rare and not comfortable, of being read by someone with a longer file on her than she had on herself.

"She knew you," he said. "That was my impression. But I only held the papers." He stood, which took a while, and shook her hand. "The wiring is original, so mind the fuses. And Miss Tan — the street remembers her, even if the family doesn't. You may find people want to talk to you. Let them."

The house stood in the middle of the old street's long terrace, one narrow face in a row of narrow faces, its shutters the faded blue-green of something repainted many times and then left alone for a long time. THE former shop sign had been taken down at some point; she could see its ghost, a pale rectangle above the door, like the mark a picture leaves on a wall. Fifteen feet wide, she estimated, out of habit. They were all fifteen feet wide, the old shophouses — taxed on frontage, so they grew backwards instead, room after room after room, the way secrets do.

The key turned with the small resistance of a lock kept oiled by an old person who oiled things on a schedule. Then she was inside, in the smell.

Camphor, first. Then dust, sandalwood ash, old paper, starch, and under it all something floral and faintly medicinal she couldn't name, the smell of a category of shop that no longer existed. The front room was dim, the electricity not yet on, rain-light coming grey through the airwell further back and laying a wet silver rectangle on the floor. She stood still and let her eyes adjust, and the house came forward out of the dark a little at a time, the way a photograph develops.

It had been a shop, that was plain. A long teak counter ran down one side, glass-topped, and in the glass cases beneath were trays and trays of beads. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands — glass seed beads in vials and tobacco tins and folded paper packets, sorted by colour with a rigour that made her architect's heart sit up: rows of reds running rust to rose, golds, greens, a blue section deep as the evening outside. On the wall behind the counter hung wooden lasts in the shapes of feet, dozens of pairs, small to smaller, and framed above them, alone, a single embroidered slipper-front under glass — a peony worked in beads so fine the flower seemed to have a pulse in the failing light.

Kasut manek. Her grandmother had a phrase for the beaded slippers, produced whenever period dramas came on television: *nobody has the hands for it anymore.* Said with a strange flat finality, and the channel usually changed soon after.

So the amah's daughter had been a beader. A good one, if the shop had lasted; a known one, if Mr. Devan's street remembered. Su-Lin walked the length of the counter trailing one finger in the dust, doing the sum her family had apparently never bothered to do: staff do not own shophouses on Heeren Street. Craftswomen who work sixty years and spend nothing might. The house wasn't a mistake. It was a life. Somebody should have known that.

The rooms went back and back. A middle hall with an altar table against the wall, its brass polished, its ash fresh enough that the incense must have burned within the year — someone had tended this after the death, or before it, meticulously. Photographs on the side wall, and she made herself walk past them slowly: unsmiling groups in the stiff arrangements of old studios, women in kebaya, men in high collars. She recognized no one. She stopped anyway at one, a household assembled on front steps she half-knew — the mansion further up the street, she realized, the one with the plaque now, the ancestral house her family had sold in the seventies and spoke of the way you speak of a war you won. Rows of faces by rank. At the very edge of the back row, behind the seated children, stood a young woman in an amah's white blouse, looking not at the camera but very slightly down, at the head of the small girl seated in front of her.

The airwell held a garden gone leggy in pots, and a birdcage, empty, its little door rusted open. Rain came down the shaft in a fine communal mist. Beyond it the kitchen: one cup, one bowl, one pair of chopsticks in the rack. A calendar from 2004, stopped in November.

She found the camphor chest in the room above the shop, under the window to the street. It was black with age, carved with plum blossoms, and locked, and it was the only locked thing in an unlocked house, which she would think about later. The smallest key on Mr. Devan's ring fitted it.

Blankets, on top, folded with hospital corners. A grey cardigan. A layer of tissue paper gone brittle as insect wings. And beneath the tissue, set square in the centre of the chest like the stone in a ring: a biscuit tin.

It was English, old, the lid printed with roses and a hunting scene rubbed to ghosts by handling. The sort of tin every household of a certain age kept full of sewing things, and she expected thread and thimbles as she worked the lid off with her thumbnails.

Letters. The tin was full of letters, dozens deep, each folded small in its envelope, the bundles tied — she lifted one out into the rain-light — with red thread. Silk, she thought. The knots were beautiful and deliberate. The paper had gone the colour of weak tea, and the writing on the envelopes was Chinese, brushed in a small, upright, disciplined hand, and every envelope she turned over was addressed the same way, two characters and nothing more. No stamp. No postmark. Letters that had never been sent, or never needed sending.

Beneath the letters, wrapped in a square of faded indigo cloth: a slipper. One beaded slipper, left foot, a woman's, the peony-and-phoenix pattern worked rust and gold and jade on a black ground, the beads so small her eye kept refusing them. It was heavier than it looked, the way dense work always is. She turned it in her hands. The beadwork was flawless except that it had been worn — truly worn, moulded to a foot, the sole shaped by walking. One slipper, kept for sixty years. You did not have to be an architect of anything to know that the other one existed somewhere, and that the story was in the gap between them.

There was a small drawstring pouch that held a ring of green jade, warm-toned, a woman's size, threaded on a cord as if it had been worn at the neck and not the hand. And there was, at the very bottom of the tin, face down, a photograph.

She knew it was a wedding photograph before she turned it over, from the size and the studio weight of the card. What she did not expect was the fold.

A young man stood beside a table with a vase of lilies, in the high-collared suit of the thirties, one hand resting on the table's edge in the posed way of studio portraits, the other at his side — no. Not at his side. His hand was holding another hand, at the very edge of the image, and there the photograph had been folded back on itself, a sharp old crease pressed flat by decades in the tin, so that the person whose hand he held was bent away behind the picture, out of sight. The bride — the drape of a sleeve said bride, said kebaya, said the first inch of a story and no more — had been folded out of her own wedding.

The man was perhaps twenty-one. He had a lean, definite face, light-eyed for a Chinese, with brows like the strokes of a fast writer, and he was almost, almost smiling — the smile of someone instructed to be solemn by a photographer he found funny. Su-Lin sat back on her heels on the floor of a dead woman's bedroom and understood that she was going to spend more than a weekend in Malacca.

She did not unfold the photograph. She was surprised by her own certainty about this: the fold was sixty years old and it was not hers. Not yet. Instead she turned the card over. On the back, in the same small upright hand as all those unsent envelopes, someone had written a single line in Chinese. Her characters were shamefully rusty — her grandmother had tried, Saturday mornings, workbooks, bribery, defeat — but these she could read, because the first two were a name she had written on red packets her whole life.

*For Mei Lan, when she asks.*

Mei Lan was her grandmother.

The rain had stopped without her noticing, the way it does there, all at once, leaving the street outside loud with dripping and the first brave motorbikes. Su-Lin sat with the tin in her lap in the greying light and looked at the name for a long time.

Her grandmother had been born in Malacca; that much was family record. Born in 1942, the war baby, the miracle baby — *your great-great-grandmother lit joss sticks for a year*, the story went, a family that had lost its son to the war being given a granddaughter to go on with. She had left for KL as a girl and, to Su-Lin's knowledge, had been back twice, for funerals, under protest. She did not speak about Malacca. She was a brisk, contained, formidably competent woman who ran a household the way Su-Lin ran a project site, and the one time Su-Lin had asked her — school project, family tree, Standard Five — what the old house had been like, her grandmother had looked at her for a moment with an expression the eleven-year-old had no name for, and said: "Cold floors. Finish your homework."

*For Mei Lan, when she asks.*

She had never asked. Sixty years of never asking, and a tin that had waited anyway, in a locked chest, in an untidied house, left with lawyer's precision to the one member of the family too far from the story to know it was dangerous.

Su-Lin took out her phone while the reasons not to were still assembling. Her grandmother answered on the fourth ring, television news in the background, eight in the evening, the hour of her programmes.

"Su-Lin. Is something wrong?"

"No, Ah Ma. I'm in Malacca. The house — Great-Aunt Foong's house. I collected the keys today."

A sound of the television being muted. Then her grandmother's voice, level as a shelf. "Your mother said you were doing that today. Good. Devan and Sons, still? Get the valuation in writing." A pause the exact width of politeness. "It's late to drive back. Where are you staying?"

"Ah Ma, the house is full of her things. She was a beader — kasut manek, a whole shop of it, you should see the work. And there are photographs, and — she kept letters. A whole tin of letters." Su-Lin heard her own voice going quick and made it slow. "There's a beaded slipper in the tin. Just one. And a photograph with your name on the back. It says—"

"I have never owned a beaded slipper."

The sentence arrived flat and complete, a door meeting its frame. It was also, Su-Lin registered — the way she would register, on a site, a beam an inch from where the drawings put it — an answer to a question she had not asked.

"Ah Ma—"

"It's late, Su-Lin. Lock that house properly, the old locks are nothing. We'll talk when you're back." And the line went, not angrily; her grandmother had never needed anger; the call simply ended, the way her calls ended when the subject was finished. Except that the subject had not been finished. The subject had not even been started.

Su-Lin sat in the last of the light with the phone warm in her hand.

Below her the house ticked and dripped and settled, long and narrow and full, every room of it going back, back, away from the street's face into the dark, the way secrets do; and it occurred to her — the thought arriving with the calm of true late-night thoughts, though it was barely evening — that of all the family, the dead woman had chosen the one who measured houses for a living. *She said you would want to see it as it was.* As it was. Untidied. Nothing removed.

She looked down at the tin. The topmost bundle of letters sat under its red thread, squared to the corners by a hand that had squared things all its life.

"All right," Su-Lin said, out loud, to the empty house, to the folded bride, to the small upright handwriting of a woman her family had misfiled for seventy years. "I'm asking."

She untied the first knot.
