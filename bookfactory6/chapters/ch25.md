# The Niche

Hafiz's van drew up at twenty past eight, and Su-Lin was standing in the shop doorway waiting for it, plaster dust to her elbows, the night still on her like weather. She had not slept and had not tried to. At five she had made tea in the one-cup kitchen and let it go cold beside her on the hall floor, and at seven she had heard the street begin — shutters, the kopitiam's first charcoal smoke, a motorbike with opinions — and had gone on sitting where she was, because whatever else she had broken in the night, she was not going to add to it by letting him find out from the hall.

He read her hands first, then her face, and walked past her without a word.

The ancestral hall was as she had left it at four in the morning. The altar shrouded in its dust sheet like a witness with its eyes covered. The floor sheeted and taped. The wall to the left of the altar open at last, its little arched dark eye looking back into the room after sixty years, the soot ghost of old lamps still on the head of the arch. And on the dust sheet below, in rows squared to the skirting, the bricks — each one numbered in carpenter's pencil, course by course, eleven of them, in the order they had come out.

Hafiz stood in front of the wall for a long time. Then he crouched by the rows of brick, and she watched him take in the numbering, the grid pencilled on the surviving plaster, the little digital camera sitting on its folded cloth with its counter reading sixty-three.

"You numbered them," he said.

"Course order. They can go back exactly where—"

"You numbered them." He stayed crouched, not looking at her. "That's the part I can't even be angry at properly."

He was, though. It came out level and slow, which was worse than loud. He had written his name across this house, he said. The works programme was his. The conservator was booked. He had told the trust, in a letter with his signature at the bottom of it, that the owner understood what waiting was for. She did not defend herself. She offered the camera, the sixty-three photographs, the timestamps, the taped perimeter, and he looked at it all and said, "Recording yourself breaking an agreement doesn't unbreak it. It files it," and she took that too, because it was true.

Then he stood, and rubbed the back of his neck, and said, "Show me what couldn't wait three weeks."

She brought the lacquered box to the counter in the shop, where the light was, and lifted the fitted lid. The child's slipper lay on its faded silk, the peony no bigger than a coin, the phoenix tail making its turn in six beads. Beside it, flat and level, the ruled paper with its browned purple chop.

"It's a birth record," she said. "November 1942. The mother's name is Ho Mei Foong." Her voice was doing something strange and she let it. "The child's name is Mei Lan. My grandmother's name is Mei Lan. She was born in November 1942. The column for the father is empty."

Hafiz looked at the paper for a long time without touching it. Then he looked at the slipper, longer. Then he put the lid back on the box with two hands, gently, the way you close a door in a house where someone is sleeping.

"Three weeks," he said, and it was not an accusation now; he was weighing it, the way he weighed timber. "I'd like to tell you I would have waited. I document buildings for a living partly so I never have to find out what I'd do." He picked up his torch. "It still cost, Su-Lin. The agreement was with me, not the trust."

"I know. I'm not asking you to say it's all right."

"Good," he said. "Then we can skip that part and get to work."

He sent the crew up to the roof and spent the morning doing, after the fact, everything that should have been done before it: the niche measured and drawn, the soot gradient photographed, the mortar sampled, her sixty-three photographs printed at the shop by the bridge and numbered into the record in sequence. The report went to the trust under both their names, and her section was in her own words, at his insistence: *the wall was opened by the owner, alone, without approval, in the following steps.* The trust's reply took ten days and expressed displeasure in official grammar, accepted the retrospective record, and added a paragraph about future works that they both read standing up, like a school report.

He called her Miss Tan for eight days. Not punishment, she decided, watching him hand her a torch with the courtesy of a stranger; the first name had been part of the agreement, and the agreement was being rebuilt from the footings, and you do not load a structure while the footings cure. On the ninth day, up a ladder, without turning round, he said, "Su-Lin. Pass the level," and she passed it, and neither of them remarked on it, and the wall between them was back in bond.

The paper conservator came down on a Thursday, produced by Hafiz's conservation courses in Penang the way the street produced everything — sideways, personally vouched for. Dr. Sim was a small dry woman with a canvas roll of instruments, and the examination happened at the museum, on Puan Aini's table, on the archive's foam cradles, because Puan Aini said documents were like patients and you brought them to the theatre, not the theatre to them.

It was procedural, and Su-Lin, who had braced for drama, found the procedure better than drama. Raking light along the folds. A linen tester screwed into an eye socket like a jeweller's. The ultraviolet lamp in the curtained back room: "Paper made after the fifties mostly carries optical brighteners," Dr. Sim said. "Under UV it shouts at you. Old paper keeps quiet." The ruled sheet lay on the black cloth and kept quiet, a dull dead mauve, not one fibre of it shouting. The stock itself was thin and browned and short-fibred — "occupation paper; everyone was making do, you can practically feel the year in it" — brittle exactly where sixty years of folds said it should be brittle. The ink had been brushed on and had aged down into the fibre, not sitting on the surface the way fresh ink sits on old paper when somebody is being clever. The chop's seal-paste had browned at its edges in the same slow chemistry as the chops on documents no one had ever questioned. The ruling was letterpress, and you could read the impression of the type from the back, like braille.

Then Puan Aini put on fresh gloves and brought out the archive's own occupation-era registrations, three of them, brown as tea, and laid them alongside. Same printed form series. Same ruling. Same registry office chop, in the same corner, at the same slight habitual tilt — some clerk's wrist, sixty years dead, matching itself across the table. Even the numerals were cousins.

"Understand what I can and cannot say," Dr. Sim said, packing her roll. "I cannot certify that a document tells the truth. I can tell you this one is consistent in every material respect with late 1942, and that nothing about it is new, and nothing about it is imitating anything. It is not pretending to be a wartime registry record. It is one."

"Paper can be old and still lie," Puan Aini said. She squared the document on its cradle with one gloved finger, her small liturgical gesture. "But look at what this one would have to be. A lie manufactured in 1942, inside the registry office, with the registry's own chop — and then never used. Hidden in a wall for sixty years at some cost, by someone who wanted it to exist and never wanted it seen. Miss Tan, lies are made to be *used*. This was made to be true somewhere, even if only inside a wall."

That night Su-Lin cleared the shop counter, put the lamp on, and did what she did when a thing would not resolve: she drew it.

Two drawings, side by side on graph paper, like a record drawing and an as-built. On the left, the family as she had been taught it, the tree from the Standard Five homework: the Towkay and his wife at the top; their son Boon Siew, *died in the war, no issue*; their daughter Bee Nio, married to Dr. Lim; Bee Nio's daughter Mei Lan, the war baby, the miracle; Mei Lan's daughter; and at the bottom, in her own blunt pencil, herself. On the right, what the tin and the wall said: Ho Mei Foong; below her, connected by one line, Mei Lan, born November 1942, father's column empty on the paper and full in the letters — *dear husband; our daughter* — and below Mei Lan the same two boxes as before, her mother, herself, unchanged, because that part of the tree had never been the lie.

She sat looking at the right-hand drawing and caught herself in an error she had been making all week. In her head, all week, she had been calling Mei Foong her great-great-grandmother — promoting the dead a generation, out of some instinct that a war must stand further away from you than it does. The paper refused to flatter her. Her mother's mother's mother. Three steps. She counted them twice with the pencil point, box to box to box, the way she checked a dimension chain, and the count closed both times.

Great-grandmother. The letters on the counter were her great-grandmother's letters. The hand she had been learning to read for three months, stroke by stroke, was her great-grandmother's hand.

And if the letters were true where the register was blank — *dear husband*, fifty envelopes with one name on them — then the man in the photograph, the almost-smile, the high collar, the schoolboy who had argued rice yields two doors down and bought coffee for the clerks, was not the family's lost great-granduncle at the edge of the story. He was her great-grandfather. She looked at the left-hand drawing, at Bee Nio's box, at the woman whose stern studio face had presided over the KL house's hallway her whole childhood under the label *great-grandma*, and moved her, in pencil, one column sideways: the sister of her great-grandfather. An aunt, standing all these years where a mother had been drawn.

Nothing fell off the tree. That was the thing she had not expected, the thing that made her put the pencil down and sit very still. The blood did not thin; it rerouted. The Tan name still reached her — it simply came the back way, through the nailed door, down the servants' stair, through a girl in a borrowed kebaya and a boy who never came back from the padang. And the family story, the one told at every reunion — *the miracle baby, heaven's repayment, your great-great-grandmother lit joss sticks for a year* — stood up suddenly meaning something no teller of it had ever meant. The house that lost its son had been handed its son's daughter, unlabelled, and had given thanks to heaven without ever knowing how much it had to be thankful for.

Which meant — she made herself say it in full, silently, the way Teacher Yap made her write out corrections in full — the folded photograph in the tin was a wedding portrait of her own great-grandparents.

She went upstairs and looked at it a long time that night, at the crease, at the two beaded feet surviving in their half-inch wedge at the bottom edge. She did not open it. The fold was addressed, and not to her. Not yet.

She told her mother the careful half on the Thursday, by telephone, with the sentences prepared in advance like documents for a submission: that Great-Aunt Foong had been privately married to Tan Boon Siew during the war; that there was a wedding photograph, and letters; that there was a birth record of a child born in 1942, mother's name Ho Mei Foong. She did not say the child's name. Down the line, in KL, she heard her mother pull out a chair and sit.

The lunch happened on Sunday, at her mother's table, under the wedding photographs of three living generations, and it was ugly in the specific quiet way her family did ugliness, which was catered. Uncle Beng was there, because of course Uncle Beng was there.

They began with *impossible* — there was no wedding, everyone knew there was no wedding, the boy was betrothed to an Ipoh family — and Su-Lin noted, with something between vertigo and admiration, that her family's denial and the street's memory were word for word the same, sixty years apart, and only one of them had ever been revised. Then, inside ten minutes, the table moved on from whether it was true to what it would cost, and stayed there for two hours.

"Who else knows?" her mother said. "Does the developer know? These things get out, valuations move—"

"A marriage means a widow," Uncle Beng said, doing sums on the tablecloth with a dry finger. "A child means descendants. Descendants means claims — the Towkay's estate was distributed on the basis that the son died without issue, you understand what that means if some grand-nephew of hers walks in with a lawyer—"

"And the *optics*," her mother said. "If this gets out. A servant. People will say the family—" and stopped, and rearranged the fish with the serving spoon, and did not finish, because there was no way to finish that sentence that survived being heard.

"Where are these papers now?" Uncle Beng asked, not looking at Su-Lin. "In that house? Alone? Girl, papers get lost. Houses don't."

Nobody disagreed with him. That was the moment Su-Lin stopped eating and simply watched, from a small professional distance, the way she watched a structure take load. News had arrived at this table — a marriage kept secret for sixty years, a child, a woman who had written to a dead man for half a century two doors from where his family burned joss for him — and the table had weighed it. Converted it, on arrival, into exposure, claims, window, price. She had spent three months thinking of the money-first reflex as her family's vice, some KL corrosion. Sitting under the ancestors' photographs, she understood it was an inheritance. The one heirloom that had come down the official line undamaged, from the Towkay who had rewarded a girl for saving his firm with two dollars and a compliment about training: when the unaccountable arrives, price it. The estate had passed down its accounting long after it passed down anything else.

"And Su-Lin." Her mother caught her at the door, voice dropped to the register of heart medication. "Not a word of this to your Ah Ma. You hear me? At her age. Promise me."

Su-Lin looked at her mother for a moment, and said nothing, and her mother took the silence for the promise it was not, because taking silence for agreement was also, it turned out, a family craft.

In the car, on the way out of the city, the Nokia rang on the passenger seat: the developer's office, the third time that week, the voice mail afterwards full of the words *window* and *improved* and *final*. She drove and let it ring itself out. By Wednesday the missed-call log showed the same number nine times, stacked like bricks, and she read the list once, with interest, the way you read a barometer, and answered none of them, then or ever.

"Uncle. You told me to come to you before the family, when I found out who was standing in the folded part. I went to the family first. I'm sorry. You get the whole of it; they got half."

Uncle Raju turned his cup a half-turn on its saucer. "Families are landlords," he said. "They collect first. Sit. Pay an old man his arrears."

She told him. The kopitiam was empty in the dead mid-morning, his son wiping the far counter, and she told him the street's own corner of it: that the bride folded out of the photograph was Sister Foong; that there had been a wedding after all, in February of 1942, before an altar, with no guests and no announcements and no kueh.

"No kueh," Uncle Raju repeated, at last, as though that were the detail that made it true — a wedding invisible to the street because it had never once touched the machinery by which the street saw. He was quiet for longer than she had ever known him quiet. "Sixty years I sat two doors from her," he said. "I remember everything, girl. I have made a profession of it. And she sat there being the biggest thing this street didn't know." He shook his head slowly, and there was no slyness in his face at all, only something old and level. "You know what the street knew instead? I will tell you what we knew.

"From 'forty-nine until her hands stopped — call it forty years — every bride worth the name in this town walked her first steps in that woman's beadwork. They came from Muar. From Seremban. Once from Penang, and Penang thinks it invented everything. They came as girls and they came back as mothers, bringing daughters, and she measured the daughters' feet kneeling on that shop floor the same as she had measured the mothers'. Apprentices in and out of that doorway for decades — half the beading hands in the state came out of her window. The lamp over her counter burned late every night of my life; the street set its watch by it." He set the cup down, precisely, on its own ring. "Your family called her staff. The street called her sifu. You know this word? Master. Not master of servants — master of the hands. It is not a word we spend, girl. We spent it on her."

He looked at her then, sharp again, the barometer-look. "There is more. It is walking up and down behind your eyes."

"There's more."

"Street business?"

"Family business."

"Then take it to the family it belongs to." He picked up his pen and pulled the crossword towards him, dismissal and blessing in one economical motion. "And when it is done — come. I will trade you the parts I have been too polite to guess in daylight. My price is coffee. You know how I take it: made by somebody else."

The roof was finished the week the rains came back, and when the scaffold came down out of the airwell, the birdcage came down with it, off its old hook, for the first time in — Hafiz turned it in his hands and read it like everything else — decades. A bamboo dome gone grey, wire fine as the beading needles in the counter, two little porcelain cups still in their rings, the small door standing open on the rust of its hinge, the way it had stood the first evening she ever entered the house.

They cleaned it on the shop counter between them, the last small job of the works, not in the programme and not billed. Hafiz mended the wire with roofing offcuts and talked about his grandfather, who had kept merbok — the little zebra doves, the whole street of his boyhood raising cages up bamboo poles on Sunday mornings, old men squinting up into the glare arguing about which bird's cooing had the deeper well in it. "There's a bird man in Merlimau still," he said, oiling the hinge until the little door swung without complaint. "Merbok with a voice on him like a flute at the bottom of a well, my cousin says. Housewarming present. I'm only the postman."

"No bird," Su-Lin said.

He looked at her.

"The cage stays open."

She took a length of soft copper wire from his bag and did it herself, there on the counter: wired the little door back against the bars, open, neatly, in a figure-eight lashing that would outlast the hinge — the way you prop a door for someone expected, no more explanation than that, because she had none; it had arrived in her with the firmness of a load path, from somewhere below argument. Hafiz watched her do it and asked nothing, which she had come to understand was his way of agreeing, and then he went up the ladder and hung the cage back on its hook in the airwell, where it turned slowly in the grey light, door open, ready.

---

The register she had made the first weekend told the shape of the war better than any history she had read since. One letter from 1941, alone, before everything. Three from 1942. One from the first weeks of 1943 — the birth letter, the only letter in the tin with joy in it, rendering a daughter to her father like a statement of account — and then nothing. Two years of nothing, the worst years holding their breath in the gap between two envelopes. And then September 1945, and from there the letters marched on, one a year, sometimes two, down the whole second half of the century.

The September 1945 letter took her four nights, alone at the counter under the lamp, at the pace it had cost. On the fifth night Teacher Yap read her notebook, and his red ballpoint came down and drew one small exact circle.

"Wrong," he said. "You have written *my silence was the loudest*. She did not write *silence*. Silence is weather — it happens to you. Look at the characters. She built a noun out of a verb with her own hands: the saying-of-nothing. A thing she did. A thing made, girl, like a slipper. Carry-able." He recapped the pen. "Write it as she built it and the sentence will stand up."

Rewritten, the sentence stood up, and the whole letter stood up behind it:

*Dear husband. The flags have changed again. I am still your amah. Our daughter called another woman Mother today, and I said nothing, and the saying-nothing is the loudest thing I have ever done. Your name has gone up on a tablet in the front hall of your father's house, and I clean it, and nobody wonders why the amah cleans it so well — cleaning is what I am for. The rice has come back to the town. The truth has not. I have checked the accounts twice: it is not coming. I will keep them anyway. — Your wife, in the only office that never changed flags.*

Su-Lin sat for a long time with her hands flat on the counter glass, over the trays of beads sorted rust to rose, and let the letter and the lunch table stand in the same room of her head until she could see them clearly: sixty years apart, the same craft, and not the same at all. Her great-grandmother's saying-nothing had been built by hand, under occupation, to keep a child alive, and had cost the maker everything, daily, for the rest of her life. Her family's saying-nothing was the same object mass-produced: promise me, not a word, her heart, the optics — a craft with the love sanded off, handed down the official line along with the pricing instinct, until nobody could remember that it had ever been for anything.

*For Mei Lan, when she asks.* Sixty years the tin had waited on that hinge, and the hinge had never turned, because the woman who was owed the asking had spent her whole life being carefully, expertly afraid of the answer — *I have never owned a beaded slipper*, flat as a door meeting its frame, answering what nobody had asked. The two of them had kept their disciplines at each other across a whole half-century, the mother waiting to be asked, the daughter refusing to ask, and the tin sitting between them like a held breath.

Somebody had to fail at the family craft. Su-Lin found she was qualified.

She went upstairs and brought the tin down to the lamp, and took out the photograph, and unfolded it.

The crease was sixty-two years old and opened stiffly, in stages, like a joint waking. And the hidden half came up out of itself unfaded — kept face-in against the card all these years, protected by its own concealment, so that the bride emerged a shade brighter than her own wedding, as if that part of the day had been stored somewhere the decades couldn't reach.

Su-Lin had braced to recognize her, and did not, and the not-recognizing was its own quiet blow. She had met Great-Aunt Foong perhaps four times: a small grey woman at the edge of group photographs, straight-backed, leaving before the food. There was no grey woman here. Here was a girl of nineteen, slight, in a kebaya cut for someone grander, her chin level, her spine already the spine of the woman who would square envelopes to counter edges for sixty years — looking into the camera with an expression Su-Lin knew intimately after three months inside her handwriting: solemn on instruction, and about to fail at it. The same almost-smile as the man whose hand she held. Two people keeping straight faces for a photographer they found funny, on the one morning of their lives.

And on her feet, neat as type against the studio floor, the slippers. Peony and phoenix, rust and gold and jade rendered grey by the film: the pair complete, that morning in 1942 — the left one now lying sixty years in a tin upstairs, the right one gone wherever right ones go.

Su-Lin set the photograph on the counter, open, both faces up, the crease running between them like a mended wall.

Then she called the hotel off Jonker Street, where she had once slept for exactly one night, and asked for two rooms.

"From when, miss?"

"Friday."

"How many nights?"

"I don't know yet," Su-Lin said. "Keep them."

She carried the Nokia out to the airwell, where the one reliable bar lived, into the last of the evening light coming down the shaft. Above her the cage hung on its hook, turning a little, its door wired back, ready for whatever chose to come. She scrolled to AH MA and stood for a moment with her thumb over the key, at the bottom of the well of her great-grandmother's house, in the place where the rain came in.

She pressed the green key and lifted the phone to her ear, and far away in Kuala Lumpur, in a tidy house at the hour of the evening programmes, a telephone began to ring.
