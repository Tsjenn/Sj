# The Grave

They climbed Bukit Cina at seven, before the heat, and Su-Lin's grandmother went up the first slope faster than she did, in canvas shoes bought the previous afternoon, carrying a plastic bag of cleaning things assembled at the sundry shop the way another woman assembles an argument.

Hafiz had driven them and stayed with the car at the foot of the hill, because Su-Lin had said *family* in the tone that means it and he had said *I'll be here* in the tone that means that. It was the last week of August, and the flags hung out below for the month read from up here as a rash of red and white among the roofs, forty-eight years old and a week from a birthday.

The hill was a hill of dead: terraces of graves going up and over and down the far side, the old ones sunk to a lip of coral-lime, the newer shaped like armchairs, like laps — a plaster arm around a mound, a stone table in front as though the dead kept house and received.

"They sat us on the tables at Cheng Beng," Mei Lan said, stopping, not for breath. "Out of the grass, because of snakes." She looked up the slope. "I have not been on this hill since nineteen fifty-six. Read me the paper."

Su-Lin read it walking. It had come out of a file in Mr. Devan's cabinet older than she was: a burial right in the name of Ho Mei Foong, spinster, of Heeren Street, bought in September 1961, with a plot number and a sketch plan whose north arrow had been drawn by somebody who cared about it. Fees paid annually. And a second sheet, dated 1998: the same woman paying those fees forward thirty years in one sum, with a note in Chinese Mr. Devan had had translated for the file: *so that no one need be asked.*

"Nineteen sixty-one," Mei Lan said. "Why then?"

"Uncle Raju says they closed the hill to new burials soon after; if you didn't hold ground you didn't get any. She was thirty-nine."

"Thirty-nine," said her grandmother, "and buying a hole in the ground next to us." It came out flat, but she had said *us*, and Su-Lin heard it go past like a bird.

The Tan graves lay two-thirds of the way up, and Su-Lin knew them before they were named, because she had spent three months inside a dead woman's handwriting and the crescent was in it: nine graves in a rough curve, said the letters, and now fourteen, the curve grown by two generations and stopped. Grass to the knee between them, the plaster furred grey-green with a wet season's mould, the red paint in the characters gone the colour of old brick.

Mei Lan stood at the edge of it with her bag in her hand. "Nobody has come."

"The family sold the mansion in seventy-four. The sweeping arrangement ran a few years, then the man who did it died."

"I know when we sold the mansion. I was there." She put the bag down and set out gloves, a grass knife, a brush and a bottle of water on the nearest stone table, squared, the way you set out instruments. "Well. The work comes before the praying."

"Says who?"

"Says everybody," said Mei Lan — and stopped with the brush in her hand, hearing herself, arriving for the first time that morning at a thing she knew without knowing how. "Says the hill," she said, more slowly, and put the gloves on.

They cut the lalang back from the two newest graves. Su-Lin took hold of it barehanded once and learned why the gloves, and her grandmother said "I did tell you" without looking up, which was the nearest thing to tenderness she carried before nine. Under the grass the stones came up: NEO GUAT ENG, her dates, and the long column of descendants cut smaller down the right-hand side; and beside her, TAN HOCK LYE, 1961.

"My grandmother. And my grandfather, who was frightened of nothing, and took us to Kuala Lumpur because he had decided the future was there, and was right, and was dead in four years." Mei Lan rinsed the brush. "There is my father's name. And there is mine, under his. Lim Mei Lan."

Su-Lin knelt in the grass and looked at her grandmother's name cut in granite in 1961 under the wrong line of the wrong family, and said nothing, because there was nothing yet that could be said standing up.

"She is not here," Mei Lan said. "Read the number again."

Su-Lin turned the sketch plan to its north arrow and then turned herself, and did what she had been trained twelve years to do: stopped looking at objects and looked at the space between them. The crescent held its shelf of the hill with the confidence of purchased ground. Downhill the slope fell to a band of lalang and a scatter of poor small stones, some no more than a lump of concrete with the characters scratched in while it was wet.

At the near edge of that band, past the last of the family's mould-grey plaster, stood a small granite stone, plain, with no plaster arms; and the grass round it was cut, squared off a foot all round. On the ledge at its foot stood a jam jar with ixora going brown in it, two candle stubs burnt to their tin, and one orange.

"There," Su-Lin said.

Her grandmother walked down through the long grass and stopped in front of the stone, and read it, and stood.

HO MEI FOONG, it said, with the county of her birth cut small above it, a place in Kwangtung neither of them had seen, and her dates, 1922 to 2004, and the year of the cutting, 1998 — six years early, because she had ordered her own stone and had it kept in the carver's yard.

And down the right-hand side, where on every other stone on that hill the sons and daughters stand in their ranks in the column that says *this one was ours and we put her here* — the granite had been dressed smooth, and polished, and left empty.

"It isn't blank," Su-Lin said. "Ah Ma, it's kept. You leave stone rough when you're not using it. That has been dressed and polished, the same as the face. She paid a carver to prepare a column and put nothing in it. That isn't an omission, it's a reservation. You draw a space that way when what goes in it isn't allowed yet."

Mei Lan said nothing.

Su-Lin looked back up the slope at the fourteen stones on their lip of family ground, and something in her went cold and began to measure.

She paced it, heel to toe, up through the lalang to the corner of the last plaster arm; and again, because the first count came out at a number she did not want; and a third time.

Eleven feet.

"Eleven feet," she said out loud.

"What?"

"From her stone to theirs." The sentence arrived whole, out of a night in 1943 she had read at a counter under a lamp with a dictionary: *I sleep eleven feet from my daughter's door. I have measured it. I do not intend to stop measuring it.* "It's in a letter, from when she went to work in your father's house." She did not look round. "It's a coincidence. Hills aren't drawings—"

"She could," said Mei Lan. "You are talking about a woman who measured a room by lying down in it. I have watched her price a pair of shoes to the cent from across a shop by looking at a foot." She had not taken her eyes off the stone. "Say the rest."

Su-Lin put it down.

"She's outside the line. The family ground stops at the horn of the crescent, and she bought the nearest plot that wasn't in it. Inside was impossible: inside is for wives and daughters of the house, and by their books she was staff — and she knew exactly how their books worked, because she kept them." Her mouth had gone dry. "She had thirty-nine years to change her mind. She only paid the fees forward, so nobody would ever have to be asked whether she could stay."

"Near," said Mei Lan. "Never inside."

"Yes."

"Give me the drawing. One sentence. You are an architect."

Su-Lin looked at the eleven feet of lalang between a woman and the family she had been born into, and said: "She's buried the way she stood in the photograph."

Her grandmother made a sound.

It was not a sound Su-Lin had heard from her in thirty-four years, and it was not weeping and not speech; it was more like a beam giving — the low noise a thing makes when it stops carrying a load it has carried so long that everyone mistook the strain for the shape of the object.

Then Mei Lan went down onto her knees in the wet grass in her good grey trousers, and put both hands flat on the granite ledge, and broke.

Su-Lin did not touch her. It was the hardest deliberate thing she had ever done: her grandmother had been upright in front of other people since 1957, and a hand on her back now would be a hand asking her to stop.

It went on. Sixty-two years does not come out fast.

What came out with it, in pieces, spoken to a headstone, was the furniture of a life that had never been explained; and Su-Lin understood that she was listening to an inventory.

That there had been an amah in the doctor's house when she was small, who slept in the passage; that she had known in the animal way children know which adult would come if she was sick in the night — always that one — and had never once asked why the servant came before the mother.

That she had been six when the amah left, and was told she had been let go, and cried for a month in a house where crying was untidy; and had found her again a week later, four doors down, in a shop doorway with a bride's slipper in her hand. That nobody had explained how a dismissed amah came to own a shophouse on Heeren Street, and she had never asked, because at six a miracle is not a question, and by the time it could have been one she had learned that the past was a room in that family with a door on it.

That the coldness had not been hers. That she had been taught it — *we do not discuss Malacca, the old house, your mother's health, the war* — and had taken it up like a household craft and done it beautifully for fifty years, on her children, on her granddaughter, in a telephone call in the rain about a tin of letters. *I have never owned a beaded slipper*, flat as a door meeting its frame.

And that she could bead. That came last and worst. That her hands at eleven could put six beads to the stitch and hold a line straight over four inches, taught at a long table under a lamp by a woman not related to her, who made her unpick a whole evening's work in front of two apprentices without one word of comfort, which had made her furious and had made her good. That she had beaded four years, put it down at fifteen in Kuala Lumpur, never been able to say why, and had said *nobody has the hands for it anymore* at televisions for forty years with her own hands in her lap knowing perfectly well what they could do.

"She taught me," Mei Lan said to the stone. "That is not a thing an amah does. Amahs mind you. She taught me, and then let them take me to Kuala Lumpur and never wrote me one letter in forty-seven years, and I thought—" Her voice went. "I thought she stopped caring for me when I stopped being small. I have thought it since I was fifteen. I built things on it."

She got her breath back the way she did everything, in stages.

"She wrote you ninety-one of them," Su-Lin said.

"They are addressed to a dead man."

"They're about you."

Her grandmother nodded once, without turning round. Then, in a voice sanded down to the wood: "Get the flask."

They did the rites the way people do who have been taught the form of one thing and are performing another.

Mei Lan swept the ledge and set out what she had: three cups from the sundry shop, one still with its price sticker, which she peeled off and pocketed because she was not going to leave rubbish; a flask of tea made that morning with a leaf from the merchant Uncle Raju had been unbearable about; three joss sticks; and jasmine for the jam jar, the dead ixora laid tidily at the side rather than thrown away.

She lit the joss and planted it, and knelt again, and poured: three times the cup filled and tipped out at the foot of the stone and set back and filled again. Su-Lin, who had stood through a hundred of these and never once watched, watched, and saw that her grandmother's hands knew the count.

Then Mei Lan sat back on her heels and did not bow.

"I don't know what I am allowed to do," she said. "A niece bows. A daughter kneels and does the three. If I do the three and I am wrong, I am a fool on a hill. If I bow, I am doing what they made her do at the back of a photograph." She put her palms down on the wet ground. "I would rather be a fool."

She put her head down to her hands, three times, slowly, an old woman with a bad knee doing a thing her body had never done; and Su-Lin watched a lifetime of correct behaviour go into the grass.

When she came up her face was a wreck and entirely steady.

"She was my mother," she said.

Su-Lin's eyes stung.

"She was my mother. Your great-grandmother." Mei Lan turned, still on her knees, and looked up, and her voice was not loud and not broken and it was the most naked thing Su-Lin had ever heard from anyone in her family. "Say it so I can hear it outside my own head. I have been saying it in there since three o'clock this morning and I can no longer tell whether it is true or whether I have talked myself into it; and if it is only in my head I will take it apart again by tonight. That is what I do. Say it out loud, where I can hear it in somebody else's mouth."

Su-Lin stood beside her with the joss smoke going up between them, and said it in the ordinary voice she used for reading a level off a staff.

"Ho Mei Foong was your mother. She is my great-grandmother. Her husband was Tan Boon Siew, and he is my great-grandfather, and you are their daughter, and you were born on the tenth of November, in the rain, at Ayer Keroh, and you weighed five catties on a rubber scale."

Her grandmother closed her eyes.

"Read me the one with the weight in it," she said. "The joy one."

Su-Lin took out the notebook and read it to the stone.

*Dear husband. You have a daughter. She was born on the tenth of November, in rain, and weighed five catties on the scale they use here for rubber, and she has your brow — do not be vain about it. She is called Mei Lan. She eats like your father drives a bargain and sleeps like your mother forgives, which is to say briefly, on conditions. I have opened an account in your name and I enter her in it daily. You told them to tell me the accounts are mine now. Very well. I am keeping them for two.*

Down the hill somewhere a grass-cutter started, and stopped, and started.

"Sixty-two years," said Mei Lan, to nobody, "and the first person who ever told me I was hers was a piece of paper in your handwriting."

---

Hafiz drove them into the town, to a kopitiam that was not Uncle Raju's, because neither woman had a face fit for Uncle Raju yet.

Mei Lan drank half a cup of tea and became, in about ninety seconds, a retired branch manager.

"Right. What can be corrected?"

Su-Lin had the list. "Legally, almost nothing that matters. Your birth was registered in 1946 off the household record: mother Tan Bee Nio, father Lim Cheng Hoe. To change it you need a court declaration, and for that, evidence a judge will accept about the parentage of four dead people. The wartime paper is ruled foolscap with a rubber chop from an estate hospital that stopped existing in 1948. My lawyer's words: *it's beautiful and it isn't evidence.* Three years and a lot of money to be told no more expensively."

"I am sixty-two and I have a knee. I am not spending my sixties in a queue at the registration department for a document telling me what I already know." Mei Lan turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer — a gesture Su-Lin had watched an old man make two streets away a hundred times and never once connected to her own grandmother's hands. "Besides. It is the wrong office. The government kept no column for what she was; every paper she ever carried was accurate and completely false. There is no sense in asking that counter now to be truthful about her. It would be one more form with the truth left out."

"Then what?"

"Then we use the office that took her," said Mei Lan. "The dead keep better records. Nobody in that hall has ever asked me for a birth certificate in my life."

---

The memorial hall behind the prayer hall at Cheng Hoon Teng was cool as a cellar and smelt of sandalwood ash, and the tablets stood in tiers from floor to above head height, black lacquer and gold characters, ranked like a congregation that has learned to stand very still.

The clerk was seventy and delighted; nobody came about the old shelves any more, he said, only the new ones. He found the Tan shelf in his ledger with one finger, and there they were, lodged in 1974 when the mansion went to a buyer from Singapore and its hall was emptied in an afternoon. NEO GUAT ENG, in gold, with her dates and her titles. Beside her, a hand shorter, plain, with no dates at all: NEO GUAT KIM. And at the end of the row, small and dark and gold-charactered, cut in September 1945 for a man of twenty-two with no grave anywhere in the world: TAN BOON SIEW.

At the end of the shelf there was a space of about four inches.

"Who pays for this shelf?" Su-Lin asked.

It was in the ledger, and the ledger went back. Annual dues, in a hand that changed as the clerks changed: *Ho Mei Foong, Heeren Street.* Every year from 1974 to 2003 without one gap; and then a sum lodged with the temple, enough to keep the whole shelf in incense for thirty years, paid by the same woman, who was not on it, and who had never once — the clerk checked the visitors' book with real regret — signed the book downstairs.

Mei Lan looked at the four inches of empty shelf beside her father's tablet for a long time.

"That," she said.

The carver's shop was up past the temple, a narrow lot smelling of teak dust, run by a man in a singlet who made shop signs, mah-jong tables and tablets, who fetched a chair for the old lady without being asked and got out his brush and a sheet of newsprint.

"Name of the deceased?"

"Ho Mei Foong."

"Relationship, for the line under. Husband's house, if there is one."

The bandsaw ran at the back. Mei Lan sat with her handbag on her knees.

"Write it," she said. "Ho Mei Foong. Wife of Tan Boon Siew."

The carver wrote it and turned the sheet round; and Mei Lan took the brush and did it again in her own hand — fast, definite strokes, no hesitation in them, a hand trained by somebody in a shop with a lamp over the table and never afterwards lost. Su-Lin looked at the six characters going onto the newsprint, thought of a young man in a studio photograph with brows like the strokes of a fast writer, and went out to stand on the five-foot way among the offcuts.

Ten working days, said the carver, and the gold ten days after; and he would deliver it to the hall himself and set it in, which is not a thing a man does for a fee. Mei Lan paid half in cash, asked for a receipt, and put it in her handbag; and that was the marriage of February 1942 entered at last in the only register ever open to it.

---

They came back to the shophouse at six, and Su-Lin put on the lamp over the counter and made tea neither of them drank.

Her grandmother walked the shop as she had the first evening, which was not how anyone else walked it: not looking at the beads, looking at the heights of things. The counter. The stool. The hook where the good lamp hung. Then the middle hall behind, the brass polished, and the small slipper on its square of faded silk where Su-Lin had put it back — a child's slipper the length of a palm, its sole white and unmarked.

Mei Lan stood in front of the altar table with her handbag over her arm.

"I asked her about that when I was thirteen. Standing exactly here. I asked where the other one was, and she said there never was another one, and told me I was standing in her light." She let a breath out through her nose. "Fifty years I have believed a woman who was in the room."

Then she opened the handbag.

What came out was a plastic wallet, the flat kind a bank used to give with a new passbook, printed with the name of a Kuala Lumpur bank a bigger one had swallowed in the nineties, the plastic yellowed and soft as cloth along the folds, taped at the top with a strip that had dried out and been replaced, more than once, by somebody careful.

She worked it open and took out a slipper.

A woman's beaded slipper, right foot: the peony worked rust into gold, the phoenix tail barred jade and night-blue, forty small watchful suns down the length of it, the beads so fine the eye refused them. The black ground had greyed along one edge where the plastic had lain for half a century. The sole was shaped by walking.

Su-Lin did not trust herself to reach out, so she didn't.

"We left on the third of September, nineteen fifty-seven," Mei Lan said. "Three days after Merdeka. The lorry came in the morning and my grandfather stood in the road with a list, and my mother said, pack only what is yours." She held the slipper flat on both palms, the way you carry something full. "I went down to the shop at five that morning, while she was asleep at her counter, and took it out of the second drawer under the till, where it lived wrapped in blue cloth, and put it in my school bag. I have never told one living person until this minute."

"Ah Ma—"

"I did not know why. I want that understood, because I have had sixty-two years to think of a reason and never once produced one that would stand up." Her voice was perfectly level and her hands were not. "I knew she kept it. I had seen her take it out after the shutters were down, twice, and hold it, and put it back. I was fourteen and being taken three hours away from the only person who ever answered a question of mine straight, and I took the thing she loved so that she would have to—" She set her jaw and finished it, because she was that kind of woman. "So that she would have to come and get it. And she never came. And I could not send it back, because that would have been confessing, and I could not throw it away. So I carried it through a marriage, four houses and two floods, at the back of every wardrobe I have owned, and never asked myself the question. Not once. Su-Lin. I audited other people for a living."

She looked down at it.

"It is the only thing I have ever stolen," she said.

Su-Lin went up the stairs and came back with the indigo cloth, and unwrapped it under the lamp: the left one, heavier than it looked, sixty years under a tin lid, its black ground unfaded.

They put them together on the altar shelf, beside the child's slipper on its silk. It took a moment to do properly. Mei Lan moved the left one a quarter of an inch with one finger, and then again, until the toes were level.

The two of them stood back and looked at the pair.

"Well," said Mei Lan, in the voice she used for closing an account, and got no further; and out in Heeren Street the lamps came on down the whole terrace at once, the way they do, and somebody two doors up rolled a shutter down. And on the altar shelf of a house bought with silence and paid for with forty years of beadwork, a pair of wedding slippers stood side by side for the first time since a February morning in 1942, toes level, a hand's width apart, in the light.
