# The Grave

They climbed Bukit Cina at seven, before the heat, and Su-Lin's grandmother went up the first slope faster than she did, in canvas shoes bought the previous afternoon for the purpose, carrying a plastic bag of cleaning things she had assembled at the sundry shop the way another woman might assemble an argument.

Hafiz had driven them and stayed with the car at the foot of the hill, near the old well with its wire cover and its coins, because Su-Lin had said *family* in the tone that means it, and he had said *I'll be here* in the tone that means that, and had unfolded a newspaper across the steering wheel. It was the last week of August, and the town below was hung with flags for the month — small ones on wires across Jonker Street, a paper one gummed to the back of a trishaw — so that from up here, with the light coming grey-pink off the strait behind the container gantries, the flags read as a rash of red and white among the roofs, forty-eight years old and a week from its birthday.

The hill was a hill of dead. Su-Lin had known it the way you know a fact about your own town, which is to say not at all, until she stood in it: terraces of graves going up and over and down the other side, the old ones sunk to a lip of coral-lime in the grass, the newer ones shaped like armchairs, like laps — a curved plaster arm around a mound, a stone table set in front as though the dead kept house and received.

"They sat us on the tables," Mei Lan said. She had stopped, not for breath. "Cheng Beng. They put the children up out of the grass because of the snakes and gave us the oranges to hold, and we were not to eat them. I ate one." She looked up the slope with her chin level. "I have not been on this hill since nineteen fifty-six."

"Do you know the way?"

"I knew the way at thirteen. Read me the paper."

Su-Lin read it walking, which she was good at and her grandmother was not, and did not say so. It was a photostat of a photostat out of a file that had waited in Mr. Devan's cabinet since before Su-Lin was born: a burial right on the hill in the name of Ho Mei Foong, spinster, of Heeren Street, purchased in September 1961, with a plot number and a sketch plan whose north arrow had been drawn by someone who cared about it. Fees paid annually. And a second sheet, dated 1998, on which the same woman had paid those fees forward thirty years in one sum, by bank draft, with a note in Chinese that Mr. Devan had had translated for the file: *so that no one need be asked.*

"Nineteen sixty-one," Mei Lan said. "Why then?"

"Uncle Raju says they closed the hill to new burials soon after. If you didn't hold ground you didn't get any." Su-Lin turned the sheet over. "She was thirty-nine."

"Thirty-nine," said her grandmother, "and buying a hole in the ground next to us." It came out flat, but she had said *us*, and Su-Lin heard it go past like a bird.

The Tan graves lay two-thirds of the way up, and Su-Lin knew them before they were named, because she had spent three months inside a dead woman's handwriting and the crescent was in it: nine graves in a rough curve, said the letters, and now there were fourteen, the curve grown by two generations and stopped. Grass to the knee between them. The plaster arms furred grey-green with a whole wet season's mould, the red paint in the characters gone the colour of old brick, and across the two newest, at the downhill end, a wash of dried mud where the slope had let go in some rain and never been put back.

Mei Lan stood at the edge of it with her bag in her hand. "Nobody has come."

"The family sold the mansion in seventy-four. The sweeping arrangement went on a few years and then the man who did it died."

"I know when we sold the mansion. I was there. I signed nothing and was told everything twice." She put the bag down and took out gloves, a grass knife, a scrubbing brush, a bottle of water, and set them on the nearest stone table in a row, squared, the way you set out instruments. "Well. The work comes before the praying."

"Says who?"

"Says everybody," said Mei Lan, surprised into it — and stopped with the brush in her hand, hearing herself, arriving for the first time that morning at a thing she knew without knowing how she knew it. "Says the hill," she said, more slowly, and put the gloves on.

They cut the lalang back from the two newest graves. Su-Lin took hold of it barehanded once and learned why the gloves, the long blade drawing a fine line across the pad of her thumb, and her grandmother said "I did tell you" without looking up, which was the nearest thing to tenderness she carried before nine in the morning. Under the grass and the mud-wash the stones came up: NEO GUAT ENG, and her dates, and the long column of descendants cut smaller down the right-hand side; and beside her, sharing the plaster arm, TAN HOCK LYE, 1961, with his own column of names.

"My grandmother," said Mei Lan, scrubbing. "She died before I was old enough to be properly frightened of her. And that one is my grandfather, who was frightened of nothing, and took us to Kuala Lumpur because he had decided the future was there, and was right, and was dead in four years." She rinsed the brush. "There is my father's name. And there is mine, under his. Lim Mei Lan."

Su-Lin knelt in the grass and looked at her grandmother's name cut in granite in 1961 under the wrong line of the wrong family, and said nothing, because there was nothing yet that could be said standing up.

They did as much of the crescent as two people can do before the sun clears the shoulder of a hill, and stopped, because fourteen graves is a week's work and they had come for one. Mei Lan straightened with a hand at the small of her back and looked along the row, and then out over the tops of the graves below, to the strait.

"She is not here," she said. "Read the number again."

Su-Lin read the number, and the sketch plan, and turned it to the north arrow and then turned herself, and did the thing she had been trained for twelve years to do, which was to stop looking at objects and start looking at the space between them. The crescent occupied its shelf of the hill with the confidence of purchased ground. Downhill of it the slope fell away to a band of lalang and a scatter of small stones, unrelated, poor, some no more than a shaped lump of concrete with the characters scratched in while it was wet.

At the near edge of that band, below the crescent's downhill horn, past the last of the family's mould-grey plaster, stood a small granite stone, upright, plain, with no plaster arms at all; and the grass around it was cut. Cut recently and cut properly, squared off a foot all round; and on the ledge at its foot stood a jam jar with a fistful of ixora going brown in it, two candle stubs burnt down to their tin, and one orange.

"There," Su-Lin said.

Her grandmother did not move for a moment. Then she walked down through the long grass and stopped in front of the stone and read it, and stood.

HO MEI FOONG, it said, with the county of her birth cut small above it in the old way — a place in Kwangtung neither of them had ever seen — and her dates, 1922 to 2004, and the year of the cutting, which was 1998: six years before it was wanted, because she had ordered her own stone and paid for it and had it kept in the carver's yard.

And down the right-hand side, where on every other stone on that hill the sons and daughters and grandsons stand in their ranks, smaller than the name, in the column that says *this one was ours and we put her here* — the granite had been dressed smooth, and polished, and left empty.

Su-Lin looked at it and felt the floor of her chest go out from under her, professionally, the way it went when she read a drawing and understood the building.

"It isn't blank," she said. "Ah Ma. It's kept. You leave stone rough when you're not using it. That has been dressed and polished, the same as the face. She paid a carver to prepare a column and put nothing in it." Her voice had gone quick and she let it. "That's not an omission. That's a reservation. You draw a space that way when the thing that goes in it isn't allowed yet."

Mei Lan said nothing.

Su-Lin turned and looked back up the slope at the swept lip of family ground and the fourteen stones sitting on it, and something in her went carefully cold and began, without being asked, to measure.

She paced it. Heel to toe, up through the lalang from the small stone to the corner of the last plaster arm, counting; and then again, because the first count came out at a number she did not want; and then a third time, without the tape she had never in her adult life left behind before, so that she had to do it with her body, like a woman measuring cloth when the tape is lost.

Eleven feet.

"Eleven feet," she said out loud.

"What?"

"From her stone to theirs." Su-Lin stood in the grass between the two and heard the sentence arrive whole, out of a night in 1943 that she had read at a counter under a lamp, four evenings, with a dictionary: *I sleep eleven feet from my daughter's door. I have measured it. I do not intend to stop measuring it.* "There's a letter, from when she went to work in your father's house. She wrote it down." She did not look round. "It's the same distance. It's a coincidence, hills aren't drawings, she can't have—"

"She could," said Mei Lan.

The two of them stood in the grass with the sun coming over the shoulder of the hill.

"She could," her grandmother said again. "You are talking about a woman who measured a room by lying down in it. I have watched her price a pair of shoes to the last cent from across a shop by looking at a foot." She had not taken her eyes from the stone. "Say the rest. You have carried it up this hill for an hour and you are going to put it down."

Su-Lin put it down.

"She's outside the line. The family ground stops at the horn of the crescent, and she bought the nearest plot to it that wasn't in it — as near as she could get without being inside. Twenty feet would have made her a stranger. Inside was impossible, because inside is for wives and daughters of the house, and by their books she was staff, and she knew exactly how their books worked, because she kept them." Her mouth had gone dry. "She did it in 1961. She had thirty-nine years to change her mind and never did. She only moved the fees forward, so that nobody would ever have to be asked whether she could stay."

"Near," said Mei Lan. "Never inside."

"Yes."

"Give me the drawing, Su-Lin. One sentence. You are an architect."

Su-Lin looked at the eleven feet of lalang between a woman and the family she had been born into, and said: "She's buried the way she stood in the photograph."

Her grandmother made a sound.

It was not a sound Su-Lin had heard from her in thirty-four years, and afterwards she was never able to describe it to anybody, because it was not weeping and it was not speech; it was more like a beam giving — one structural noise, low, that a thing makes at the moment it stops carrying a load it has carried so long that everyone has begun to mistake the strain for the shape of the object.

Then Mei Lan went down onto her knees in the wet grass in her good grey trousers, and put both hands flat on the granite ledge, and broke.

Su-Lin did not touch her. It was the hardest deliberate thing she had ever done, and it was correct; she knew it the way you know a load path. Her grandmother had been holding herself upright in front of other people since 1957, and a hand on her back now would be a hand asking her to stop. So Su-Lin stood two feet behind her left shoulder and let her.

It went on. Sixty-two years does not come out fast.

What came out with it, in pieces, in no order, spoken to a headstone, was the furniture of a life that had never been explained; and Su-Lin, standing behind her, understood that she was listening to an inventory.

That there had been an amah in the doctor's house when she was small, who slept in the passage, and that she had known in the animal way children know which of the adults in the world would come if she was sick in the night — always that one — and had never in fifty years asked herself why the servant came before the mother.

That she had been six when the amah left, and was told she had been let go, and had cried for a month in a house where crying was untidy; and had found her again a week later, four doors down and across, standing in a shop doorway with a bride's slipper in her hand. That nobody had ever explained how a dismissed amah came to own a shophouse on Heeren Street, and she had never asked, because at six a miracle is not a question, and by the time she was old enough to make it one she had learned from every adult around her that the past was a room in that family with a door on it.

That the coldness had not been hers. That she had been taught it — *we do not discuss Malacca, we do not discuss the old house, your mother's health, the war; stop asking* — and had taken it up like a household craft and done it beautifully for fifty years, on her own children, on her own granddaughter, in a telephone call in the rain about a tin of letters. *I have never owned a beaded slipper*, flat as a door meeting its frame.

And that she could bead. That came last and worst. That her hands at eleven could put six beads to the stitch and hold a line straight over four inches, taught at a long table under a lamp by a woman who was not related to her, who corrected her wrists and made her unpick a whole evening's work in front of two apprentices without one word of comfort, which had made her furious, and which had made her good. That she had beaded four years and put it down at fifteen in Kuala Lumpur and never taken it up, and had never once been able to say why, and had said *nobody has the hands for it anymore* at televisions for forty years with her own hands lying in her lap knowing perfectly well what they could do.

"She taught me," Mei Lan said to the stone. "That is not a thing an amah does. Amahs mind you. She taught me. She made me good at something, and then let them take me to Kuala Lumpur and did not write me one letter in forty-seven years, and I thought—" Her voice went. "I thought she stopped caring for me when I stopped being small. I have thought that since I was fifteen. I have built things on it."

She stayed where she was and got her breath back the way she did everything, methodically, in stages.

"She wrote you two hundred and nine of them," Su-Lin said.

"They are addressed to a dead man."

"They're about you."

Her grandmother nodded once, without turning round. Then, in a voice sanded down to the wood: "Get the flask."

They did the rites the way people do who have been taught the form of one thing and are performing another, which Su-Lin came to think later was the only honest way anything has ever been done on that hill.

Mei Lan swept the ledge with her hand and set out what she had: three little cups from the sundry shop, one still with its price sticker, which she peeled off and put in her pocket because she was not going to leave rubbish; a flask of tea made that morning with the hotel kettle and a leaf from the merchant on Jonker Street that Uncle Raju had named and been unbearable about; three joss sticks; and a small bunch of jasmine bought at the gate from a woman with a bicycle, to go in the jam jar with the dead ixora, which she took out and laid tidily at the side rather than throw away.

She lit the joss from Su-Lin's lighter and planted it, and knelt again, and poured. Three times the little cup was filled and tipped out at the foot of the stone and set back and filled again; and Su-Lin, who had stood through a hundred of these and never once watched, watched, and saw that her grandmother's hands knew the count.

Then Mei Lan sat back on her heels and did not bow.

"I don't know what I am allowed to do," she said. "A niece bows. A daughter kneels and does the three. If I do the three and I am wrong, I am a fool on a hill. If I bow, I am doing what they made her do at the back of a photograph, for sixty-two years." She put her palms down on the wet ground. "I would rather be a fool."

She put her head down to her hands, three times, slowly, an old woman with a bad knee doing a thing her body had never done; and Su-Lin stood behind her and watched a lifetime of correct behaviour go into the grass.

When she came up, her face was a wreck and entirely steady.

"She was my mother," she said.

Su-Lin's eyes stung.

"She was my mother. Your great-grandmother." Mei Lan turned, still on her knees, and looked up at her granddaughter, and her voice was not loud and not broken and it was the most naked thing Su-Lin had ever heard from anyone in her family. "Say it so I can hear it outside my own head. I have been saying it in there since three o'clock this morning and I can no longer tell whether it is true or whether I have talked myself into it; and if it is only in my head I will start taking it apart again by tonight. That is what I do. I take things apart. So say it out loud, in the open, where I can hear it in somebody else's mouth."

Su-Lin came and stood beside her at the stone, with the smoke of the joss going up straight between them, and said it out loud at nine in the morning in the ordinary voice she used for reading a level off a staff.

"Ho Mei Foong was your mother. She is my great-grandmother. Her husband was Tan Boon Siew, and he is my great-grandfather, and you are their daughter, and you were born on the tenth of November, in the rain, at Ayer Keroh, and you weighed five catties on a rubber scale."

Her grandmother closed her eyes.

"Read me the one with the weight in it," she said. "You said there is one with joy in it. I want the joy one."

Su-Lin took the notebook out of her back pocket, because she carried the translations everywhere now, the way she had once carried a site diary, and found the page, and read it to the stone.

*Dear husband. You have a daughter. She was born on the tenth of November, in rain, and weighed five catties on the scale they use here for rubber, and she has your brow — do not be vain about it. She is called Mei Lan. She eats like your father drives a bargain and sleeps like your mother forgives, which is to say briefly, on conditions. I have opened an account in your name and I enter her in it daily. You told them to tell me the accounts are mine now. Very well. I am keeping them for two.*

The tea had gone into the ground. Down the hill somewhere a grass-cutter started, and stopped, and started.

"Sixty-two years," said Mei Lan, to nobody, "and the first person who ever told me I was hers was a piece of paper in your handwriting."

They came down on the temple side, past the well where the coins lay in the water. Hafiz saw them coming, folded his newspaper, got out and opened both doors, asked nothing at all, and drove them to a kopitiam that was not Uncle Raju's, because neither woman had a face fit for Uncle Raju yet.

Mei Lan drank half a cup of tea, set it down, and became, in about ninety seconds, a retired branch manager.

"Right. What can be corrected?"

Su-Lin had the list; she had made it at two in the morning three nights running, because a list was the only thing she had known how to do with what was in the tin. "Legally, almost nothing that matters. Your birth was registered in 1946, in the catch-up registration after the war, off the household record: mother Tan Bee Nio, father Lim Cheng Hoe. To change it you need a court declaration, and for that you need evidence a judge will accept about the parentage of four dead people, two of them buried before Merdeka. There is nothing to test. The wartime paper is a sheet of ruled foolscap with a rubber chop from an estate hospital that stopped existing in 1948. My lawyer's words were, and I'm quoting, *it's beautiful and it isn't evidence.*"

"So the answer is no."

"The answer is no, unless you want three years and a lot of money to be told no more expensively."

"I am sixty-two and I have a knee. I am not spending my sixties in a queue at the registration department to be issued a document telling me something I already know." Mei Lan turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer — a gesture Su-Lin had watched an old man make two streets away a hundred times, and had never once connected to her own grandmother's hands. "Besides. It is the wrong office."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning she never applied to that one." Mei Lan looked out at the street, where a man was hanging one more flag. "The government never kept a column for what she was; she wrote that herself, and you read it to me at half past one this morning, and I have been chewing it since. Every paper she ever carried was accurate and completely false. There is no sense in my going to that same counter now and asking it to be truthful about her. It would only be one more form with the truth left out."

"Then what?"

"Then we use the office that took her," said Mei Lan. "The dead keep better records. Nobody in that hall has ever asked me for a birth certificate in my life."

---

The memorial hall behind the prayer hall at Cheng Hoon Teng was cool as a cellar and smelt of sixty years of sandalwood ash, and the tablets stood in tiers from the floor to above head height, hundreds of them, black lacquer and gold characters, small dark uprights ranked like a congregation that has learned to stand very still.

The clerk was seventy and delighted; nobody came about the old shelves any more, he said, only the new ones. He found the Tan shelf in his ledger with one finger and took them up two steps of a wooden stool, and there they were, lodged in 1974 when the mansion on Heeren Street went to a buyer from Singapore and the ancestral hall was emptied in an afternoon. NEO GUAT ENG, in gold, with her dates and her titles. Beside her, a hand shorter, plain, with no dates at all: NEO GUAT KIM. And at the end of the row, small and dark and gold-charactered, cut in the last week of September 1945 for a man of twenty-two who has no grave anywhere in the world: TAN BOON SIEW.

At the end of the shelf there was a space of about four inches.

"Who pays for this shelf?" Su-Lin asked.

The clerk was pleased to be asked, because it was in the ledger, and the ledger went back. Annual dues, entered every year in a hand that changed as the clerks changed: *Ho Mei Foong, Heeren Street.* Every year from 1974 to 2003, without one gap. And in 2003 a lump sum lodged with the temple, enough to keep the whole Tan shelf in incense and dues for thirty years, paid by the same woman, who was not on the shelf, and who had never once — the clerk checked the visitors' book with real regret — written her name in the book downstairs.

Mei Lan stood in the smoke and looked at the four inches of empty shelf beside her father's tablet for a long time.

"That," she said.

The carver's shop was up past the temple, a narrow lot with a bandsaw at the back and the smell of teak dust, run by a man in a singlet who made shop signs, mah-jong tables and tablets, and who fetched a chair for the old lady without being asked and got out his brush and a sheet of newsprint to take the wording down.

"Name of the deceased?"

"Ho Mei Foong."

"Relationship, for the line under. Husband's house, if there is one."

The bandsaw ran at the back. Mei Lan sat with her handbag on her knees.

"Write it," she said. "Ho Mei Foong. Wife of Tan Boon Siew."

The carver wrote it and turned the sheet round; and Mei Lan took the brush from him and did it again in her own hand — fast, definite strokes, no hesitation anywhere in them, a hand trained by somebody in a shop with a lamp over the table and never afterwards lost — and Su-Lin, looking down over her grandmother's shoulder at the six characters going onto the newsprint, thought of a young man in a studio photograph with brows like the strokes of a fast writer, and had to go and stand out on the five-foot way for a minute among the offcuts.

Ten working days, said the carver, and the gold ten days after; and he would deliver it to the hall himself and set it in with the family's leave, which is not a thing a man does for a fee. He named a price that would not have paid for the fittings of one bathroom in a KL project, and Mei Lan paid half in cash and asked for a receipt and got one and put it in her handbag; and that was the marriage of February 1942 entered at last in the only register that had ever been open to it.

---

They came back to the shophouse at six with the light going long down Heeren Street, and Su-Lin put on the lamp over the counter and made tea neither of them drank.

Her grandmother walked the shop the way she had walked it the first evening, which was not the way anyone else walked it: not looking at the beads, looking at the heights of things. The counter. The stool. The hook where the good lamp hung. And then the middle hall behind, with the altar table against the wall and the brass polished, and the small slipper standing on its square of faded silk where Su-Lin had put it back the week she found it — a child's slipper the length of a palm, its sole white and unmarked.

Mei Lan stood in front of the altar table with her handbag over her arm.

"I asked her about that when I was thirteen," she said. "Standing exactly here. I asked her where the other one was, and she said there never was another one, and told me I was standing in her light." She let a breath out through her nose. "Fifty years I have believed a woman who was in the room."

Then she opened the handbag.

What came out was a plastic wallet, the flat kind a bank used to give with a new passbook, printed with the name of a Kuala Lumpur bank that a bigger one had swallowed in the nineties, the plastic gone yellow and soft as cloth along the folds, taped at the top with a strip that had dried out and been replaced, more than once, by somebody careful.

She worked it open and took out a slipper.

A woman's beaded slipper, right foot: the peony worked rust into gold, the phoenix tail barred jade and night-blue, forty small watchful suns down the length of it, the beads so fine the eye kept refusing them. The black ground had greyed a little along one edge where the plastic had lain against it for half a century. The sole was shaped by walking.

Su-Lin did not trust herself to reach out, so she didn't.

"We left on the third of September, nineteen fifty-seven," Mei Lan said. "Three days after Merdeka. The lorry came in the morning and my grandfather stood in the road with a list, and my mother said, pack only what is yours." She held the slipper flat on both palms, the way you carry something full. "I went down to the shop at five that morning, while she was asleep at her counter, and took it out of the second drawer under the till, where it lived wrapped in blue cloth, and I put it in my school bag under my books, and I got into the lorry. I have never told one living person until this minute."

"Ah Ma—"

"I did not know why. I want that understood, because I have had sixty-two years to think of a reason and have never once produced one that would stand up." Her voice was perfectly level and her hands were not. "I knew she kept it. I had seen her take it out at the counter after the shutters were down, twice, and hold it, and put it back. I was fourteen, and I was being taken three hours away from the only person in the world who ever answered a question of mine straight, and I took the thing she loved so that she would have to—" She stopped, and set her jaw, and finished it, because she was that kind of woman. "So that she would have to come and get it. And she never came. And I could not send it back, because sending it back would have been confessing, and I could not throw it away. So I carried it through a marriage, four houses and two floods, in a bank bag, at the back of every wardrobe I have ever owned, and looked at it perhaps once a year, and never asked myself the question. Not once. Su-Lin. I audited other people for a living."

She looked down at it.

"It is the only thing I have ever stolen," she said.

Su-Lin went up the stairs then, and came back with the indigo cloth, and unwrapped it on the counter under the lamp: the left one, heavier than it looked, sixty years under a tin lid, its black ground unfaded.

They put them together on the altar shelf, beside the child's slipper on its square of silk. It took a moment to do properly. Mei Lan moved the left one a quarter of an inch with one finger, and then again, until the toes were level.

The two of them stood back and looked at the pair.

"Well," said Mei Lan, in the voice she used for closing an account, and got no further; and out in Heeren Street the lamps came on down the whole terrace at once, the way they do, and somebody two doors up rolled a shutter down, and the street went on with its evening. And on the altar shelf in the middle hall of a house bought with silence and paid for with forty years of beadwork, a pair of wedding slippers stood side by side for the first time since a February morning in 1942, toes level, a hand's width apart, in the light.
