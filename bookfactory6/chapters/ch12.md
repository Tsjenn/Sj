# What the Sea Brought

The Japanese barber shut his shop in the last week of July, and Mei Foong saw it happen because Thursday was a mail day and the post office counter looked across the street at his doorway. He had been in Malacca longer than she had — a small, courteous man who kept a caged canary above his mirror and cut half the quarter's hair for thirty cents — and now he stood in his own doorway in his good suit, packing his mirrors into a tea chest, wrapping each one in newspaper whose headlines were about him. Nobody helped him and nobody troubled him. The kopitiam men watched over their cups with the particular Malacca courtesy that consists of seeing everything and remarking nothing, and by Saturday the shop was an empty room with a pale rectangle over the door where his sign had been, and the canary was gone, cage and all, to wherever he was going.

That same week the wireless in the front hall said the word *frozen*. The assets of Japanese subjects, said the calm English voice from Singapore, were frozen — and Mei Foong, who by nineteen had kept books long enough to hear money the way Third Aunt heard a pot about to catch, understood it at once and better than the front hall did. Money that could not move was money that had been declared an enemy. Trade did not taper; it stopped, the way a clock stops. The Towkay read the announcement twice at breakfast, once in English and once in the Chinese paper's plainer language, and said nothing at all, which in that house was itself a kind of headline.

For four years the war had been a thing the sea kept elsewhere. Now the sea began delivering it in instalments. The roads off the river mouth filled that autumn until the anchored ships stood closer together than anyone could remember, and the lighters went out loaded and came back loaded: out with rubber — smoked sheet at prices that made the Towkay's chalkboard look like a fever chart, for the whole world was suddenly buying rubber the way a man buys medicine — and back with cargo that no manifest explained. Coils of barbed wire. Corrugated iron in bundles. Drums and crates stencilled with letters that were not for reading. Soldiers appeared in the town on their days of leave, sunburnt men with wide hats and vowels that swung like hammocks, come from the other side of the world to garrison somebody's certainty, and rumour came in with every tide: the Japanese army had walked into the south of Indochina, which is why the money had been frozen, and Indochina, said anyone who had ever seen a map, was not far the way China was far. Boon Siew stood at the jetty in the evenings checking cargo he no longer needed to check and read the ships the way other men read the racing page. "Real things," he said to her once, quietly, over the manifest, "going to real places. That was always my favourite part. It has stopped being my favourite part."

The drills stopped being funny in October. The year before, when the wardens had first come round about lights, the street had treated the exercise as free theatre — whole families out on the five-foot way to watch the warden argue with old Madam Chee about her altar lamp, boys running behind the volunteers on the padang and drilling with broomsticks in mockery so exact it was almost respect. This year the gummed paper went onto the windows in neat crosses without being asked for twice, and when the practice signal sounded and the town put itself out light by light, nobody brought chairs to watch. Mei Foong stood in the black kitchen court during the October blackout with the household breathing around her, and above the roofline the sky over the strait was enormous and unhelpful, and from three houses down came the sound of a neighbour's baby crying and being hushed, and nobody laughed anywhere on the street, and that — the absence of one boy laughing — frightened her more than the siren had.

There was no morning on which the Towkay announced that his son had been right. Belief came to him the way water comes into a boat, seam by seam, unconfessed. He laughed less at the wireless; then he stopped laughing at it; then he began to ask Mei Foong questions at the account table that had no business in the accounts. How much rice did the house use in a month, exactly, cooked and uncooked? How long did a picul of it keep in this climate, and what spoiled it, and what stopped the spoiling? What stores did the firm hold that could not be eaten, and which of them — say it plainly, girl — could be sold in a bad year for more than money?

Then, on an evening in November, he summoned her to the account room alone, shut the door, and sat down opposite her, which he had never once done in seven years, and laid his hands flat on the table like a man showing he held no cards.

"Prices are at the top," he said. "A man sells at the top and puts something by. That is all this is." Both of them heard what it was not, and neither said so; a man who cannot say *my son was right* says *prudence*, and prudence that year worked eighteen hours a day. "Something will be put by. Rice at the godown. Certain other things here, in the house. It will be done quietly, by different hands, and no one pair of hands will do all of it — the kitchen will know one part, the Bibik another, the estate men a third. No one will hold the whole." He looked at her then the way he looked at a column that had come out right against his expectation, twice. "Except you. You are never wrong, and you are not seen. You will keep the record. If a thing is stored and not written, it is lost; if it is written and found, it is a rope. So it will be written in a way that is not worth finding, and kept in a place that is not worth searching, and you will decide both, and you will not tell me what you decide. What I do not know, no one can ask me for." He paused, and finished the way he closed a year: "The whole is yours, Miss Ho."

She bought the book herself, the next mail day, from the stationer two doors from the post office: a school exercise book, the cheapest kind, soft blue covers gone furry at the corners in the shop's damp, ruled feint, a multiplication table printed on the back as though arithmetic were still a thing children needed saving from. From the front, in pencil, she filled eleven pages with what any inspector of anything would expect from a servant girl who kept the kitchen's sums — kueh costings, bead stock, thread bought and thread used, the price of eggs across three months. From the back, upside down and inward, in her small upright hand, in ink, she kept the truth. No dollars, no names. Weights and places. *West room, third mattress, seam nearest wall: eight.* *Long bolster, Bibik's chamber: twelve.* *Godown, behind: rice, forty-one sacks; salt, four; matches, thirty gross; kerosene, nine tins; quinine, in the flour drum, six hundred tablets.* Two hands ruled toward each other from opposite ends of the same book, the lie walking forward, the truth walking backward, and between them thirty blank pages like a frontier.

The wall went up at the godown across three nights at the turn of the month. Two carpenters were fetched down from one of the upcountry estates, men who had built curing sheds and asked no questions of anyone who paid in notes, and they took the godown's back bay — a blind recess between brick piers that had held nothing but broken dunnage since the flood year — and planked it shut, studs and tongue-and-groove, a wall where no wall had been. Boon Siew and Mei Foong aged it themselves after the carpenters had gone home to Muar: rubbed the raw planks with ash and lamp-black and river mud, nailed the old broken shelving back across it, stacked the same dusty rubbish against it that had leaned against the brick before, and stood back in the lantern light to look at their work — a room that did not exist, breathing rice through its seams. Forty-one sacks lay behind it in the dark, lime-dusted against the weevils, and the smell of them came through faintly, sweetish, like a secret badly kept, and for the first week Mei Foong was certain the whole street could smell it, and the street smelled nothing, because the street was busy with its own seams.

The gold moved in smaller journeys. Twice a week through November the Towkay walked out in the cool of the morning wearing his second-best jacket, unremarkable as a man going to breakfast, and came back from the goldsmiths' street with his pockets no heavier to look at; and in the evenings, behind the shut door of the west room, the Bibik and Ah Yoke sat sewing. They were the two finest needles in the house and they sewed like women at a funeral, without talk, unpicking mattress seams and bolster ends, laying the little taels — each a flat tongue of gold no bigger than two fingers, a bit more than an ounce of everything the firm had spent thirty years becoming — in rows along the kapok, and closing them in again with stitches nobody living would notice. Once, only once, Mei Foong heard the Bibik speak over the work. "I sewed pearls into Bee Nio's wedding quilt," she said, to the seam, "for luck." And Ah Yoke, thimble ticking, answered the only way that house answered anything: "Different luck now. Sew."

The book Mei Foong kept as Ah Yoke had taught her without ever meaning to teach it — she had been awake, years ago, pretending sleep, while a bodkin unpicked a waistband in the moonlight, and she had learned then where women keep the things that keep them. She sewed the exercise book flat into her own quilt, below the fold where a quilt is doubled at the foot, and unpicked it by candle-end once a month for the counting, and sewed it back with thread from three different reels so the seam never looked new. The house of seven doors slept on its gold that December. She slept under the account of it. On bad nights she could feel it there through the kapok, thin and hard along her shin like a splint — the whole, which was hers.

---

Ah Yoke drilled the kitchen court the way she did everything, which is to say completely, and without once explaining why in words longer than the drill itself.

She began with the children, because the children were the part of the court that could be neither carried nor reasoned with: the washerwoman's two grandchildren, the gardeners' three boys, the tally clerk's small daughter who came on Saturdays and was always underfoot by the well. She lined them up by the rice bin and numbered the doors for them — the seven doors of the house that had never in its life needed its doors numbered — and gave each child a door and an adult and a place, and made the whole court walk it slowly, and then walk it quickly, and then do it to her clap. The siren means this. The littlest to Third Aunt, under the big kitchen table, which was teak, two inches of it, older than the Towkay; the boys to the arch by the well, backs to the shaft, hands over heads, and not one inch nearer the well itself or she would settle the Japanese question herself in advance. Whoever was caught near the front of the house ran for door five and the Jonker side lane and the deep drain there, and did not come back for a shoe, a bird, a mother, anything, until the second signal. She drilled them at midday, mid-chore and sulking; she drilled them once out of a dead sleep, standing in the court in the dark clapping like a temple block, and stood over the confusion with a face like a passbook until the smallest gardener's boy arrived last, crying, at the wrong door; and she drilled it again the next day and the next until every child in the court could do it weeping, eating, or half asleep, because, she said — the entire sermon she ever gave on the subject — "Fear is slow. Feet must not be."

The court changed around the drilling all that season, quietly, in the manner of the court. The great water jars were kept filled now to the brim and a zinc pail of sand stood by each door with a paddle in it, and Third Aunt went through her kitchen like a magistrate, condemning the crocks with loose lids, moving the oil where a falling shelf could not find it. The old condensed-milk tin still lived in the rice bin under the scoop — that other war had not ended for the kitchen just because a nearer one was arriving — but Mei Foong noticed that Third Aunt had taken to keeping the bin itself full to the top, and the spare sack behind it, and a third sack behind that, unremarked, unentered in any book of hers; and she noticed, too, that she noticed it professionally now, the way a customs man cannot cross a street without seeing what the street carries. Everyone in Malacca had become a small smuggler of the future. It was the town's new trade, and no one declared it.

Dr. Lim came to dinner in the middle of November with a cloth armband in his jacket pocket and let the Bibik discover it, which was his way of announcing things. He had put his name down with the Medical Auxiliary Service; two evenings a week and Sundays he drilled at the hospital with stretcher parties and splints and the arithmetic of casualties, and came home late smelling of carbolic soap with his spectacles fogged, and taught the household — servants included, at his insistence, the whole court sitting in rows like a strange school — the triangular bandage, the pressure point, the way to carry a person heavier than yourself. He still made jokes, but they had grown smaller, and he administered them the way he administered quinine, in measured doses, for prevention. "The stretcher is the easy part," he told his row of amahs and gardeners, kindly. "You will all be surprised how light a person is when it matters."

It was at that same table, a week later, that the Towkay announced Bee Nio would go to Ayer Keroh.

He announced it as a thing arranged, because that was the only way he knew how to give a gift: the country house at Ayer Keroh, the wooden bungalow up on its piers among the young rubber he had bought in the year of the slippers, a dozen miles inland behind the town — a healthy distance from any harbour, any target, any noise; quiet air for a woman four months gone with the family's first grandchild; an amah to go with her; the syce to drive Dr. Lim out on Sundays. Dr. Lim, appealed to, polished his spectacles and said mildly that the air at Ayer Keroh was certainly quieter. The Bibik was already ordering the packing in her head; Mei Foong could see the lists forming behind her eyes.

"No," said Bee Nio.

She said it while examining her own fingernails, in the voice she used for returning a length of cloth, and the table mistook it at first for the opening bid of a negotiation, because Bee Nio's noes had always been the beginning of her yeses. The Towkay explained the arrangement again, more slowly, as to a customer. Bee Nio let him finish. Then she set her hands flat on the tabletop — and Mei Foong, standing at the sideboard with the ladle, saw with a small shock whose gesture it was: his own, the flat hands, the no-cards — and said: "You are all very anxious to wrap me in cotton wool and post me upcountry like a vase. I am not a vase. My husband will spend his nights carrying other people's families out of whatever comes, and he will do it better if his own family is where he can see it. I stay where he works. The vase can go to Ayer Keroh." And then, because she was still Bee Nio, she added: "Besides, the country house smells of latex and lamp oil and the mosquitoes there have no respect for anyone's condition. If I am to be uncomfortable I will be uncomfortable on Heeren Street, where the kueh is better."

The Towkay tried twice more in the following days and discovered what the house discovered with him, with a certain awe: that his daughter, who could be moved by a ribbon, could not be moved by an argument; that under seven layers of vanity, laid down over twenty years like lacquer, there was teak. It was the Bibik who ended it, unexpectedly, over the sewing one evening: "Leave her. A woman who will not be sent away is not a fool. She is her grandmother." Nothing further was said. The bungalow at Ayer Keroh stayed empty that December, swept weekly by the estate watchman's wife, waiting — though no one alive yet knew for whom.

The first days of December were monsoon days, the rain arriving each afternoon like a creditor, and it was the rain that gave them the hour, because the monthly count fell due on a Thursday and by the time Mei Foong had finished the salt the sky over the river had come down to the rooftops.

They did the count together now; that too had arranged itself without being decided. She could not carry the lantern and the lamp-black rag and shift dunnage alone, and of the three people who knew the wall was a wall, two were the Towkay and his son, and the Towkay did not carry things. So on count days Boon Siew came down to the godown at the shut of business, and they moved the broken shelving in the practised order, and drew the two loose boards, and stood the lantern on the rice, and she read the book backward while he touched each thing the book named — forty-one sacks, four of salt, the matches, the kerosene, the quinine in the flour drum — because Ah Yoke's law ran here too: a count is eyes and hands, never eyes alone.

The rain came in over the river while they were at the kerosene, first as a greyness in the high louvres, then all at once, a monsoon burst on a corrugated roof, which is not a sound so much as a ceiling of sound, sealing the godown under it like a lid coming down. Going out in it was not to be thought of; the lane outside would be a foot of moving water inside ten minutes. Boon Siew turned up the lantern. Mei Foong sat down on the rice where the book made her sit to write, and he sat on the salt, across the little space, in the room that did not exist, and for a while they let the roof roar and said nothing, and it was the old good silence, the margin silence, wide enough for two.

Then he said, "Ah Foong. I want to read you an account."

She knew from the first three words that it was not the godown's. She put the exercise book face down on her knee, which was as close as she could come to stopping her own ears, and he laid it out exactly as she had taught him without meaning to teach it — worst number first.

"After the New Year I am going to my father. I will tell him the Ipoh matter is ended. I will write to Siew Ean myself, before anyone else can, because she asked me for one thing only, which was not to be lied to, and ending it is the truth arriving late. The gifts go back with the letter. That is the honourable half, and it is the cheap half." The lantern hissed; the roof roared; he kept his voice at the level of the count. "Then the expensive half. He will disown me. Perhaps not in the first hour — in the first hour he will think it is a fever, or a girl, or schoolboy talk. When he understands that it is none of those, he will do it properly, because he does everything properly. The firm, the house, the name — struck off, all of it. I have done that arithmetic all year and it always comes to the same total, and I am telling you the total before anything else, because you keep the books, and I will not come to you with the cost hidden."

"Boon Siew—"

"Singapore," he said, over her, gently, the only time in his life he ever talked over her. "There is not a shipping office on that island that would not take a man who can read a manifest and knows every hull in the Malacca roads by her waterline. I have my own two hundred and eleven dollars, which is not money, but it is a floor. Rooms above a shop somewhere off the quays. Two people who can both keep books do not starve, Ah Foong; it is the one thing bookkeepers are promised." And then, because the whole speech had been engineering and this was the load it carried: "If it comes to disowning, I will go, and I am asking you to come. Not as anything hidden. As my wife."

The rain hit the roof in a long gust and eased, hit and eased, like something trying doors.

Mei Foong looked at her own hands on the blue exercise book and made herself do what she did with every account that mattered: read it twice before answering, once for what it said, once for what it left out. Nineteen years old; and inside her, word-perfect, in Ah Yoke's exact order with the stops in Ah Yoke's exact places, sat the story of the two sisters, the one who stayed and the one who ran, the bundle, the ring, the word *no* looked at like a door — a story with its ending torn off, which was itself a kind of ending, if you had ears.

"No," she said. And then, because he had earned the whole of it and not the coin of it: "Hear which no it is before your face does that. I am not saying I do not — " and she stopped, and made herself solvent, and paid it out plainly, the thing four years of margins had said in code: "I have loved you since the bicycle. Since before I knew what the word was for. There. It is said, and I will not fold it away again, and it changes nothing, because it was never the missing figure. The missing figure is this: I have stood on that hill above the town every Cheng Beng for seven years, behind a family that is not mine, and I know what it is when a name comes off an altar. They will not just shut the door on you. They will take you off the tablets. Your grandfather, your sons, the whole line before and behind — cut, at you, like a thread. I came out of a country where whole families ended in a season, and I was carried out alive because one stubborn woman decided I would be, and I will not — " her voice held; she made it hold — "I will not be the knife your family is cut with. Not for rooms over a quay. Not for me at any price, because I have added myself up, Boon Siew, I do it yearly, and I do not come to that much."

"You come to more than the entire ledger," he said, "and this once your figures are wrong." He leaned forward on the salt sacks, elbows on knees, and in the lantern light his face was his father's for a moment, the closing-a-year face, and then entirely his own. "You are pricing the wrong asset. You count what I lose: house, firm, tablets, name. Everything on that list came to me the way weather comes — I did nothing to earn it and can do nothing to deserve it; it is inheritance, it is rain. But family — " and he said it the way he had once said *we are the larder*, quietly, with the whole of himself behind it, so that she knew it had been assembled over years and was only now being launched — "family is what you build, not what you inherit. Ah Yoke is more your family than any tablet on that altar is mine, and no one gave her to you; she was built, day over day, comb-stroke over comb-stroke. What I inherit, my father can cut. What I build, with you, no one alive can cut, and I would rather one room of built than seven doors of inherited. That is my whole case. I have no other."

The rain filled the silence for a long time, softer now, the gusts further apart, the creditor tiring.

"It is a good case," Mei Foong said at last, and her voice was not quite steady, and she let it not be. "It is the best case I have ever heard, and I would hear it argued for fifty years, and I am not accepting it tonight. Because you are still promised, and your father still trusts you, and both of those are true until the New Year, and I will not take delivery of you a minute before you are honestly mine to take. Everything I have ever had in this house was borrowed, or passed down, or paid for by somebody else's years. Not this. This one thing arrives honest and whole and owing nothing, or it does not arrive." She looked up from the book and met him. "So: after the New Year, you will do what you will do — I cannot stop you, and I am not sure I want to, and God forgive me for that. Until then, nothing is said, nothing is touched, nothing is stolen. The sea is bringing something, Boon Siew. Everyone in this town can feel it; your father is sleeping on gold because of it. Let it come. When it has come, we will know what everything costs, and we will settle then, at true prices."

He was quiet. Then he stood, and crossed the little space between the rice and the salt — one step, it was one step — and she watched him come the way she had watched the cage door swing open once, an inch of open dark, and she stood up too, into it, wanting it more than she had wanted any single thing in nineteen years of wanting carefully.

And stepped back.

It was her choice; she made it with her whole body, one clean pace back against the rice, and held up between them, in both hands, the blue exercise book — the one book they kept together that had no margins in it, nothing scribbled sideways, no birds, no weather, nothing but the counted truth — and he looked at it, and at her over it, and understood her exactly, as he always had, all the way down.

"Entered," he said. Not lightly. He took the lantern instead of her hand.

"Carried forward," said Mei Foong.

They finished the count aloud, both voices level, forty-one sacks and the matches and the quinine, and closed the boards, and moved the shelving back in the practised order; and when the rain had thinned to dripping they went out separately into the washed lane, ten minutes apart, the amah's girl first with the book already against her spine under her blouse, where it rode home to its seam.

On the Monday the house woke before it was waked.

Afterwards nobody could rightly say what had come first — the thud, felt more than heard, far off down the world to the south, that might have been thunder and was not; or the telephone ringing in the front hall in the dark, which had never once happened before six in any year of the house's life; or simply the Towkay's tread on the stairs, faster than his tread had been in a decade. By the time Mei Foong reached the front hall with the kitchen behind her, the walnut wireless was warming, its dial-light the only light, and the household was assembling around it out of the dark in the order of no order at all — the Bibik with her hair down her back, grey and astonishing, a woman nobody living had seen unpinned; Bee Nio with a shawl over her nightdress and one hand already resting, flat and still, where the child was; Dr. Lim beside her with his spectacles on and his jacket over his pyjamas and the armband, Mei Foong saw, already through his fingers like a rosary; Third Aunt with the rice scoop still in her hand, having stopped mid-task at a bin she had been filling in the dark, which meant she too had been awake; Ah Yoke at the back where the servants stood, making, with her eyes, in the gloom, a count of children.

The set warmed. The voice, when it came up out of the hum, was the same calm Singapore English that had said *frozen* in July, reading slowly, as if dictation speed were a kindness; and it said that in the early hours of the morning enemy landings had been attempted on the coast of northern Malaya; that fighting was in progress at Kota Bharu, in Kelantan, on the north-eastern shore; that the situation was receiving attention; that further announcements would follow. Then it said other things, and nobody in the room could afterwards remember what they were.

Kota Bharu. Mei Foong stood among the household with the name going through her like cold water. She had checked it once in the atlas Boon Siew left where she could reach it, years ago, tracing the country she lived in and did not know: the far top corner, rice country, the other sea. The war had not crossed the English sea to fight the English fleet after all. It had come the other way, out of the dark, onto a beach, in the rain — by water, at night, like everything that year: the whole season's cargo arriving at once, declared at last.

No one moved. Seven doors' worth of house stood silent around one lit dial, the Towkay nearest it, both hands flat on the cabinet top, head bowed to it as if to a well he was listening down; and he did not look at his son, and his son did not look at him, and neither of them said the thing that stood in the room with them, because it had stopped mattering who had been right. Across the crowded dark Boon Siew found her — the amah's girl, at the back, beside Ah Yoke — and they looked at each other the width of the front hall, and what passed between them was the whole argument of their lives arrived at its examination: the cage, the open door, the cat over the wall; *let it come; we will settle at true prices.*

Outside, above the kitchen court, the sky was going grey. Nobody had fed the merbok yet; its cover was still on; it could not have known anything. In the first light, over the silent house, it began to sing anyway, because it could do nothing else.
