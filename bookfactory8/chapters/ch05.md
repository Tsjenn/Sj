# Reading a Company With No Accounts

Look up Nvidia's last quarter and you will find a document, filed on a
date, signed by directors, checked by an audit firm, presented in the same
line-item order as the quarter before it. Look up OpenAI's last quarter
and you will find a headline. The headline may be accurate. It is not a
filing, and the difference between those two things is the subject of this
chapter.

Two of the companies that matter most to the model layer of this stack —
OpenAI and Anthropic — publish no audited accounts. They are not obliged
to. Neither is Stargate, nor most of the neoclouds' private peers, nor the
application startups in Part Five. A book that reads the accounts has to
say plainly what it does when there are none, and then apply that standard
without exception. Otherwise the discipline is decorative.

The short version of the standard: report what is disclosed, name who
disclosed it and when, and never let a reported number sit in the same
sentence as an audited one without saying which is which.

## What a filed set of accounts actually buys you

It is worth being precise about what you lose when a company is private,
because the loss is specific rather than general.

A listed US issuer files a 10-K once a year and a 10-Q each quarter. Those
documents are audited (the annual one) or reviewed (the quarterly ones) by
an independent firm which signs its name and carries liability for the
opinion. They follow a prescribed order. Revenue is recognised under a
written standard, ASC 606 in the United States or IFRS 15 elsewhere, which
tells you when a sale counts and when it does not. Segments are broken out
according to how management actually runs the business, not according to
what flatters it. Related-party transactions get a note. Customer
concentration gets a note. Commitments and contingencies get a note. The
comparatives from last year sit alongside, in the same columns, so you can
see the direction of travel without doing arithmetic yourself.

None of that is charity. It is a bargain: you may sell shares to the
public, and in exchange the public gets to see the books on a timetable it
does not have to ask for.

A private company has made no such bargain. What you get instead is
whatever the company chooses to say, whenever it chooses to say it, in
whatever form it likes, to whichever audience it selects. That is not
dishonesty. It is the absence of an obligation.

## Why these companies have no accounts, and why that is unremarkable

There is a persistent assumption that a company as large as OpenAI or
Anthropic must be legally required to publish something by now. It is not.

Capital is raised privately under exemptions from registration —
Regulation D under the Securities Act of 1933 being the workhorse. A
company selling shares under Rule 506 files a Form D with the SEC, usually
within fifteen days of the first sale. Read one. It gives you the issuer's
name and address, the exemption claimed, the type of security, the total
offering amount and the amount sold. It gives you no revenue, no profit,
no balance sheet, no auditor. It is a notice, not a report.

The other threshold people expect to bite is Section 12(g) of the
Securities Exchange Act, which forces registration on a company that grows
too widely held. The JOBS Act of 2012 raised that threshold from five
hundred holders of record to two thousand — or five hundred who are not
accredited investors — alongside total assets above ten million dollars,
and excluded shares held through employee compensation plans from the
count. That last exclusion matters more than the number. A company can
have thousands of employees holding equity and still sit comfortably below
a threshold counted in holders of record, because those employees do not
count and because institutional investors hold through a small number of
record entities.

So a company can now be worth more than most of the constituents of a
national index, employ thousands of people, sign contracts with
governments, and file nothing that resembles accounts. This is a
structural feature of modern capital markets, not a loophole any
particular company invented.

## What a private valuation actually is

Here is the sentence to keep: a private valuation is the price of the last
shares sold, multiplied by all the shares, on terms you cannot see.

Take the two most-quoted figures in this industry. Anthropic closed a
sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five
billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable
private company in the world, as reported by CNBC in May 2026. OpenAI
closed a hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round in March 2026 at an
eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, with Amazon reported
as investing fifty billion dollars in that round, per valueaddvc and
Forbes reporting in 2026.

Both figures are real in the sense that a round happened at a price. Both
are widely misread. Consider what the arithmetic actually does.

Take an illustrative company, with round numbers chosen for clarity and
belonging to no real company. It has ninety million shares outstanding
before the round. A new investor buys ten million newly issued preferred
shares at ten dollars each, putting in a hundred million dollars. The
post-money valuation is ten dollars multiplied by the hundred million
shares now outstanding: one billion dollars.

Notice what just happened. A hundred million dollars of actual cash,
negotiated between one buyer and one seller, has set the notional price of
nine hundred million dollars of shares that nobody bought, nobody sold and
nobody bid for. In a listed company the marginal trade also sets the
price, but that marginal trade happens thousands of times a day, in both
directions, by strangers with opposing views, and anyone who disagrees can
sell. Here it happened once, in a negotiation, between parties with a
shared interest in the number being high.

Post-money valuation is therefore an arithmetic convention. It is a useful
one — it is how ownership percentages are calculated — but it answers the
question "what price did the newest shares change hands at" and not the
question "what is this company worth".

## The terms you cannot see

The convention has a second and larger problem. It assumes every share is
worth the same as the newest one. They are not, because the newest shares
usually come with protections the older ones lack.

The academic work on this is settled enough to cite. Will Gornall and Ilya
Strebulaev, in "Squaring Venture Capital Valuations with Reality",
published in the Journal of Financial Economics in January 2020, valued
each share class of a sample of unicorns separately rather than assuming
uniformity. They found reported post-money valuations averaged forty-eight
per cent above fair value, with fourteen companies more than a hundred per
cent above. Common shares — the ones founders and employees hold — were
fifty-six per cent overvalued. In their sample, sixty-five of a hundred
and thirty-five companies lost unicorn status once the terms were priced.
They also counted how often specific protections appeared: IPO return
guarantees in fifteen per cent of cases, vetoes over a down-round IPO in
twenty-four per cent, and seniority over all other investors in thirty per
cent.

Those three terms are worth understanding individually, because they
recur.

**Liquidation preference.** The commonest term. A preferred investor gets
its money back before common shareholders get anything. A one-times
non-participating preference means the investor takes the greater of its
money back or its pro-rata share. It sounds mild. It is not, at low exit
values.

Take an illustrative company again, round numbers, no real company. It has
raised two hundred million dollars in total, all preferred with a
one-times non-participating preference. The preferred holders own forty
per cent on an as-converted basis; founders and employees hold sixty per
cent in common. The last round priced it at one billion dollars
post-money. Here is what each class actually receives at different exit
values:

| Exit value | Preferred receives | Common receives | Common as % of "its" 60% of $1bn |
|---|---|---|---|
| $3,000m | $1,200m (converts) | $1,800m | 300% |
| $1,000m | $400m (converts) | $600m | 100% |
| $500m | $200m (preference) | $300m | 50% |
| $250m | $200m (preference) | $50m | 8% |
| $200m | $200m (preference) | $0 | 0% |
| $150m | $150m (preference) | $0 | 0% |

The post-money valuation says the common is worth six hundred million
dollars. At an exit of two hundred million it is worth nothing at all. The
preferred, meanwhile, is protected across the whole lower half of the
table. Multiplying every share by the preferred price treats those two
very different instruments as identical. They are not identical, and the
gap between them is exactly the amount by which post-money overstates
worth.

**Ratchets and IPO guarantees.** Some rounds promise the investor a
minimum outcome at IPO. Illustratively: an investor puts in a hundred
million dollars and is guaranteed shares worth at least a hundred and
fifteen million at listing. If the company lists at a price that would
value the stake below that, the investor receives additional shares to
make up the difference. The company's headline valuation is unaffected.
The common shareholders absorb the dilution. A ratchet is, in effect, a
put option written by the employees in favour of the last investor, and it
never appears in the number the press prints.

**Seniority.** Where preferences stack rather than sharing pro rata, the
newest investor is paid first, then the round before, and so on. The
headline number is again unaffected. The distribution of any given exit
value is transformed.

None of this implies anything improper about any company named in this
book. These are standard commercial terms, negotiated by sophisticated
parties, disclosed to the people who need to see them. The point is
narrower and entirely practical: the headline valuation is computed as
though these terms do not exist, and for private companies you generally
cannot see whether they do.

## The same company, two prices, on the same day

There is a tell available even without seeing the term sheet, and it is
one an accountant will recognise immediately: the company itself carries
two different prices for its own shares at the same moment.

US private companies must set a fair market value for their common stock
in order to price employee share options — the so-called 409A valuation,
after the section of the tax code. It is prepared by an independent
valuation firm and refreshed periodically. It values the common stock,
which carries none of the preferences described above and cannot easily be
sold.

It comes out lower. Valuation practitioners describe the common price as
typically running at a discount of roughly twenty to forty per cent to the
preferred price at later stages, with wider gaps earlier and narrower gaps
as a listing approaches, with a discount for lack of marketability
commonly in the twenty-five to thirty-five per cent range (409A valuation
practitioner guidance, 2026; these are professional rules of thumb, not
disclosed figures for any company in this book).

Two independent processes, running inside the same company in the same
week, produce two different prices per share. Both are defensible. They
answer different questions. Only one of them gets printed.

## Reading a revenue run-rate nobody has audited

Valuation is the number people argue about. Run-rate is the number that
does the persuading, and it deserves more scepticism than it gets.

An annualised revenue run-rate is a single recent period multiplied up: the
latest month times twelve, or the latest quarter times four. It is not
revenue. It is not a forecast in any disciplined sense. It is a
measurement of one period, extrapolated.

The reported figures for the two companies in question have moved fast
enough that the date on each one is not a formality:

| Company | Reported run-rate | As at | Reported by |
|---|---|---|---|
| OpenAI | $20bn | end of 2025 | Bloomberg, August 2026 |
| OpenAI | $25bn | end of February 2026 | 2026 reporting |
| OpenAI | over $40bn | July 2026 | Bloomberg, 13 August 2026 |
| Anthropic | $9bn | end of 2025 | CNBC, 17 August 2026 |
| Anthropic | $47bn | May 2026 | 2026 reporting |
| Anthropic | $65bn | end of July 2026 | CNBC / Axios / Bloomberg, 17 August 2026 |

Read the right-hand column before the left. Not one of these numbers comes
from a filing. The Anthropic figures were, per CNBC's report of 17 August
2026, told by the company to its investors — which makes them a company
statement to a private audience, relayed by a journalist. That is a
perfectly good source. It is a different kind of source from a signed
10-Q, and the difference should never be smoothed away by writing "revenue
of sixty-five billion dollars" as though it had been filed.

CNBC also reported on 17 August 2026 that Anthropic's investors expected
growth to continue at roughly the same rate through the remainder of the
year, finishing 2026 somewhere between a hundred and a hundred and twenty
billion dollars. Note what that is: an expectation held by investors,
reported by a journalist. It is three steps from an audited figure, and
this book will not treat it as one.

When you meet a run-rate anywhere, the questions to put to it are these.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Which period was annualised? | A single strong month times twelve is a very different claim from a quarter times four. |
| Gross or net? | Revenue booked through a partner, or with a revenue share attached, can be presented either way. Under ASC 606 the gross-versus-net question turns on whether the company is principal or agent. Nobody applies that test to a run-rate. |
| Contracted or delivered? | Committed multi-year contracts are not the same as consumption already served. |
| Are credits counted? | Compute provided in exchange for equity or credits, and services billed against those credits, can circulate without cash changing hands in the ordinary way. |
| Who said it, to whom? | A statement to investors, a statement to the press, and a statement in a filing carry different consequences for the person making it. |
| What was the churn? | A run-rate is a snapshot with no retention information in it at all. |

None of those questions accuse anyone of anything. They are the questions
an auditor would have to answer before signing, and the fact that nobody
has answered them is the entire content of the word "unaudited".

## What stays invisible even when a run-rate is published

Revenue is the figure private companies volunteer, because it is the one
that flatters. Everything an accountant would want alongside it stays
dark, and the list is worth setting out explicitly, because a reader who
has only a run-rate should know the size of what is missing.

There is no gross margin. For a model company the dominant cost is
compute, and whether a dollar of revenue arrives with thirty cents or
eighty cents of inference cost attached is the difference between a
business and a subsidy. Nothing in a run-rate tells you which.

There is no cash position and no runway. A company can raise sixty-five
billion dollars and still be consuming cash faster than any revenue line
suggests, and the only external evidence of the burn is the sort of
equity-method disclosure discussed below.

There are no purchase commitments. Multi-year compute contracts, data
centre leases and chip supply agreements would appear in a listed
company's commitments note, often running to tens of billions and often
non-cancellable. Privately, they appear nowhere, and a commitment is an
obligation whether or not anyone has written it down for you.

There is no deferred revenue, which for an enterprise business is the
single most useful forward indicator there is. There is no share-based
compensation charge, which for a company competing for research staff is
likely to be large, and which sits between a company's stated margins and
its real ones. There is no customer concentration disclosure, so you
cannot tell whether a run-rate rests on ten thousand customers or on
three. And there is no related-party note, which matters most precisely
where the investors are also the suppliers and, in some cases, the
customers — the structure examined in Part Six.

The correct posture is not suspicion. It is proportion: a published
run-rate is one line from an income statement that has perhaps forty
lines, and it is the line chosen by the company. Treat it as one data
point, name its source, and resist the temptation to reason from it to
anything it cannot support.

## The audited window: reading a private company through someone else's filings

Here is where the accountant's angle earns its keep. You cannot read
OpenAI's or Anthropic's accounts. You can read the accounts of the listed
companies that own pieces of them, and those companies are obliged to say
things.

There are two mechanisms, and they behave very differently.

**The equity method.** Where a holder has significant influence, it
records its share of the investee's profit or loss in its own income
statement. That single line is a window. In its quarterly filing published
on 29 October 2025, Microsoft recorded a reduction to net income for its
share of OpenAI's losses; The Register, reporting on 29 October 2025,
noted the figure as 3.1 billion dollars net of tax and 4.1 billion before
tax, and inferred from Microsoft's stake an OpenAI quarterly loss of
something above 11.5 billion dollars. Microsoft's stake was set at
twenty-seven per cent when OpenAI completed its restructuring into OpenAI
Group PBC on 28 October 2025, a stake reported at the time as worth about
135 billion dollars, with the agreement valuing OpenAI at 500 billion
dollars and technology rights running to 2032 (Fortune, Al Jazeera and
others, 28 October 2025).

That inference is worth pausing on, because it demonstrates the method and
its limits at once. The disclosed number is Microsoft's share. The
investee's total loss is derived by dividing by the stake. The derivation
requires assumptions — that the stake used for accounting equals the
headline stake, that the period aligns, that no other adjustments sit in
the line. Divide by the wrong denominator and you are out by billions.
This book will report the disclosed figure and name it as such, and will
say when a number is a press inference rather than a disclosure.

**Fair value through profit or loss.** Where the holder does not have
significant influence, the rules take a different route. Under the US
standard introduced by ASU 2016-01, an investor marks minority equity
stakes to fair value and runs the change through net income. For a private
investee, "fair value" is anchored to observable price changes — which in
practice means the last round.

Follow that chain. A privately negotiated round price, unaudited and
unfiled, becomes the input to a fair value measurement, which flows
through the audited income statement of a listed company, which is then
read by index funds, analysts and the wider market as earnings.

The size of the effect in 2026 was not marginal. Alphabet's first-quarter
2026 results, released on 29 April 2026, reported other income reflecting
a net gain of 37.7 billion dollars, primarily from net unrealised gains on
non-marketable equity securities; the release stated that the net effect
of the gain on equity securities of 36.9 billion dollars increased the tax
provision, net income and diluted earnings per share by 8.2 billion
dollars, 28.7 billion dollars and 2.35 dollars respectively, with net
income up eighty-one per cent to 62.6 billion dollars on revenues of 109.9
billion dollars. Alphabet subsequently disclosed in a July 2026 filing
that its Anthropic stake stood at roughly 124.3 billion dollars as at 30
June 2026. Amazon's second-quarter 2026 results, released in July 2026,
included non-operating pre-tax income of 53.4 billion dollars, primarily
from its investments in Anthropic, as reported by Variety and others in
July 2026. Fortune reported on 30 April 2026 that roughly half of Google's
and Amazon's reported profits for the period came from the Anthropic
stake rather than from operations, and CNBC reported in August 2026 that
Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had been distorting the
corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them.

Note carefully what has and has not been said. The accounting is correct
and mandatory; Alphabet and Amazon had no discretion to omit these gains,
and both flagged them clearly. No impropriety is alleged or implied. What
the reader should take is structural: a large slice of two of the world's
biggest reported profit numbers in 2026 derived from a valuation that no
auditor tested, on a company that publishes nothing, arrived at in a
private negotiation. The gains are unrealised and non-cash, and the same
mechanism runs in reverse if the next round prices lower.

The practical instruction is simple. When you read the earnings of a
company that holds a large private stake, find the line and take it out
before you form a view of the operating business. Alphabet's own release
showed the reader how, by giving the per-share effect separately. Most of
the useful information in a set of accounts is in the reconciliations.

## Three prices for the same company

Pulling the threads together, a private company of this kind carries at
least three simultaneous prices, and they answer three different
questions.

| Price | What it is | What question it answers | Who produces it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Post-money valuation | Last preferred price × all shares | What did the newest shares cost? | The company and its newest investor |
| 409A common fair value | Independent valuation of common stock | What is an employee's option worth for tax purposes? | An independent valuation firm |
| Holder's carrying value | Fair value in a listed holder's accounts | What must a shareholder book under accounting rules? | The holder, inside audited accounts |

A fourth exists where employees are allowed to sell in a tender offer or
on a secondary platform. Those prices are quoted, but they are thin, often
apply to common rather than preferred, are frequently subject to transfer
restrictions and company consent, and can trade at either a discount or a
premium to the headline. They are evidence. They are not a market price in
the sense that a listed quotation is.

When a headline says a private company "is worth" a number, it almost
always means the first row of that table. It is the least informative of
the three about the economics of the business, and the only one produced
by parties with a shared interest in it being large.

## What changes when the filing arrives

The information gap is not permanent. Anthropic confidentially submitted a
draft registration statement — a draft S-1 — to the SEC, announced by the
company on 1 June 2026 and reported by CNBC the same day. A confidential
submission means the document is not publicly available on EDGAR; the
detailed financials remain private until the company chooses to make the
filing public. Bloomberg reported on 13 August 2026 on OpenAI's run-rate
in the context of an anticipated listing, and on 17 August 2026 on
Anthropic's.

This book makes no prediction about whether, when, or at what price any
company lists. That would be forecasting a share price, which it does not
do. What can be said factually is what a listing would change: an S-1
carries audited financial statements, an auditor's opinion, a risk-factor
section written by lawyers who are liable for it, related-party notes,
customer concentration disclosure, and a capitalisation table showing
every share class and its preferences. The moment those documents become
public, most of this chapter stops being necessary for the company
concerned — and a great many confident statements made during the private
years will become checkable for the first time.

That is a reason to be careful now rather than a reason to wait. Numbers
written today are read against filings published later.

## The standard this book applies

From here on, whenever a private company appears in these pages, the
following rules hold without exception.

Every figure carries its date and its source in the sentence itself. Not a
footnote, not a bibliography — the sentence. If the source is a journalist
reporting what a company told investors, the sentence says so. If it is a
company statement, the sentence says so. If it is a filing, the sentence
says which filing.

Reported figures are never presented as equivalent to filed ones. The word
"revenue" without qualification is reserved for audited revenue. Anything
else is a "reported run-rate", an "annualised run-rate as told to
investors", or whatever it actually is.

Post-money valuations are described as what they are — the price of the
last round — and never as what a company is worth. Where a valuation is
quoted, the round, the date and the reporter are quoted with it.

Inferences are labelled as inferences. Where a private company's numbers
are derived from a public holder's disclosure, the disclosed number is
given first and the derivation shown, so the reader can disagree with the
arithmetic.

Nothing here is investment advice. This book gives no price targets, makes
no recommendation to buy or sell any security, and does not predict share
prices. Its ambition is narrower and more durable: that a reader who
finishes it can pick up a filing, or a headline about a company with no
filing, and know which questions to ask.

And every figure in this book is a snapshot. The run-rates above moved
between the writing and the reading. The valuations moved. For a listed
company, the current number is in the latest 10-K or 10-Q on EDGAR, free,
within minutes. For a private company there is no such place to look, and
the honest answer to "what is it now" is that nobody outside the company
knows, including the people quoting the last round.

That asymmetry is not a defect in the reporting. It is the defining
feature of the model layer as it stands in 2026, and the rest of this book
treats it as a fact about the industry rather than a gap to be filled with
confident guesses.
