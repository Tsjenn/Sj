# Microsoft and OpenAI

Somewhere in the related-party pages of Microsoft's fiscal 2026 annual report, filed for the year ended 30 June 2026, sits a sentence that no previous filing contained. Microsoft recorded twenty-four point one billion US dollars of revenue from commercial arrangements with OpenAI, inclusive of revenue-sharing payments, and carried six billion dollars owed by OpenAI as a receivable at the year end, per Neowin's and Techzine's reporting of the filing in 2026. Against total fiscal 2026 revenue of three hundred and thirty-one point eight billion dollars, announced by the company on 29 July 2026, that is roughly seven per cent of everything Microsoft sold, arriving from a single private counterparty that it also part-owns.

That sentence is the reason this chapter exists in a part of the book about ownership rather than in the part about models. The commercial history of these two companies has already been told here as a deal. What follows is narrower and more literal: the arrangement read clause by clause, in the five categories that actually carry money — capital in, compute committed, revenue share, distribution rights, model access — and then an honest accounting of how much of each clause is genuinely public.

Begin with the asymmetry, because everything else follows from it.

## One filer, two parties

Only one side of this relationship files audited accounts. Microsoft is a listed United States registrant and must disclose material related-party arrangements, material commitments and the accounting treatment of significant investments. OpenAI is private. It publishes no audited financial statements, files no annual report, and discloses what it chooses to disclose, when it chooses to.

The practical consequence is that almost everything the public knows about this relationship has reached the public through one of three channels, and they are not of equal quality:

| Channel | What it gives you | How far to trust it |
|---|---|---|
| Microsoft's own filings and results materials | Audited or reviewed figures, prepared under a disclosure regime, with dates | Highest — but shaped by what a filer must say, not by what a reader wants to know |
| Joint announcements by the two companies | The existence and headline shape of each amendment | Accurate as to what was agreed; silent on price, schedule and remedy |
| Press reporting of terms, citing people familiar | Percentages, caps, structures | Useful, uneven, and never a substitute for a document |

The previous chapter set out the general toolkit for reading a cross-holding: size of stake, rights attached, commercial agreement alongside, accounting treatment, and what the whole package obliges each side to do. This relationship is the hardest possible case for that toolkit, because four of the five elements are visible only from one side of the table. Where a figure below comes from a filing, this chapter says so. Where it comes from a journalist's account of a private document, it says that instead, and the reader should weigh the two differently.

## Clause one: capital in

Start with the number everyone quotes and almost nobody defines. Microsoft's investment in OpenAI is generally reported as thirteen billion dollars. That figure is a commitment, not a payment.

Microsoft's own disclosures separate the two. Its fiscal 2026 annual report shows a total commitment of thirteen billion dollars, of which eleven point nine billion had been funded as at 30 June 2026, per the 2026 reporting of that filing. Coverage of the earlier quarterly filings put the funded amount at eleven point six billion as at 30 September 2025 and eleven point eight billion as at 31 March 2026. Set them in a column and the shape of the thing becomes obvious.

| Date | Committed | Funded | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 September 2025 | 13.0 billion dollars | 11.6 billion | Reporting of the quarterly filing, 2025 |
| 31 March 2026 | 13.0 billion dollars | 11.8 billion | Reporting of the quarterly filing, 2026 |
| 30 June 2026 | 13.0 billion dollars | 11.9 billion | Reporting of the fiscal 2026 annual report, 2026 |

Two hundred million dollars over three quarters, into a company that raised one hundred and twenty-two billion dollars from other people in the middle of that period. Whatever the thirteen billion is, it stopped being a live funding line some time ago.

An accountant reads a committed-but-unfunded balance the way a lender reads an undrawn facility. The commitment is a promise to pay on terms; until it is drawn, no cash has moved, and the unfunded portion tells you something about the schedule and the conditions attached. What it does not tell you is whether the missing one point one billion is a milestone yet to be met, a tranche deliberately unspent, or simply a payment sitting on the wrong side of a year end. The filing gives the number; it does not give the reason.

The composition of the funded amount matters even more, and here the record is reported rather than filed. Both the 2019 announcement and the subsequent rounds were described at the time as being delivered over years and in substantial part as Azure compute rather than cash — Semafor reported in November 2023 that a significant portion of the ten billion dollars announced that January was taking the form of compute credits. An investor whose contribution is partly its own product has a different exposure from one that wires money. The cash cost to the supplier is the cost of providing the service, not the face value of the credit, and the credit returns to the supplier as revenue when it is consumed.

None of that is improper, and this book alleges nothing. It is ordinary in software, and it is disclosed in outline. But it changes the arithmetic of "thirteen billion dollars invested" in ways no public document lets an outsider unwind. What is not public: the split between cash and credits; the price at which compute credits were valued when contributed; the instalment schedule; the conditions attached to the remaining commitment; and what, if anything, happens to the unfunded balance if it is never drawn.

What the ownership actually is, by contrast, is clean and dated. On the recapitalisation of 28 October 2025, Microsoft's position converted into conventional equity: roughly twenty-seven per cent of OpenAI Group PBC on an as-converted diluted basis, valued at about one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars at the time, per CNBC's report of the announcement and the companies' own statements. Note the arithmetic that produces the second figure. A twenty-seven per cent stake "valued at" one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars is twenty-seven per cent of a private valuation set by a negotiated round. It is not a market price, and no shares changed hands at it.

## Clause two: compute committed

The second clause is the largest number in the relationship and the least understood.

In the same October 2025 agreement, OpenAI contracted to purchase an incremental two hundred and fifty billion dollars of Azure services, and Microsoft gave up its right of first refusal to be OpenAI's compute provider, per the companies' announcements and the October 2025 reporting by GeekWire and Data Center Dynamics. In April 2026 the arrangement was amended again: Microsoft remains OpenAI's primary cloud partner, with OpenAI products continuing to launch first on Azure unless Microsoft cannot support the required capabilities, while OpenAI became free to serve customers through other cloud providers, per Microsoft's blog post of 27 April 2026 and CNBC's report the same day.

A purchase commitment of that size is not revenue and must not be read as revenue. It is a contractual obligation of the buyer, disclosed by the seller as a pipeline, which converts into revenue only as services are delivered and consumed. Three things determine whether it converts:

The first is time. Two hundred and fifty billion dollars over an unstated number of years is a different business from two hundred and fifty billion dollars over three. The schedule has not been published.

The second is price. A commitment denominated in dollars of services says nothing about the rate card behind it. Committed capacity is generally cheaper than spot; a customer of this scale negotiates. Whether the two hundred and fifty billion buys the same compute a smaller tenant would get for two hundred and fifty billion is not disclosed and, on ordinary commercial practice, is unlikely.

The third is the customer's own funding. A purchase commitment is only as good as the counterparty's ability to pay it, and the counterparty here is a company widely reported to be loss-making at the operating line, funding itself through successive private rounds. This is precisely why the six billion dollar receivable in the fiscal 2026 filing is worth pausing on: it is money already invoiced and not yet collected from a related party whose accounts nobody outside can read.

Take an illustrative example, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. A cloud operator signs a ten-year, one hundred billion dollar commitment with a customer, and books nothing on signature. It then spends twenty billion dollars of capital expenditure building capacity shaped to that customer's workload. If the customer consumes on schedule, the operator earns a return over the asset's life. If the customer consumes half, the operator owns purpose-built capacity it must re-let to tenants who did not ask for it, while depreciating the whole of it. The commitment transferred volume risk on paper; the capital expenditure kept the asset risk at home. That is the shape of every large compute commitment in this industry, and it is why the disclosure to watch is not the headline commitment but the capital expenditure that answers it.

What is not public here: the delivery schedule by year; the pricing; whether the commitment is take-or-pay and on what terms; what happens on termination or change of control; and how much of Microsoft's own capital expenditure is being built specifically against it.

## Clause three: revenue share

The third clause is the one that runs uphill, from the smaller company to the larger.

OpenAI has been reported since 2025 to pay Microsoft twenty per cent of its top-line revenue, with TechCrunch reporting in May 2025, citing The Information, that OpenAI had told investors it expected to reduce the share paid to its business partners, Microsoft included, to around ten per cent by the end of the decade. Running the other way, Microsoft had been paying OpenAI a share of the revenue it earned from reselling OpenAI's models through Azure — an arrangement whose existence became publicly legible mainly at the moment it was cancelled.

The April 2026 amendment changed both legs. Microsoft will no longer pay a revenue share to OpenAI. OpenAI's revenue-share payments to Microsoft continue through 2030 at the same percentage but subject to a total cap, per the companies' announcements of 27 April 2026; CNBC and Forbes reported that cap at thirty-eight billion dollars. Trade coverage carried estimates of the saving to OpenAI relative to an uncapped arrangement; those are third-party models, not disclosed figures, and this book does not repeat them as facts.

Why does a percentage of the top line matter so much? Because of where it sits in the income statement. A revenue share is charged on gross revenue, before the cost of serving that revenue. Figures shared with investors and reported in 2026 by PitchBook and Morningstar put OpenAI's gross margin at roughly thirty-nine per cent in the first quarter of 2026, up from about thirty-three per cent a year earlier — unaudited, and reported rather than filed. Hold those two reported numbers side by side. On a business retaining thirty-nine cents of gross margin per dollar of revenue, a twenty-cent revenue share consumes roughly half of the gross margin before a single researcher, salesperson or data-centre lease has been paid for.

That is the whole reason the cap was worth negotiating, and the reason it is denominated in absolute dollars rather than in a lower percentage. A percentage scales with success; a cap does not. If OpenAI's revenue grows as its investors expect, a thirty-eight billion dollar ceiling reached earlier is a smaller share of a bigger number every year after that. The company converted an open-ended participation in its future revenue into a fixed, finite liability with an end date. From the other chair, Microsoft converted an uncertain long-dated stream into a bounded one and simultaneously stopped an outbound payment. Both sides can reasonably describe that as an improvement, which is usually the sign of an amendment that was going to happen anyway.

Now the disclosure problem, which is instructive. Microsoft's twenty-four point one billion dollars of fiscal 2026 revenue from OpenAI is a single line covering commercial arrangements inclusive of revenue-sharing payments. It is not broken down. Analysts said so publicly at the time — KeyBanc's Jackson Ader was quoted in 2026 coverage of the filing observing that how much of the figure came from cloud services and how much from revenue sharing remained unresolved. That distinction is not pedantry. Cloud revenue is a service sold to a customer who could, after April 2026, buy it elsewhere. Revenue share is a contractual claim on someone else's sales, capped and expiring in 2030. One is a business; the other is an annuity with a stated end. A reader who cannot separate them cannot judge the durability of seven per cent of Microsoft's revenue.

## Clause four: distribution rights

The fourth clause is the one with the least money attached in any single year and, arguably, the most strategic weight.

The original bargain gave Microsoft an exclusive licence to OpenAI's intellectual property for its own products, and gave OpenAI a route into every large enterprise on earth through Microsoft's contracting, compliance and billing. The October 2025 agreement kept that shape while adding exits. Under its terms, OpenAI remained Microsoft's frontier model partner, Microsoft retained exclusive IP rights and Azure API exclusivity until artificial general intelligence, and OpenAI gained three specific new freedoms: to develop some products jointly with third parties, to provide API access to United States government national security customers, and to release open-weight models meeting stated capability criteria, per the companies' announcements of 28 October 2025 and the law-firm and trade summaries of the terms.

The April 2026 amendment removed the exclusivity itself. Microsoft's licence became non-exclusive, and OpenAI became free to sell its products across any cloud, while Microsoft's licence to OpenAI intellectual property for models and products continues through 2032, per the companies' announcements and CNBC's reporting of 27 April 2026.

Read those two paragraphs as a lawyer would and notice what survived. Microsoft did not lose access; it lost the right to deny access to others. Those are very different assets. Exclusivity is worth something only while the counterparty has no alternative and the rival has no substitute — and by 2026 both conditions had failed, from opposite directions. What Microsoft kept is a long-dated licence, running six years from the date of the amendment, over technology it did not develop.

Two features of the distribution clause deserve emphasis because they recur throughout this part of the book.

First, distribution rights are the only element of this relationship that flows in both directions simultaneously. Microsoft distributes OpenAI's models to enterprises; OpenAI's brand distributes Microsoft's cloud to developers. Neither leg is invoiced directly. Both show up, if at all, in someone's revenue line for reasons the disclosure does not attribute.

Second, distribution rights are the element most likely to be renegotiated quietly. A revenue share has a percentage and a cap; a compute commitment has a number. A right to "launch first on Azure unless Microsoft cannot support the required capabilities" is a clause whose operation depends entirely on a judgement neither party will publish. Whether it binds in practice will be visible only in behaviour — which products appear where, and when — and not in any document.

## Clause five: model access, and the clause about the end of the world

The fifth clause is the strangest thing in modern commercial drafting, and it is worth setting out precisely because it is so often described loosely.

Microsoft's rights were tied from the early agreements to the arrival of artificial general intelligence, a term with no agreed technical definition, whose declaration rested with OpenAI. The October 2025 rewrite replaced assertion with procedure. Under the announced terms, any declaration of AGI by OpenAI is now verified by an independent expert panel; Microsoft's intellectual property rights to research — defined as the confidential methods used in developing models and systems — run until that panel verifies AGI or through 2030, whichever comes first; and Microsoft's intellectual property rights to models and products run through 2032 and now include models developed after such a declaration, subject to safety guardrails. That is the substance of the joint announcements of 28 October 2025 and the professional summaries published alongside them.

From an accountant's chair, the original clause was unmodellable. A contract that terminates on one party's own philosophical judgement, with tens of billions of dollars of rights attached, cannot be valued, cannot be scheduled and cannot be audited; a reviewer asked to assess the recoverability of an asset dependent on it would have had nothing to work with. The revision does not define artificial general intelligence — nothing does — but it converts an unverifiable assertion into a governed process with a named decision-maker and two hard dates. That is exactly what lawyers do when asked to make a mission statement bankable, and it is the single most consequential drafting change in the whole history of this arrangement.

What is not public: the composition of the expert panel, the criteria it will apply, the evidence it may demand, the standard of proof, whether its decision can be challenged, and what precisely changes in the money when it decides. Reported terms of OpenAI's March 2026 financing add weight to that last gap — Bloomberg reported that thirty-five billion dollars of Amazon's fifty billion dollar participation was contingent on OpenAI either going public or reaching a defined milestone of artificial general intelligence. When a definition nobody has agreed on triggers cash flows of that size across more than one contract, the definition has stopped being a philosophical question and become an accounting one.

## What the accounts show, and what they stopped showing

The most valuable disclosure in this entire relationship is not a clause. It is an accounting policy, and it changed.

Microsoft accounts for its OpenAI holding under the equity method — the treatment the previous chapter set out, in which an investor with significant influence but not control books its share of the investee's results. For the quarter ended 30 September 2025, Microsoft disclosed that its OpenAI investment had reduced quarterly net income by three point one billion dollars, per CNBC's report of the results in October 2025. Journalists immediately did the obvious arithmetic: three point one billion divided by roughly twenty-seven per cent implies a quarterly loss at OpenAI in the region of eleven and a half billion dollars, a calculation published by The Register and others in late October 2025.

Be careful with that number, and notice why. It was not disclosed by anybody. It is an inference drawn by outsiders from one investor's share, applying an ownership percentage that was itself changing, to a company whose accounting basis and period may not align with the investor's. It is arithmetic on a disclosure, not a disclosure — the difference between reading a filing and reading between its lines. It is also, for a while, the only quantified window the public had into the losses of one of the most consequential private companies in the world.

That window then narrowed. Following the recapitalisation, Microsoft records gains or losses based on its share of the change in OpenAI's net assets rather than its share of OpenAI's operating profit or loss, per the 2026 analyses of the company's filings. The distinction is technical and its consequences are large. A share of operating loss tells you how the business performed. A share of the change in net assets moves with everything that changes net assets — including capital raised from third parties. A company that loses money heavily and raises far more than it loses can show its investor a gain.

That is not hypothetical. Microsoft's fiscal 2026 results materials, published on 29 July 2026, state that net gains from investments in OpenAI increased full-year net income by four thousand nine hundred and sixty-three million dollars and diluted earnings per share by sixty-seven cents; other income and expense reached ten billion dollars on a GAAP basis, and was slightly negative once the OpenAI effect was excluded. So within a single fiscal year, the same holding in the same company took roughly three billion dollars off one quarter's net income and added nearly five billion to the year's — with the change in accounting basis doing much of the work.

Nothing about that is irregular. The treatment follows from the restructured instrument, and Microsoft disclosed both the effect and the basis. But it is the clearest illustration this book can offer of a rule stated at the outset: an investor's earnings can be moved substantially by an investee whose own accounts the reader will never see, through a mechanism in which no cash changes hands. CNBC reported in August 2026 that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them. This is the machinery of that distortion, in one company, in one year.

| Effect of OpenAI net investment gains on full-year net income | +4,963 million dollars |

## What is not public

This chapter has flagged gaps as it went. Gathered in one place, the list is longer than the disclosures.

The agreement itself has never been published. No version of it, in any amendment, is available to read. Everything above describes summaries issued by the parties and figures extracted by reporters.

Within the clauses: the split of Microsoft's funded investment between cash and compute; the valuation applied to compute contributed as capital; the conditions on the unfunded balance; the year-by-year schedule of the two hundred and fifty billion dollar Azure commitment; the pricing behind it; whether it is take-or-pay; the termination and change-of-control provisions on either side; the exact revenue-share percentage in force after April 2026; whether the thirty-eight billion dollar cap counts payments already made or only future ones; the composition and procedure of the AGI panel; and the split of Microsoft's twenty-four point one billion dollars between cloud services and revenue share.

And beyond the clauses, the largest gap of all: OpenAI's audited accounts, which do not exist in public form. Every margin, loss and run-rate figure attributed to OpenAI anywhere in this book is a company statement or a press report of a private document. The revenue share, the compute commitment and the equity stake are all claims on a set of financial statements no outsider has read.

One qualification, in fairness to the filer. Microsoft is not withholding these details out of preference; it is disclosing what its regulator and its auditors require of a company of its size, applying materiality thresholds designed for a business selling three hundred and thirty-one billion dollars a year. Seven per cent of revenue from one counterparty crossed a threshold, and the disclosure duly appeared for the first time in fiscal 2026. That is the system working as designed. It simply was not designed to explain a relationship of this shape, in which an investor is also a supplier, a customer, a licensee, a landlord of compute and a competitor, all at once.

## How to read the next amendment

There will be another one. On the record set out here, the terms have been rewritten roughly once a year since 2023, and each rewrite converted something soft into something contractual: exclusivity into a right of first refusal, a right of first refusal into a purchase commitment, an uncapped revenue share into a capped one with an end date, a philosophical trigger into a panel with a deadline.

When it arrives, four places in the accounts will answer more than any press release. The related-party disclosures, where revenue from the counterparty and the receivable balance say how much is actually being billed and how much collected. The purchase commitment and remaining performance obligation disclosures, where promises either convert into revenue or sit. The investment line and its accounting policy note, where the basis of recognition — share of operating result, share of net assets, fair value — determines whether the investee's year flatters or damages the investor's. And the capital expenditure guidance, which reveals how much of the supplier's own balance sheet is being committed against one customer's promises.

None of this is investment advice, and no view is offered here on either company's shares. The claim is narrower and, for the purposes of this book, more useful. The most examined relationship in technology is examined mostly through announcements, and announcements are written to be quoted. The filings are written to be defended. When the two disagree, or when one of them is silent, the reader now knows which gaps are structural and which are simply not yet disclosed — and where, at the next quarter end, to go and look.
