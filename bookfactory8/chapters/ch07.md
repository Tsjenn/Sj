# The Bottom of the Stack

Take an AI accelerator out of its packaging. The company whose name is printed on it has never operated a factory and does not intend to. The company that made the silicon did not design it and does not sell it. The company that made the machine which printed the transistors has no competitor anywhere on earth. The memory stacked beside the logic came from a different firm again, in a different country, running a different business on a different cycle. And the step that joined all of those pieces into one part was performed by yet another company, or by a specialised division that behaves like one.

Five businesses, one component. None of them could produce it alone. Each of them earns a strikingly different amount of money for its trouble, and the gap between them is not explained by how hard the work is.

That is the subject of this chapter. Not the physics — the physics has been well covered elsewhere and is not what an accountant can add to. The subject is the division of labour: who does which step, who owns what, and why an industry organised this way produces chokepoints rather than a supply chain. A chokepoint, it turns out, is worth considerably more than a market share. Understanding why is most of what you need in order to read the rest of this part of the book.

Some scale first, so the later numbers have something to sit against. Global semiconductor sales reached 791.7 billion US dollars in 2025, up 25.6 per cent on the 630.5 billion recorded in 2024, according to the Semiconductor Industry Association's figures published in early 2026; logic products alone accounted for 301.9 billion of that, up 39.9 per cent. The same body's 2026 projection, as reported by Tom's Hardware in 2026, put global sales at around 975.4 billion dollars for the year — revised up sharply from an earlier spring estimate of 760.7 billion. Note that revision. It is a forecast changing by more than two hundred billion dollars inside a few months, which tells you something about the confidence anybody should attach to a semiconductor forecast, including the ones in this book.

## Nobody makes a chip

The sentence sounds wrong, and it is the most useful thing to understand about this industry. There is no company anywhere that takes an idea for a processor and turns it into a finished part using only its own people, its own buildings and its own machines. Not one. The closest anybody comes is Samsung, which designs, manufactures, makes memory and packages — and even Samsung buys its lithography tools from a Dutch supplier like everybody else.

What exists instead is a sequence of specialists, each of whom does one step extremely well and hands the work on.

Design comes first, and it is mostly software. A modern accelerator is described in a hardware description language, simulated, verified and laid out using electronic design automation tools before a single wafer is touched. Two companies dominate that tooling: Synopsys and Cadence. Estimates of their combined share differ by source — market commentary in 2026 has put the pair at around 85 per cent of the electronic design automation market, with individual splits variously given as roughly 38 and 36 per cent, or as 46.0 and 35.1 per cent as at 2024. Those figures do not reconcile, which is normal for privately compiled market-share estimates, and the honest reading is the direction rather than the decimal: two firms, plus Siemens, and very little else. Synopsys closed a 35 billion dollar acquisition of Ansys on 17 July 2025, per 2025 and 2026 reporting on the transaction, which is a large number for a business most people outside the industry have never heard of.

Then the design must be manufactured. The manufacturer is called a foundry, and at the leading edge one of them takes most of the work. In the first quarter of 2026, TrendForce put combined revenue for the world's top ten foundries at a record 47.95 billion US dollars, up 3.7 per cent on the previous quarter, with TSMC's revenue at nearly 35.86 billion and its share of that top-ten total at 72.3 per cent, up from 70.4 per cent in the fourth quarter of 2025. Behind it: Samsung on 6.5 per cent, SMIC on 5.1, UMC on 3.9, GlobalFoundries on 3.3, Hua Hong on 2.5, and Tower, Nexchip, VIS and PSMC on 0.8 per cent each. Read that list twice. The second-placed firm has less than one-tenth of the leader's share.

The foundry cannot operate without equipment, and here the concentration is more extreme still. In the first half of 2025, CINNO Research put the combined semiconductor revenue of the top ten equipment makers above 64 billion US dollars, with ASML first at roughly 17 billion and Applied Materials second at roughly 13.7 billion, followed by Lam Research, Tokyo Electron and KLA. Within that group, one company occupies a position with no parallel in modern industry: ASML is the only maker of extreme ultraviolet lithography systems, holding 100 per cent of EUV and, on 2026 market commentary, around 94 per cent of lithography overall, after Nikon and Canon left the field more than a decade ago. Chapter ten is about how that happened. What matters here is that it is a fact about the machines, not a fact about salesmanship.

After manufacture comes assembly, packaging and test: cutting the wafer into individual dies, mounting them, connecting them, sealing them and verifying that they work. This is the step that has gone from an afterthought to a constraint, and the chapter returns to it.

Memory sits alongside all of this rather than inside it, and is best treated as a separate world with its own producers, its own economics and its own violent cycle. Part Two gives it two chapters of its own for that reason.

## Four words worth learning

Four terms do most of the work in this industry, and they are usually explained badly. They are not technology categories. They are business models, and each one describes a different answer to the same question: which of the expensive things do we own?

A **fabless** company designs chips and owns no factories. It owns intellectual property, engineering talent and customer relationships, and it buys manufacturing as a service. Nvidia is fabless. So are AMD, Qualcomm, Broadcom, Apple's silicon group and, at one remove, every cloud provider designing its own accelerator. The model's appeal is visible in a single comparison: Nvidia reported revenue of 215.9 billion US dollars for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026, up 65 per cent, with GAAP operating income of 130.4 billion and a gross margin of 71.1 per cent, per its results announcement of February 2026 — earned without owning a fab.

A **foundry** manufactures chips designed by other people and sells nothing under its own brand. It owns the most expensive assets in the industry and none of the customer relationships that use them. TSMC is the pure-play foundry; UMC, GlobalFoundries, SMIC and several others operate the same model at different points on the technology curve.

An **IDM**, or integrated device manufacturer, designs and manufactures its own products. This was the original structure of the entire industry. Intel is the best-known survivor, Texas Instruments and the memory makers are others, and Samsung runs an IDM alongside a foundry that competes for other people's work.

An **OSAT** — outsourced semiconductor assembly and test — takes finished wafers and turns them into packaged, tested parts. ASE Technology Holding, Amkor and JCET are the largest. It is the least glamorous description in the industry and, as the numbers below show, that shows up in the margin.

| Model | Owns | Does not own | Earns from | Principal risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fabless | Designs, IP, customers | Fabs, tools, packaging lines | Scarce design and architecture | Being unable to buy capacity |
| Foundry | Fabs, process technology | The product, the end customer | Capacity others cannot build | A fab that is not full |
| IDM | Everything except the tools | The tools | Whole-product margin | Carrying fab cost through a downturn |
| OSAT | Packaging and test lines | The design, the wafer | Throughput and yield | Being substituted or in-sourced |

The categories leak, as categories do. TSMC packages as well as manufactures. Samsung is an IDM, a foundry and a memory maker at once. Intel is an IDM trying to be a foundry. Broadcom is fabless but sells systems. Treat the four words as a description of where a company's capital sits, not as a taxonomy to be defended.

## Why it unbundled

The industry did not start this way. For its first three decades a semiconductor company was expected to own its fabs, because owning fabs was what made you a semiconductor company. The change dates to 1987 and the founding of TSMC on the proposition that manufacturing could be sold as a service rather than performed as a private necessity. Chapter eight tells that story properly.

The economics that made the split inevitable are simpler than the history. A fab is an almost pure fixed cost. It costs an enormous amount to build and equip, it depreciates on a schedule regardless of how busy it is, and its cost per wafer therefore depends overwhelmingly on how full it runs. Trade estimates published in 2026 put the cost of building and equipping a leading-edge 2-nanometre-class fab module of roughly twenty thousand wafer starts a month at 25 to 35 billion US dollars. That is an industry estimate rather than a disclosed figure, and it should be read as an order of magnitude, but no plausible version of it is small.

Now consider what that fixed cost does to two different companies.

Take an illustrative fab, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Suppose it costs twenty billion to build, depreciates over five years at four billion a year, and can produce a hundred thousand wafers a year at full utilisation. Run it flat out and depreciation alone contributes forty thousand dollars per wafer. Run it at half capacity and the same charge contributes eighty thousand. Nothing about the process changed. The only variable was whether the machines had work to do.

A designer with one product line cannot keep such a thing full. A foundry serving three hundred customers across a dozen industries can, because the customers' cycles are not synchronised. That is the whole argument for the split, and it is an argument about utilisation, not about technology. Once one company could offer manufacturing at a utilisation rate no individual designer could match, designing without a fab stopped being a compromise and became the better business. The design houses shed their factories. The factories consolidated. And the equipment makers, serving all of them, consolidated too, because the research required to produce each successive generation of tool grew faster than the number of customers who could pay for it.

The last stage of that logic is the one people miss. Specialisation did not merely divide the work. It concentrated each divided piece, because each piece had its own economies of scale, and those economies were brutal.

## Not a chain, a set of chokepoints

The phrase "semiconductor supply chain" gets used constantly and quietly misleads. A chain implies links of roughly equal character, each with alternatives, each replaceable at some cost. What actually exists is a sequence in which some steps have many suppliers and some have one.

Count the credible options, at the leading edge, step by step.

| Step | Realistic suppliers at the leading edge | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Design tools | Two, plus a third | Synopsys and Cadence at roughly 85 per cent combined, per 2026 market commentary; Siemens behind |
| EUV lithography | One | ASML at 100 per cent of EUV, 2026 market commentary |
| EUV optics | One | Carl Zeiss SMT; ASML bought a 24.9 per cent stake in it for one billion euros in 2016, per Bloomberg, November 2016 |
| Leading-edge manufacture | One dominant, two attempting | TSMC at 72.3 per cent of top-ten foundry revenue in Q1 2026, per TrendForce |
| Advanced packaging | Effectively one at volume | TSMC's advanced packaging reported sold out through 2026, trade reporting 2026 |
| High-bandwidth memory | Three | SK Hynix holding the largest share, per 2026 industry analysis |

That is not a chain. It is a series of doors, several of which have a single keyholder.

The Zeiss line in that table deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the clearest illustration of the structure. ASML has no competitor in EUV. ASML also has no alternative supplier for the optics inside its own machines: the mirrors are made by one German firm, to tolerances nobody else has demonstrated, and ASML thought that relationship important enough to buy a quarter of the business and fund its expansion. A chokepoint, examined closely, usually contains another chokepoint.

Two consequences follow, and they run in opposite directions.

The first is pricing power. Where a buyer has no second source, price is set by what the buyer can bear rather than by what the seller's competitors charge. This is not a moral observation. It is the ordinary consequence of a supply curve that cannot move.

The second is fragility. A structure with single points has single points of failure. Every argument in this book about concentration risk, about Taiwan, about export controls, is ultimately an argument about the same handful of doors. The industry has built extraordinary efficiency by specialising, and it has paid for that efficiency in redundancy it no longer has.

## The test: a bigger share, worth far less

Here is the observation that makes the chapter's argument concrete, and it is one an accountant is well placed to notice.

ASE Technology Holding has a larger share of its own niche than TSMC has of foundry. On TrendForce's 2024 rankings, reported by Evertiq in May 2025, the top ten OSAT companies had combined revenue of 41.56 billion US dollars, up 3 per cent on 2023, of which ASE took 18.54 billion — roughly 44.6 per cent of the top-ten total. Amkor was second at 6.32 billion, or 15.2 per cent, its revenue down 2.8 per cent on the year, and JCET third at 5.0 billion, or 12 per cent, up 19.3 per cent. ASE's share of that group is not far off half.

Now the valuations. TSMC's market capitalisation stood at roughly 2.17 trillion US dollars as at August 2026, per companiesmarketcap. ASE Technology Holding's stood at about 83.5 billion dollars as at 17 July 2026, and Amkor's at about 16.1 billion in July 2026, per the market-data trackers stockanalysis and companiesmarketcap. Every one of those figures moves daily and will have moved by the time this is read.

Round it. A company with roughly 45 per cent of the top-ten packaging market is worth something on the order of four per cent of a company with 72 per cent of the top-ten foundry market. Share of market, by itself, explains almost nothing.

The margins explain a great deal more.

| Company | Step | Gross margin | Period | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nvidia | Design (fabless) | 71.1 per cent GAAP | Fiscal year ended 25 January 2026 | Company results announcement, February 2026 |
| TSMC | Leading-edge manufacture | 67.7 per cent | Second quarter 2026 | Company results, up 9.1 points year on year |
| UMC | Mature-node manufacture | 32.5 per cent | Second quarter 2026 | Company results, 2026 |
| GlobalFoundries | Mature-node manufacture | 28.3 per cent | Second quarter 2026 | Company results announcement, 5 August 2026 |
| ASE (ATM segment) | Packaging and test | 27.3 per cent | Second quarter 2026 | Company unaudited results, 2026 |
| ASE (group) | Packaging, test and EMS | 20.1 per cent | First quarter 2026 | Company unaudited results, 2026 |
| ASE (EMS segment) | Electronics assembly | 8.9 per cent | Second quarter 2026 | Company unaudited results, 2026 |

Read down that column and the industry rearranges itself in front of you. It is not organised by how sophisticated the work is; packaging a high-bandwidth memory stack onto a logic die is not simple work. It is organised by how easily the buyer could go somewhere else.

The two foundry rows in the middle make the same point inside a single business model. UMC and GlobalFoundries run real fabs, employ real process engineers and manufacture chips that go into cars, phones and industrial equipment. They earn roughly half TSMC's gross margin, because what they make can also be made by several other people. A foundry is not automatically a chokepoint. Only the leading edge is, and only while it stays ahead.

And the bottom row is the clearest of all. ASE's electronics manufacturing services segment turned NT$61,875 million of revenue in the first quarter of 2026 into a gross margin of 9.5 per cent, falling to 8.9 per cent in the second, per the company's unaudited results for those quarters. Its assembly, testing and materials segment turned NT$112,434 million into 26.0 per cent in the first quarter and NT$125,069 million into 27.3 per cent in the second, with ATM revenue up 36 per cent year on year. Same holding company, same management, same country, margins nearly three times apart — because one segment does work that many firms could do and the other does work that fewer can.

## What actually makes a chokepoint

Four conditions, and a company needs most of them.

**No technical substitute.** Not "no cheaper alternative" — no alternative. There is no second machine that prints at EUV wavelengths at volume. There is no set of tools that replaces the two design suites without re-training an industry. This is the hardest condition to meet and the only one that cannot be bought.

**A qualification lag.** Even where an alternative exists on paper, switching takes time that the customer does not have. A chip design is qualified for a specific process at a specific fab. Moving it is a project measured in quarters at best, and it puts the product's schedule at risk. The lag is the moat. In advanced packaging, trade reporting in 2026 described lead times of well over a year at the leading providers, with capacity sold out ahead — and a lead time is a moat with a number attached.

**Capital intensity that deters entry.** If matching the incumbent costs tens of billions and takes half a decade, the number of rational entrants is small. TSMC guided 2026 capital expenditure to 52 to 56 billion US dollars in its January 2026 results, above analyst expectations, and then raised the 2026 range to 60 to 64 billion on 16 July 2026 while committing a further 100 billion dollars to Arizona, taking its total announced United States investment to 265 billion, per reporting from Tom's Hardware, EE Times and Reuters in July 2026. An entrant is not competing with a company. It is competing with that annual number, repeated.

**Accumulated knowledge that is not written down.** Process recipes, yield learning, the specific ways a given tool misbehaves in a given fab. This is why buying the same equipment does not produce the same result. It is also the condition that makes chokepoints durable long after their patents expire.

Add a fifth condition that is political rather than industrial: export licensing. Where a government must approve a shipment, the chokepoint acquires a second gatekeeper. This book treats export controls factually, in chapter sixty-nine, and takes no position on them. For present purposes the point is structural: a control regime can only be applied where a single supplier exists, which is precisely why the semiconductor equipment layer has become an instrument of policy and the packaging layer has not.

## Reading a chokepoint in the accounts

You do not need industry gossip to identify which layer holds the scarcity in a given year. The financial statements say it, in five places.

**Gross margin, and its direction.** A margin rising while volumes rise is pricing power. A margin rising while volumes fall is cost-cutting. TSMC's second-quarter 2026 gross margin of 67.7 per cent was up 9.1 points year on year on higher revenue, per company results — that is the first pattern.

**Capacity sold out, and how it is described.** Language such as "sold out through 2026" appears in earnings calls before it appears in the numbers. TSMC's chief executive told shareholders that advanced packaging capacity remained extremely tight and sold out through 2026, per 2026 trade reporting, and separate trade estimates put the company's CoWoS packaging capacity target at roughly 125,000 to 130,000 wafers a month by the end of 2026, against about 35,000 a month at the end of 2024. Treat the capacity numbers as trade estimates rather than disclosed figures — the company does not publish them in that form — but treat the sold-out statement as management's own words.

**Capital expenditure guidance revised upward mid-year.** A company that raises capex during the year is telling you its order book grew faster than its plan. TSMC's move from 52-to-56 billion in January to 60-to-64 billion in July 2026 is exactly that signal, and it is also a reminder of why every figure in this book carries a date: the January number was accurate in January.

**Customer concentration.** Nvidia's annual report for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026 disclosed four direct customers each above ten per cent of revenue, together about 61 per cent of the business. Concentration on that scale is what selling a scarce input to the few firms able to afford it looks like. It is a disclosure, not an allegation, and it cuts both ways: a supplier with four customers that matter has power over them and exposure to them simultaneously.

**Prepayments and purchase commitments.** When customers pay before delivery, or suppliers demand commitments years ahead, the balance sheet is recording scarcity in advance of the income statement. Chapter three sets out where those lines sit.

The complementary test is what a non-chokepoint's accounts look like when it tries to become one. Intel Foundry reported an operating loss of about 2.1 billion US dollars in the second quarter of 2026, narrowed from about 3.2 billion in the same quarter of 2025, with third-party foundry and assembly-and-test revenue from external customers of 293 million dollars in the three months ended 27 June 2026 and 467 million in the first half — figures from Intel's second-quarter 2026 results as reported by trade press including Semiecosystem in July 2026. Set the 293 million beside TSMC's 35.86 billion of quarterly foundry revenue and the scale of the attempt is clear. That is not a judgement on whether it will work; chapter seventeen deals with Intel at length. It is a measurement of what it costs to try to open a second door.

## Chokepoints move

Nothing in the structure is permanent, and the last decade has produced two clear demonstrations.

Packaging used to be the cheap step at the end. It stopped being that when accelerators grew larger than a single die could be and had to be built from several pieces joined on an interposer, with memory stacked beside them. At that moment a low-margin service became a physical constraint on how many accelerators the world could produce. Trade estimates in 2026 attributed roughly 60 per cent of TSMC's advanced-packaging allocation for the year to one customer. Those are estimates from the trade press, not disclosed figures, and should be read as such. The direction is not in doubt: the industry spent 2025 and 2026 discussing packaging capacity in the way it had previously discussed wafer capacity.

Memory made a larger move. For decades it was the worst business in semiconductors — a commodity with punishing capital intensity and a cycle that destroyed more capital than it created. Chapter twenty tells that history. Then AI accelerators turned out to be limited by memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic, high-bandwidth memory became the answer, and a commodity turned into a qualified, contracted component. The 2026 high-bandwidth memory market was estimated at about 54.6 billion US dollars, up roughly 58 per cent year on year, with SK Hynix holding the largest share and capacity reported sold out through 2026 across the major suppliers, per industry analysis published in 2026. SK Hynix announced a 38 billion dollar investment in new memory plants, reported by CNBC in August 2026. Year to date in 2026, Samsung was reported up about 114 per cent, SK Hynix about 186 per cent and Micron about 141 per cent, per IG and industry reporting in mid-2026 — share-price moves from an arbitrary calendar starting line, and not a forecast of anything.

Both moves happened because a technical requirement changed which step was scarce. Neither was predicted by the people who would benefit most.

The reverse is also possible and has happened before. A chokepoint dissolves when the technical requirement it served stops mattering, when a customer decides the price justifies building an alternative, or when a government funds one. None of those is quick. All of them are ordinary.

## Two things this is not

A chokepoint is not an accusation. Everything described here is legal, disclosed and the ordinary result of specialisation and scale. Where a structure looks uncomfortable — a single supplier, a customer that is also an investor, capacity allocated by relationship — this book explains the mechanism and what a reader should watch in the accounts. It does not allege misconduct that public disclosure does not support, here or anywhere else.

Nor is any of it advice. This book contains no recommendation to buy or sell any security, no price target and no prediction about any share price. The margin tables above are there to teach a method: find the step, count the suppliers, then look at the gross margin and see whether the two agree.

Where they disagree, one of them is wrong, and it is usually the count.

## Where to look it up

Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot. Foundry shares are recompiled quarterly by TrendForce and its competitors and move by a point or two each time. Market capitalisations move daily. Capital expenditure guidance moved by eight billion dollars for a single company inside six months of 2026.

The durable sources are the ordinary ones. TSMC, ASML, UMC, GlobalFoundries, ASE, Amkor and Intel all publish quarterly results, presentation slides and call transcripts on their own investor relations pages, and the foreign issuers among them furnish the same material to the United States regulator. Market-share estimates come from firms such as TrendForce and CINNO Research and are models, not measurements; where two of them disagree, as they frequently do, say so rather than picking the one that suits the sentence. The Semiconductor Industry Association publishes monthly and annual sales totals for the industry as a whole.

What will not change with the next quarterly compilation is the shape. The company that designs a chip does not make it. The company that makes it does not make the machines. The company that makes the machines cannot buy its optics anywhere else. And the memory arrives from a different industry entirely, on its own schedule, at its own price.

The next chapter takes the largest of those doors and looks at how it was built.
