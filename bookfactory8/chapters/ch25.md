# Amazon Web Services

On 23 April 2015, Amazon published a quarterly earnings release with a segment table it had never shown before. For nine years the company had sold computing by the hour and storage by the gigabyte while disclosing almost nothing about what the business earned. Analysts had guessed. Many had guessed that the cloud division was an expensive hobby, subsidised by books and nappies, run at a loss to keep Jeff Bezos amused. The new table said otherwise. Amazon Web Services had taken in about one point five seven billion US dollars in the first quarter of 2015, up roughly fifty per cent year on year, an annual run-rate of about six point three billion — and it was profitable, at margins the retail business had never once approached, per the company's first-quarter 2015 results and the trade coverage that April. Amazon's shares jumped on the disclosure.

That table is the honest summary of everything this chapter covers. A business built as internal plumbing turned out to be the most profitable thing its owner had ever made, and it stayed that way. By 2024, AWS produced thirty-nine point eight billion dollars of operating income on one hundred and seven point six billion of sales — while Amazon as a whole, a company with more than six hundred billion dollars of revenue, reported sixty-eight point six billion of total operating income, per the company's fourth-quarter 2024 results published in February 2025. Well over half the operating profit of one of the world's largest companies came from a division most of its customers have never knowingly touched.

Then the AI wave arrived, and it arrived awkwardly. Amazon owned more rentable computing than anyone on earth and did not own the thing everyone suddenly wanted to run on it: a frontier model. The company's two answers to that problem — a very large cheque to Anthropic, and a decade-long bet on its own chips — are where this chapter ends up. Both answers run through the accounts, and one of them recently produced the strangest line item in Amazon's history.

## The retailer's side project

The origin story is mundane, which is rather the point. In the early 2000s Amazon was rebuilding its own systems so that internal teams could get storage, computing and messaging as standardised services rather than by begging a hardware group for servers. Somewhere in that work came the recognition that if standardised infrastructure was useful inside one company, it might be sellable outside it.

The first public piece was not glamorous. Simple Queue Service, a tool for passing messages between programs, appeared in preview in November 2004, per the service launch histories AWS itself maintains. The two products that mattered came in 2006: Simple Storage Service, S3, launched in March 2006, and Elastic Compute Cloud, EC2, in August 2006 — storage by the gigabyte-month and computing by the hour, rented over the internet with a credit card, no salesperson required.

It is worth pausing on what was actually new. Renting computing was not new; time-sharing predates Amazon by decades. What was new was the shape of the transaction. No minimum commitment. No procurement cycle. No capacity planning by the customer at all. A developer with an idea at midnight could have a server two minutes later and pay a few cents for the privilege. The buyer of computing changed from a chief information officer with a budget to anyone with a laptop, and the industry has never changed back.

The accounting consequence of that shape deserves its own sentence, because it is the hinge of the whole compute layer. Before 2006, a company that wanted computing bought servers: capital expenditure, an asset on its own balance sheet, depreciation charged over years, capacity guessed at in advance and usually wrong in one direction or the other. After 2006, the same company could rent the computing: an operating expense, no asset, no depreciation, no guess. The capital expenditure did not disappear. It moved — onto Amazon's balance sheet, along with the depreciation, the utilisation risk and the capacity planning, all now pooled across millions of customers whose peaks do not coincide. AWS is, at bottom, a machine for aggregating other people's balance-sheet risk and charging a margin for carrying it. Everything else about the business model is decoration on that transfer.

A popular version of the story says Amazon was simply renting out the spare capacity it kept for the Christmas rush. The economics of the business make that account hard to credit — a serious cloud requires purpose-built capacity, not seasonal leftovers, and AWS was building data centres for external customers from early on. The tidier truth is less romantic: a retailer with famously thin margins had taught itself to run infrastructure cheaply out of necessity, then discovered that the discipline itself was the product.

The other thing Amazon had was a head start measured in years. Serious competition from Microsoft and Google took until the following decade to materialise as full alternatives. But the head start is the most over-credited part of the story, and the next section is about why.

## Cheapest and most complete

Being first in infrastructure is worth less than it looks. Infrastructure customers are sticky once committed, but they commit slowly, and a late rival with a better price and an existing enterprise relationship can win the next decade even after losing the first one. Microsoft proved exactly that. What kept AWS in front through the 2010s was not the 2006 launch date. It was a set of duller virtues, compounded relentlessly.

The first was price, and more precisely the direction of price. AWS cut prices repeatedly, publicly, and often without competitive pressure, passing through some of the falling cost of hardware. The reason to do this — before any competitor forced it — is the same logic that runs through the foundry chapters earlier in this book: infrastructure economics reward utilisation. A data centre is a fixed cost. Every additional customer spreads that cost thinner, which lowers unit cost, which permits lower prices, which attracts more customers. Scale is not a vanity metric in this business; it is the cost structure. The retailer's instinct — take the margin down, take the volume up, let the flywheel do the work — turned out to translate perfectly to computing.

The second was completeness. AWS shipped services at a pace competitors found demoralising: databases, queues, analytics, networking, identity management, machine learning tooling, hundreds of distinct products over the years. Completeness matters because of how cloud adoption actually happens. A team adopts one service. The next problem is easier to solve one shelf over than by introducing a second vendor. Each additional service is individually small and collectively decisive, because the cost of leaving is the cost of re-plumbing everything at once. Nobody migrates off a cloud because a rival's virtual machine is five per cent cheaper. The completeness is the moat, and the moat was built one unglamorous service at a time.

There is a third virtue, less visible, that an accountant notices before anyone else does: AWS learned to sell commitment. The by-the-hour price is the advertised one, but mature cloud customers buy differently — reserving capacity for a year or three in exchange for a discount, or committing to a level of spend across services. From the customer's side this is a hedge. From AWS's side it is something better: a forward order book for a business whose costs are overwhelmingly fixed and incurred in advance. A data centre is a bet on future demand; a book of multi-year customer commitments converts part of that bet into something closer to a lease portfolio. When you read any large cloud's disclosures, the committed-but-unrecognised revenue balance is the number that tells you how much of the future is already contracted, and its growth rate is the earliest clean reading of enterprise demand — earlier than revenue, which reports the past.

Put the three together and the strategy is easy to state and brutal to copy: be the cheapest place to do everything, so that doing anything anywhere else requires a justification. Through the 2010s no justification usually arrived, and AWS became the default infrastructure of the internet economy — the startups, the streaming services, the banks in cautious stages, and eventually governments.

The scoreboard, as at the time of writing: Synergy Research Group put AWS at about twenty-eight per cent of a global cloud infrastructure market worth about one hundred and forty-three billion dollars in the second quarter of 2026 alone — a market that had grown by roughly forty-three billion dollars from the same quarter a year earlier. In the first quarter of 2026, on the same firm's figures as compiled by industry trackers, the ranking read AWS around thirty per cent, Microsoft Azure around twenty-five, Google Cloud around thirteen. Still first, a decade and a half on. But the gap is not what it was, and the drift matters more than the level — a point worth holding until the squeeze section below.

## The profit engine

Now the accountant's part, because the numbers here explain Amazon's entire corporate strategy better than any press release ever has.

| Full year 2025 | 128.7bn USD (up 20%) | 45.6bn USD | 77.7bn USD | Company Q4 2025 results, February 2026 |
| Q2 2026 | 42.2bn USD (up 37%) | 16.6bn USD | 27.5bn USD | Company Q2 2026 results, July 2026 |

Do the arithmetic the table invites. In 2025, AWS supplied forty-five point six billion of the group's seventy-seven point seven billion in operating income — a little under three-fifths — on one hundred and twenty-eight point seven billion of the group's seven hundred and sixteen point nine billion in net sales, which is roughly eighteen per cent. In the second quarter of 2026 the pattern sharpened: sixteen point six of twenty-seven point five billion, about three-fifths of the profit, from about a fifth of the revenue, with the segment's operating margin reaching about thirty-nine per cent, per the company's July 2026 results and CNBC's coverage of them.

This is what the phrase "funded the retailer's entire strategy" means in practice. Everything Amazon's consumer business is famous for — free shipping as a birthright, a logistics network built to the scale of a national postal service, years of patient losses in new countries and new categories — sat on top of a profit engine that most customers never saw. The retail business supplied the culture and the customer obsession. The cloud supplied the money. An analyst who wanted to understand Amazon's risk appetite in any given year has always been better served by the AWS segment note than by anything happening on the shopping side.

To see why scale produces margins like these, take an illustrative cloud region, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Suppose the region costs one billion dollars to build and equip, and the hardware is depreciated over five years: two hundred million a year of depreciation before a single customer arrives, plus, say, one hundred million a year of power, staff and maintenance. At thirty per cent utilisation the region might bill four hundred million a year — a heavy loss. At seventy per cent utilisation, on the same fixed base, it might bill nine hundred million against barely higher running costs, and the loss becomes a fat margin. Nothing about the service improved; the same buildings simply filled up. That is the entire microeconomic engine of the cloud: costs that arrive in lumps years early, revenue that arrives by the hour, and a margin that lives or dies on the gap between them. It is also why every cloud provider is obsessed with commitments, and why a price cut that lifts utilisation can raise profit. The numbers above are invented; the mechanism is not.

The margin itself deserves a moment, because chapter twenty-three's machinery applies directly. An operating margin in the mid-to-high thirties, on a business whose main input is buildings full of depreciating hardware, reflects three things stacked together: scale amortising fixed costs, software and services sold at software margins on top of raw infrastructure, and a set of depreciation assumptions about how long the hardware usefully lives. That last item is management judgement, it moves reported profit without moving cash, and the AI era — which works hardware harder and obsoletes it faster — is exactly the environment in which such judgements deserve a sceptical annual re-reading. None of this is an allegation about anyone's numbers. It is a description of where the soft joints in any cloud margin are, and every large cloud has them.

## The squeeze, and the missing model

The diagram's third act is the least flattering one. Through the early 2020s, AWS's growth decelerated markedly as customers, squeezed by the post-pandemic economy, went through what the industry politely called "optimisation" — spending less on the cloud they already had. At the same time the two chasing clouds grew faster from smaller bases, and the market-share drift noted above set in. When AWS's growth accelerated to thirty-seven per cent in the second quarter of 2026, CNBC noted it was the segment's fastest rate in eighteen quarters — which is a compliment containing its own indictment. Eighteen quarters is four and a half years. The engine had spent the entire early AI boom running below its old pace.

That timing was the uncomfortable part. The generative AI wave broke in late 2022 and 2023, and it broke over a landscape in which Microsoft held a deep partnership with OpenAI and Google owned a frontier lab outright. Amazon held the largest pool of rentable computing on earth and no frontier model of its own. Its Bedrock service took the merchant's position — selling access to other companies' models, as a supermarket stocks other people's brands — and its internal model work stayed, by general industry assessment, behind the frontier. For a company whose whole cloud thesis was "whatever the workload, run it here," the workload of the decade was being shaped, named and marketed by its two closest rivals.

Worse, the economics of the new workload threatened the old cost advantage. AI computing runs on accelerators purchased overwhelmingly from one supplier, at that supplier's pricing. Nvidia's data-centre business booked one hundred and ninety-seven point three billion dollars of revenue in its fiscal year ended 25 January 2026, per the company's February 2026 results announcement — revenue that is, from the perspective of its customers, cost. A cloud whose identity was "cheapest" now found the largest single line of its new capital spending priced by someone else's gross margin. Being cheapest at renting Nvidia's chips is a much thinner business than being cheapest at renting your own.

The optimisation years also revealed something about the moat. Customers did not leave — the re-plumbing cost saw to that — but they proved they could spend less without leaving, trimming instances, shortening storage retention, negotiating harder at renewal. A moat keeps customers in; it does not keep their invoices up. That distinction matters for how you read cloud revenue in any downturn, and it sharpened the strategic question inside Amazon: staying the default was no longer enough if the growth, the pricing power and the narrative had all moved to a workload the company neither defined nor supplied.

So Amazon faced one problem wearing two masks: no frontier model to anchor demand, and no control over the silicon that the demand runs on. It answered both, and the answers are best read side by side, because they are the same move made at two different layers of the stack.

## First answer: buy into the model layer

The relationship with Anthropic was built in tranches, each larger than the last.

| Date | Commitment | Running total | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| September 2023 | 1.25bn USD initial, up to 4bn agreed | up to 4bn USD | CNBC, September 2023 reporting |
| March 2024 | 2.75bn USD, completing the first agreement | 4bn USD | CNBC, March 2024 |
| November 2024 | additional 4bn USD; AWS named Anthropic's primary cloud and training partner | 8bn USD | CNBC and Amazon's announcement, November 2024 |
| April 2026 | up to a further 25bn USD as part of an AI infrastructure agreement | up to 33bn USD | CNBC, April 2026 |

At the March 2024 tranche, CNBC noted it was the largest outside investment in Amazon's three-decade history. The November 2024 agreement mattered as much for its words as its money: "primary cloud and training partner" converts an investee into an anchor tenant. And the physical expression of that phrase came online on 29 October 2025, when AWS activated Project Rainier — a purpose-built compute cluster of nearly half a million of Amazon's own Trainium2 chips across a campus in Indiana, dedicated to training and serving Anthropic's Claude models, with Anthropic planning to expand to more than a million Trainium2 chips by the end of 2025, per AWS's announcement and the data-centre trade press that October.

Notice the structure, because it recurs across this industry and chapter five of this book exists to help you read it. Amazon invests billions in Anthropic. Anthropic commits billions of spending to AWS. Money goes out as an investment and a related stream comes back as revenue, from a company the investor part-owns. This is not an accusation — the disclosures are public, the logic is defensible on both sides, and Anthropic raised comparable sums from Google, which had committed up to forty-three billion dollars including ten billion in April 2026, per 2026 reporting. But a circular structure places a duty on the reader: when you see cloud revenue growth, ask how much of it is arm's-length demand and how much is a partner spending money its cloud provider invested. The answer may be perfectly satisfactory. The question must still be asked.

Then came the quarter in which the investment swallowed the income statement. Anthropic closed a sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, as reported by CNBC that month. Amazon's stake had to be remeasured accordingly. In its second-quarter 2026 results, published in July 2026, Amazon reported net income of sixty-two point six billion dollars — but that figure included fifty-three point four billion of non-operating pre-tax other income, primarily from its Anthropic investments, per the company's results announcement and the coverage of it. Roughly two-thirds of the quarter's pre-tax income was a valuation mark on a private company, and the mark-up was nearly twice the twenty-seven point five billion the entire operating business earned. Earnings per share came in at five dollars seventy-five against analyst expectations of roughly one dollar eighty-one, per CNBC in July 2026 — a "beat" produced not by selling more of anything but by a funding round's price.

Treat that line item with the respect and the suspicion it deserves. The gain is real in the sense that accounting standards require it to be recognised. It is also non-cash, unrealised, and anchored to the price of the last shares sold in a private negotiation — a price set, as chapter five explains, by the most optimistic marginal buyer, on terms outsiders cannot fully see. CNBC reported in August 2026 that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic were distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them, and Amazon's second quarter is the exhibit. A reader comparing Amazon's net income across years without stripping this item out is not analysing; they are being decorated. The operating income line, dull as ever, remains the one that describes the business.

One further wrinkle completes the picture of how tangled the model layer's ownership has become: Amazon was reported as investing fifty billion dollars in OpenAI's one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar March 2026 round at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes. The largest cloud thus holds reported positions in both leading independent labs — its partner's chief rival included. Whatever the model layer decides, Amazon has arranged to be its landlord and, increasingly, its shareholder.

## Second answer: make the silicon yourself

The second answer began a decade before the problem was visible, which is the only time such answers can begin.

In January 2015, Amazon acquired Annapurna Labs, an Israeli chip design firm, for a reported three hundred and fifty million dollars, per Bloomberg and GeekWire at the time. The price was rounding error; the capability was not. Annapurna became AWS's silicon division and worked outward from the unglamorous end: first the Nitro cards that offload networking and security from every AWS server, then Graviton, an Arm-based general-purpose processor. Only then did it climb toward the hard part. Inferentia, a chip for running trained models, was announced at the re:Invent conference in November 2018, per CNBC. Trainium, for training them, followed at re:Invent in December 2020, per TechCrunch. Trainium2 powered Project Rainier's roughly half-million-chip cluster in October 2025, and Trainium3, built on a leading-edge three-nanometre process, was announced at re:Invent in December 2025, per the data-centre trade press.

The logic is the mirror image of the Anthropic logic. The investment in Anthropic secures demand for AWS's computing; the investment in Trainium attacks the cost of supplying it. Every dollar of accelerator spending that goes to a merchant chip vendor carries that vendor's gross margin — and the vendor in question runs gross margins that chapter fifteen of this book examines at length. A cloud that designs its own accelerator does not escape the costs of manufacture; it still pays a foundry, a memory supplier and a packaging house. What it escapes is the designer's markup, and it gains a chip shaped exactly for its own data centres and software rather than for a general market. The trade-off is equally real: years of development, a software ecosystem that must be built rather than inherited, and the risk that customers simply prefer the chips they know.

By mid-2026 the bet had numbers attached. Amazon said its AI business and its custom chip operation had each passed a twenty-five billion dollar annualised revenue run-rate, both growing at triple-digit percentage rates, per its second-quarter 2026 results coverage on CNBC in July 2026. Run-rate, as always in this book, means a recent period multiplied out — not audited annual revenue — but the direction is the message. The clearest customer evidence is Rainier itself: the most compute-hungry tenant AWS has ever signed chose, with Amazon's money in its capital structure and Amazon's chips under contract, to train frontier models on Trainium at a scale of hundreds of thousands of units. Whether that choice was made on pure merit or as part of the larger financial relationship is exactly the kind of question the circular structure obliges you to ask — and from outside, the honest answer is that the public record does not separate the two.

## What to watch

The chapter's threads meet in the capital expenditure line, which has become the most consequential number Amazon publishes. Property and equipment purchases reached one hundred and sixty-nine billion dollars on a trailing twelve-month basis as at the second quarter of 2026, up sixty-four per cent year on year, per CNBC's July 2026 coverage of the results; compilations of 2026 guidance reported by CNBC in February 2026 put Amazon's planned spending for the year around two hundred billion. Spending at that scale converts, through the machinery of chapter twenty-three, into a decade of future depreciation charged against margins that investors currently admire. Whether the mid-thirties AWS margin survives the arrival of those charges depends on demand staying ahead of the depreciation schedule — and on the useful-life assumptions behind that schedule proving honest in an era when hardware ages by model generation rather than by calendar.

So the watch-list for this company writes itself, and every item on it sits in a public filing. The AWS segment note, quarterly, for whether growth and margin move together or apart. The share drift against Azure and Google Cloud on Synergy's quarterly estimates, because a profit engine losing relative position is a different machine from one holding it. The capex line against the depreciation charge, for the squeeze that arrives on a lag. The "other income" line, for marks on private AI stakes that can double reported profit without a single additional customer. And the disclosures around Anthropic — investment out, revenue in — for the day the two streams are large enough to deserve their own reconciliation.

None of this says what the shares are worth; this book does not do that, and a market capitalisation is a photograph anyway. What the record says is narrower and more durable. A retailer built the world's largest computing business by accident of discipline, funded twenty years of ambition from its margins, met the AI era without the one product the era demanded, and responded by buying into the layer above while building the layer below. Both moves are visible, dated and priced in the accounts. Read them there — not in the headlines, which in Amazon's case have lately been written by a valuation mark.
