# Intel: How the Leader Lost the Lead

In November 2024, Intel was removed from the Dow Jones Industrial Average and Nvidia was admitted in its place, a swap widely reported at the time, including by CNBC. For most of the preceding forty years that sentence would have been unthinkable. Intel was not a member of the semiconductor industry; Intel was, to a first approximation, the semiconductor industry — the company whose founders gave the business its defining law, whose processors sat in most of the world's computers, and whose manufacturing was, for three decades, simply the best there was.

This chapter is the cautionary tale the rest of the book leans on. Every other company in this part of the book — TSMC taking the leading edge, AMD taking server share, Nvidia taking the market's imagination — is, in part, a story about space Intel vacated. Understanding how the leader lost the lead is worth more than any amount of admiring the companies that picked it up, because the mechanism is general. It applies to any business whose advantage rests on a lead that must be re-won on a schedule.

Two warnings before starting. First, this is not a story about foolish people. Intel's mistakes were made by some of the most capable engineers and managers in industrial history, inside a model that had worked for thirty years. That is precisely what makes it instructive. Second, the story is not over. The company at the end of this chapter looks very different from the company in the middle of it, and honest accounting requires covering the recovery as carefully as the fall.

## The company that built the industry

Intel was founded in July 1968 by Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore, two of the eight who had earlier walked out of Shockley Semiconductor to found Fairchild, and they were joined almost immediately by Andrew Grove. Its first successful product was memory: the 1103, an early dynamic random-access memory chip, per the standard corporate histories. The microprocessor followed, at first almost as a sideline. Then, in the mid-1980s, Japanese manufacturers made commodity memory ruinously competitive, and Intel made the decision the whole industry still studies: it abandoned the business it was founded on. Grove later told the story as a question he put to Moore — if the board replaced us, what would the new management do? The answer was obvious from outside the building: get out of memories. So they did it themselves, and bet the company on microprocessors.

The bet paid for forty years. The x86 processor architecture became the standard for personal computers and then for servers, and Intel manufactured its own designs in its own fabs, on processes its own engineers developed. That combination has a name in the industry: the integrated device manufacturer, or IDM. Chapter seven set out the division of labour that now dominates — fabless designers, pure-play foundries, equipment makers — and Intel was the great exception to it. It did everything under one roof, and for thirty years doing everything under one roof was not a compromise. It was the advantage.

## What integration actually bought

It is worth being precise about why, because the same structure later became the problem, and nothing about the structure itself changed in between.

| What integration bought | The mechanism | Where it shows in the accounts |
|---|---|---|
| Process tuned to the product | Designers and process engineers in one company, co-optimising each generation | Higher gross margin than merchant rivals |
| A captive customer for every fab | Internal volume fills the fab from day one; no need to win orders | High, predictable fab utilisation |
| The cadence itself | A new process every roughly two years, alternated with new architectures — the rhythm Intel formalised in the mid-2000s as "tick-tock" | Steady R&D and capex as a share of revenue, year after year |
| Margin that funds the next node | Leading-edge performance commands leading-edge prices | Operating cash flow large enough to self-fund fabs |

Look at the second row longest, because it is the heart of both the rise and the fall. A leading-edge fab is a vast fixed cost — the general economics were set out in chapter nine, with an illustrative example. Fixed costs are only cheap when spread across volume. Intel's fabs were guaranteed volume, because Intel's own processors filled them, and Intel's processors were guaranteed manufacturing at the best process in the world, because Intel owned it. Each side of the company de-risked the other. A merchant foundry had to win orders before it could fill a fab; a fabless designer had to queue for someone else's capacity. Intel had neither problem.

And the flywheel compounded. The best process attracted the highest prices; the highest prices funded the largest capex; the largest capex built the next process first. Being roughly a node ahead of everyone else was not a happy accident that Intel kept having. It was the output of a machine built to produce exactly that result, on a schedule, for decades.

An accountant reading Intel's filings through that era would have seen the machine working: gross margins that merchant semiconductor companies could not approach, capital expenditure that looked terrifying in isolation and entirely rational against the cash flow funding it, and depreciation charges that were, in effect, the cost of admission being paid gladly. The point of chapter three — that gross margin is the scoreboard of pricing power — has no better long-run illustration than integrated-era Intel.

There was one conspicuous miss during the good years, and it mattered later. Paul Otellini, in an exit interview with The Atlantic in May 2013, said that Intel had declined to make the processor for the original iPhone, because Apple's offered price was, in his words, below Intel's forecasted cost. The forecast was wrong; the volumes were vastly larger than anyone at Intel modelled. The lasting damage was not the lost revenue. It was where those smartphone volumes went instead: to chips manufactured, in time, by TSMC. The mobile boom Intel sat out became the revenue that funded its eventual competitor's climb to the leading edge — an early, expensive lesson in the theme of this chapter, which is that in a fixed-cost industry, volume is destiny.

## The slip

The machine broke quietly, and then loudly.

The quiet part was the 10-nanometre process. Intel's first 10-nanometre silicon had originally been slated for around 2016, per the trade press of the period. It slipped to 2017, then further; in April 2018 the company conceded that volume production would not come until 2019, citing yield problems — the share of working chips per wafer stubbornly refusing to rise — and volume 10-nanometre parts finally arrived in the second half of 2019, roughly three years late, as reported at the time by Tom's Hardware and EE Times among others. One engineering decision attracted much of the retrospective blame: Intel had attempted an unusually aggressive density jump using existing lithography pushed to its limits, rather than waiting for the new extreme-ultraviolet machines described in chapter ten. The ambition was characteristic. The schedule did not survive it.

The loud part came on 23 July 2020, when Intel announced alongside its second-quarter results that its next process, 7-nanometre, was itself running about a year behind schedule. The following day Intel's shares fell sixteen per cent, the worst performer in the S&P 500, as reported by CNN Business and CNBC in July 2020; AMD's shares rose roughly fifteen per cent on the same news, per contemporary trade coverage. The market had understood something in one trading session that is worth spelling out slowly.

| Process generation | Original expectation | What happened | As reported by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10-nanometre | Around 2016 | Volume production second half of 2019 | Trade press, 2016–2019 |
| 7-nanometre | On the tick-tock cadence after 10 | Announced roughly a year late, July 2020 | Company statement; CNBC, July 2020 |

A single delayed product is a bad quarter. A delayed process node is a different kind of event, because every future product in the pipeline is designed against the process schedule. Slip the node and you slip the entire roadmap behind it — every processor, every price point, every competitive comparison — for years. And because the rival roadmaps at TSMC and Samsung kept moving, a one-year slip was not a one-year problem. It was a widening gap: they advanced while Intel repeated a year.

## Why a delay becomes a structural loss

Here is the mechanism this chapter exists to teach. It has three stages, and each one is rational for every participant.

**Stage one: customers qualify a second source.** No serious buyer of computing tolerates a single point of failure for long. When the best supplier stumbles, customers do not immediately leave — qualifying an alternative processor, board and software stack is slow and expensive — but they start the qualification. That work is a one-way door. Once a data-centre operator has validated AMD's processors, made at TSMC, alongside Intel's, the second source exists forever, and every future purchase is a competition. Apple went further: in June 2020, a month before the 7-nanometre announcement, it announced the Mac would move from Intel processors to Apple's own silicon, manufactured by TSMC, and shipped the first M1 machines that November. By October 2021 Intel was naming Apple's departure as a drag on its client-computing revenue, per The Register's coverage of its third-quarter results. AMD's parallel gains in servers are chapter sixteen's story, told from the other side of the table.

**Stage two: the scale advantage inverts.** This is the stage most narratives miss, and it is the one an accountant sees first. Recall what made integration work: guaranteed internal volume spreading the fixed cost of the world's best process. Run the logic backwards. When the process is no longer the best, the products lose ground; when the products lose ground, internal volume falls; when internal volume falls, the enormous fixed cost of fabs and process development is spread across fewer wafers, and the cost per wafer rises just as pricing power weakens. Meanwhile the volume that left — Apple's, AMD's, eventually a share of nearly everyone's — lands at TSMC, where it deepens the rival's scale instead. The same fixed-cost arithmetic that compounded in Intel's favour for thirty years began compounding against it. Nothing about the model changed. Only the sign.

**Stage three: rebuilding costs more than defending.** Defending a process lead means funding one node's development from the comfortable margins of the previous one, with full fabs and premium prices. Rebuilding a lost lead means funding several nodes' development at once, at forced-march pace, from shrinking margins, while fabs run below their designed volume and customers wait to be convinced. Every element of the task is the same as before; every economic condition attached to it is worse. That asymmetry — cheap to keep, ruinously expensive to regain — is the reason process leadership, once lost, has so rarely been recovered by anyone, and it is the single most important idea in this chapter.

## What the accounts recorded

The financial statements registered the damage with a lag, as they usually do — customers qualify alternatives quietly for years before the revenue line admits it. Then the admission came all at once.

| | 2021 | 2024 | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 79.0 billion dollars — an all-time record | 53.1 billion dollars, a third consecutive annual decline | Company results, January 2022 and January 2025 |
| Net result | Profitable | Net loss of 18.8 billion dollars — the first annual loss since 1986 | Company results, January 2025; Electronics Weekly, January 2025 |
| Foundry segment | — | Operating loss of roughly 13 billion dollars, against roughly 7 billion in 2023 | Company results, January 2025 |
| Index membership | Dow Jones Industrial Average member | Removed; replaced by Nvidia, November 2024 | CNBC, November–December 2024 |

Three years from record revenue to the first annual loss in thirty-eight years. Part of the 2024 loss was restructuring and impairment rather than trading — the accounting recognition of past optimism, taken in one swallow — but the direction needed no adjusting. In December 2024, chief executive Pat Gelsinger departed abruptly, a retirement that CNBC reported as the board's decision, and two insiders held the company as interim co-chiefs until Lip-Bu Tan, formerly chief executive of the chip-design-software company Cadence, was appointed in March 2025, per CNBC.

It is worth pausing on how fast the reputational compounding ran once it started. The same fabs, the same engineers, the same accumulated process knowledge that had constituted the industry's deepest moat were, by 2024, being valued by the market as something closer to a liability — a fixed-cost burden without the volume to carry it. Assets do not change their nature that quickly. Expectations do, and chapter two explained why the market's number is made of expectations and nothing else.

## The foundry bet

Gelsinger's answer to the crisis, announced within weeks of his arrival in 2021, was not retreat but doubling down — with a twist that inverted the company's oldest instinct.

In March 2021 he announced what he called IDM 2.0: Intel would keep designing and manufacturing its own chips, would spend twenty billion dollars on two new fabs in Arizona, and — the new part — would open its manufacturing to outside customers through a new business, Intel Foundry Services, per the company's announcement of 23 March 2021. In July 2021 came the schedule: five process nodes in four years, ending in a node called 18A, intended to return Intel to process leadership by 2025, per the company's roadmap announcement. Read against this chapter's third stage, the logic is straightforward. If lost internal volume is what breaks the fixed-cost machine, then external volume — other companies' chips filling Intel's fabs, as they fill TSMC's — is what could rebuild it. Intel would become, in part, the thing it had spent fifty years not being: a foundry, selling manufacturing as a service to the very fabless companies its old model was built to beat.

The difficulty is that a foundry's product is not wafers. It is trust, compounded over decades — chapter eight's argument about TSMC, run in reverse. A fabless customer must hand its crown jewels to its manufacturer and believe three things: that the process will yield, that the schedule will hold, and that the manufacturer — who in Intel's case still designs competing products — will never advantage its own side of the house. TSMC's founding insight was that never competing with customers was worth more than any process advantage. Intel proposes to earn the same trust while keeping the competing products. That is the strategic tension at the heart of the bet, and no amount of capital resolves it; only years of delivered promises can.

The scoreboard so far is honestly mixed. Microsoft signed on as a customer for the 18A node in February 2024, per Bloomberg — a real vote of confidence. But in the second quarter of 2026, external customers still accounted for only about five per cent of the foundry segment's revenue — roughly 293 million dollars of 5.8 billion, the rest being Intel's own chips moved through the segment at internal prices — per the company's results as reported by The Motley Fool in August 2026. The segment's quarterly operating loss narrowed to 2.1 billion dollars from 3.2 billion a year earlier, same source, and the company has pointed to the end of 2027 as its target for foundry break-even, per its own guidance as reported in 2024 and since. Most striking of all was a disclosure in the company's July 2025 quarterly filing, made under Tan: if Intel cannot win a significant external customer for 14A, the node after 18A, it may pause or discontinue development of leading-edge nodes altogether, as reported by Tom's Hardware and TrendForce in July 2025. The company that defined the leading edge for four decades put in writing that its continued presence there is conditional. Whatever else that is, it is not denial.

## The government money

Then there is the state, because by 2024 Intel's problem had stopped being only Intel's problem. The United States had one company capable of leading-edge logic manufacturing on its own soil, and that company was losing billions a year doing it. What followed deserves to be set out plainly, because it has few precedents in American industrial history.

| Date | Investor | What happened | As reported by |
|---|---|---|---|
| November 2024 | US government (CHIPS Act) | Definitive agreement for up to 7.86 billion dollars of direct funding | Company announcement, November 2024 |
| August 2025 | US government | Grants converted to equity: 8.9 billion dollars for 433.3 million shares at 20.47 dollars each — roughly 9.9 per cent of the company | CNBC, August 2025 |
| August 2025 | SoftBank | 2 billion dollars of new shares at 23 dollars each | Company announcements, August 2025 |
| September 2025 | Nvidia | 5 billion dollars of shares at 23.28 dollars each, alongside a product collaboration; completed December 2025 | CNBC, September and December 2025 |

Each row rewards a second look. The government position began as CHIPS Act grants — money awarded for building fabs, with milestones attached — and was converted in August 2025 into common stock: 5.7 billion dollars of awarded-but-unpaid CHIPS funding plus 3.2 billion from a secure-chip programme, per CNBC's August 2025 reporting. The stake was structured as passive, with no board seat and an undertaking to vote with the board, same source. An accountant notices the category change immediately. A grant is support tied to specified behaviour; equity is permanent risk capital whose value floats with the share price, held by an owner that also writes the export rules, awards the defence contracts and regulates the industry. Whether that alignment is a strength or an entanglement is a question this book only flags; what is certain is that the United States government became, in one announcement, one of Intel's largest shareholders.

The Nvidia investment carries its own irony, which chapter fifteen's discussion of investments in adjacent companies anticipated: the company that displaced Intel from the Dow buying roughly four per cent of it two years later. On the day of the announcement Intel's shares rose 22.8 per cent, their best single day since October 1987, per CNBC in September 2025. Note also what all three private injections were: purchases of shares. They strengthen the balance sheet and dilute existing holders. None of them is revenue, and none of them, by itself, fills a fab.

## What would have to be true

The brief for this chapter asks the honest question, so here is the honest answer: the conditions under which the rebuild works, stated as tests a reader can check against future filings rather than as predictions. This book does not predict share prices and does not give investment advice; it gives things to watch.

First, 18A has to yield, at volume, on schedule. The early evidence is real: Panther Lake, the first processor family on 18A, was detailed in October 2025 and reached broad availability in January 2026, per the company's announcements, and its Fab 52 in Arizona was declared fully operational for 18A volume production in late 2025, per the company and Data Center Dynamics. In its July 2026 results the company said 18A wafer output had grown more than fifty per cent quarter on quarter with yields ahead of internal targets, as reported by The Motley Fool in August 2026 — a company claim about its own progress, to be read as such, but consistent with shipping products.

Second, someone large must commit to 14A. The company's own July 2025 filing made this the explicit hinge: no significant external customer, and the leading-edge programme itself is in question. This is the single clearest test in the whole story, because it is binary, public, and set by Intel itself. A committed external anchor customer for 14A would mean a fabless company had bet its own product line on Intel's execution — trust, finally banked. Watch the announcements, and watch the risk-factor language in each quarterly filing.

Third, the foundry has to reach its stated break-even around the end of 2027, and external revenue has to become a real number. Five per cent of segment revenue from outside customers, as at mid-2026, is a start and only a start. The gap between 293 million dollars a quarter and the tens of billions TSMC collects is the distance between a strategy and a business.

Fourth, everyone involved has to stay patient for years, through cycles, through administrations, through whatever the AI market does next. Rebuilding is slow precisely because trust compounds slowly; the money is the fast part.

What does the market currently believe? As at late August 2026, Intel's market capitalisation stood at roughly 473 to 476 billion dollars, with the shares near ninety dollars — against the 20.47 the government paid and the roughly 23 that SoftBank and Nvidia paid a year earlier — and the stock up roughly 359 per cent over twelve months, per stockanalysis and companiesmarketcap, both figures moving daily. Revenue in the June 2026 quarter was 16.1 billion dollars, up twenty-five per cent year on year, with data-centre revenue up fifty-nine per cent, per the company's July 2026 results as covered by The Motley Fool. That is a dramatic re-rating, and chapter two says exactly what it is: a change in expectations, not a verdict. The market has moved from pricing Intel as a fixed-cost trap to pricing it as a plausible second source at the leading edge. The filings over the next three years will say whether the expectation was right, and a reader of this book knows to weigh the filings, not the chart. For scale, TSMC stood at roughly 2.17 trillion dollars as at August 2026, per companiesmarketcap. The gap remains the argument.

## The lesson the stack teaches

Strip the story to its mechanism and it generalises across every layer of this book.

Intel's advantage was a lead that had to be re-won on a fixed schedule, held inside a structure that made winning cheaper each time — until one slip made it more expensive each time. The integrated model was genuinely superior for thirty years, and the very coupling that made it superior is what let a single process delay propagate into products, volume, unit costs, customers and, eventually, the income statement. Tightly coupled machines compound in whichever direction they are moving. That is the reading of the flow this chapter has traced: a generation of dominance, a stumble at one node, customers prudently qualifying a second source, the fixed-cost arithmetic flipping sign, and a rebuild that costs multiples of what defence would have cost — in money, in years, and now in equity sold to a government, a conglomerate and a rival.

For the rest of this book, Intel serves as the control experiment. When later chapters describe TSMC's obsession with never missing a node, Nvidia's near-paranoid pace of platform renewal, or the hyperscalers' willingness to spend through downturns described in chapter four, the unspoken second half of each sentence is: because everyone watched what happened when the leader slowed down. The companies that own this industry are not running from their competitors. They are running from Intel's example.

And the example's final chapter is genuinely unwritten. A company holding record government backing, functioning 18A fabs, a shipping product line and a re-rated share price is not a corpse; it is a live test of whether the industry's hardest thing — regaining the leading edge once lost — can be done at all. No one has done it in the modern era. The next few years of filings will show whether Intel becomes the exception or the confirmation, and either way, the numbers will carry their dates.
