# Oracle's Second Act

On 10 September 2025, shares in a forty-eight-year-old database company rose almost thirty-six per cent in a single day — the stock's biggest one-day gain since 1992, adding roughly two hundred and forty-four billion US dollars of market value and lifting the company's capitalisation to about nine hundred and twenty-two billion, as reported by CNBC that day. Nothing had shipped. No product had launched. What moved the price was a number in an earnings release: contracted future revenue, up three hundred and fifty-nine per cent in a year.

The company was Oracle, and for most of the preceding decade the polite consensus was that its best years were behind it. This chapter is about how that consensus was overturned, what exactly the market bought on that day in September, and — because this is the accountant's book — what a five-hundred-billion-dollar order book actually is, what it is not, and why building capacity against one is a bet on the customer's survival as much as on demand.

## The company that sold the filing cabinet

Oracle began in June 1977 as Software Development Laboratories, founded in Santa Clara by Larry Ellison, Bob Miner and Ed Oates, per the company's own corporate histories. The founders had read a research paper by the IBM computer scientist Edgar Codd describing a relational model for organising data, saw the commercial potential IBM was slow to exploit, and in 1979 shipped the first commercial SQL relational database, per Britannica's corporate history. The product was eventually renamed Oracle, and then so was the company.

For the next three decades, Oracle was one of the most reliably profitable franchises in enterprise software. The relational database became the standard container for corporate records — ledgers, payrolls, orders, customers — and once a company's records live in your product, they tend to stay. Databases are the classic switching-cost business: migrating one is expensive, slow and dangerous, and the vendor knows it. Oracle priced accordingly, defended aggressively, and bought its way across the application layer through a long run of acquisitions. It was, in the vocabulary of this book, an application-and-infrastructure incumbent whose scarce thing was inertia: the sheer cost, to its customers, of leaving.

Then the industry moved, and Oracle did not. Amazon launched AWS in 2006. The workloads of the next decade — new applications, new companies, new data — went to rented infrastructure, billed by the hour, and increasingly to databases that were open-source, cloud-native, or both. Oracle did not launch a credible infrastructure cloud until October 2016, when Oracle Cloud Infrastructure appeared with a single region and a basic set of compute, storage and networking services, a decade behind the leader, per trade histories of the platform. By the time OCI arrived, the market had three entrenched hyperscalers and no obvious need for a fourth. The narrative wrote itself: legacy vendor, captive maintenance revenue, managed decline. Plenty of intelligent people held that view, and on the evidence available at the time it was not unreasonable.

## Late, and therefore free

What the narrative missed is that being a latecomer, in a capital-intensive business, confers one strange freedom: you have nothing to protect.

The three large clouds each carried an enormous installed base of general-purpose workloads, hundreds of thousands of customers, and internal claimants on every megawatt — their own search, retail, productivity and advertising businesses compete with paying customers for capacity. When generative AI arrived and a handful of model companies began asking for compute in quantities no procurement department had ever written down, the large clouds had to weigh those requests against everything else they were already committed to, and against the risk of building single-purpose capacity for a customer category that might not survive. Each of them also had a horse in the model race, which complicated selling to the others.

Oracle's position was the mirror image. OCI was small, so a single enormous customer would not distort an existing business — it would constitute the business. Oracle had no frontier model of its own, which made it a neutral landlord in a market where the biggest tenants were rivals of the other landlords. And its infrastructure had been designed late enough to be shaped around exactly the thing AI training needed: very large, tightly networked clusters of identical accelerators, sold in bulk rather than by the sliver.

There was a second, quieter reversal underneath the loud one. For years Oracle had treated the hyperscalers as the enemy, and the feeling was mutual. By 2025 the company had instead placed its database — the crown jewel, the product with the switching costs — inside its rivals' data centres, selling Oracle databases that run within Microsoft's, Amazon's and Google's clouds. The September 2025 results quantified what that did: multicloud database revenue from Amazon, Google and Microsoft grew one thousand five hundred and twenty-nine per cent year on year, per the company's release as covered by CNBC in September 2025. The percentage is spectacular mostly because the base was tiny, which is a caveat this book applies to every four-digit growth rate; the strategic content is what matters. The incumbent stopped defending a walled garden and started collecting rent wherever its customers actually were. A company willing to do that with its most protected product was, evidently, no longer being run for the quiet life.

So Oracle took the risk the larger clouds would not, at the scale they would not. The strategy, read backwards from where the capital went, was simple: buy accelerators in extraordinary quantity, build or lease the buildings and power to run them, and sign a small number of very large, multi-year contracts with the companies training frontier models — accepting a degree of customer concentration that a more comfortable incumbent would have declined.

The vehicle for much of it was announced on 21 January 2025 at the White House, where the president stood with Sam Altman of OpenAI, Masayoshi Son of SoftBank and Larry Ellison to announce Stargate: a venture intending to invest five hundred billion dollars over four years in AI infrastructure for OpenAI in the United States, with OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle and the investment firm MGX as partners, per the announcement and contemporaneous coverage. In July 2025 OpenAI announced a further agreement with Oracle covering four and a half gigawatts of Stargate capacity. The flagship campus at Abilene, Texas — planned at 1.2 gigawatts — had its first two buildings running workloads by September 2025, when the partners announced five further US sites: three led by Oracle, in Shackelford County, Texas, Doña Ana County, New Mexico, and an initially unnamed Midwestern location, and two led by SoftBank, in Lordstown, Ohio, and Milam County, Texas, per the partners' announcement and trade coverage in September 2025. With those additions the partners said the project had reached nearly seven gigawatts of planned capacity and over four hundred billion dollars of committed investment, putting it on a path to the full commitment ahead of schedule, per the same September 2025 announcement. Whatever one thinks of the arithmetic, the concrete was real.

## The quarter that repriced the company

The results Oracle reported on 9 September 2025, for its first fiscal quarter ended 31 August, were on their face unremarkable: total cloud revenue up twenty-eight per cent to 7.2 billion dollars, per the company's release. What was remarkable sat two lines lower. Remaining performance obligations — contracted revenue not yet delivered or recognised — had risen three hundred and fifty-nine per cent year on year to four hundred and fifty-five billion dollars, an increase of roughly three hundred and seventeen billion in a single quarter, driven by what Ellison described as four multi-billion-dollar contracts with three customers, per the release and CNBC's coverage.

The largest of those contracts was identified the next morning, when the Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI had signed an agreement to purchase about three hundred billion dollars of computing power from Oracle over roughly five years, beginning in 2027 — one of the largest commercial contracts ever signed, as the reporting noted.

On the earnings call, chief executive Safra Catz put a five-year staircase under the number. OCI revenue, she said, would grow seventy-seven per cent to eighteen billion dollars in the current fiscal year, and then, per Yahoo Finance's report of the call in September 2025:

| Fiscal year | OCI revenue projected by Oracle, September 2025 |
|---|---|
| FY2026 | 18 billion dollars |
| FY2027 | 32 billion dollars |
| FY2028 | 73 billion dollars |
| FY2029 | 114 billion dollars |
| FY2030 | 144 billion dollars |

Analysts' reaction, captured by CNBC on 9 September 2025, was summarised by one of them as "we're all kind of in shock." The market's reaction was the thirty-six per cent day. Bloomberg's and Forbes's wealth rankings briefly had Ellison, whose stake in Oracle is large, contesting the title of the world's richest person, per Fortune in September 2025.

The deals kept coming. On 19 September 2025 Bloomberg reported that Oracle was in talks with Meta on a cloud computing arrangement worth about twenty billion dollars; Oracle confirmed a Meta cloud deal in October 2025, per CNBC, with the twenty-billion figure remaining Bloomberg's reporting rather than a company disclosure. By the second fiscal quarter, reported on 10 December 2025, remaining performance obligations had reached five hundred and twenty-three billion dollars, up four hundred and thirty-eight per cent year on year and sixty-eight billion higher than the previous quarter, with new commitments from Meta, Nvidia and others, per the company's release. The same release showed the ordinary income statement catching up more slowly: total quarterly revenue of 16.1 billion dollars, up fourteen per cent, with cloud revenue up thirty-four per cent to 8.0 billion and cloud infrastructure revenue up sixty-six per cent to 4.1 billion. That gap — a backlog compounding at hundreds of per cent while recognised revenue grew at fourteen — is not a contradiction; it is what a long-duration order book looks like while the capacity to serve it is still being poured. But it does mean the two numbers were describing different decades, and the market was pricing the far one. By the fourth quarter, reported on 10 June 2026, the backlog figure was six hundred and thirty-eight billion.

A database company written off five years earlier now carried the largest disclosed order book in the history of commercial computing. The question an accountant asks next is what, precisely, that number is.

## What a backlog is, and what it is not

Remaining performance obligations are a creature of the revenue recognition standard. When a customer signs a contract, the vendor does not book the revenue; it books the obligation, and recognises revenue only as the service is actually delivered, quarter by quarter, over the life of the agreement. RPO is the running total of what has been contracted but not yet delivered. It is a genuine, audited disclosure, and it is genuinely informative: it is the closest thing the accounts offer to a photograph of demand.

But it is not revenue, it is not cash, and it is not certain. Four qualifications matter here, and each one matters more at Oracle's scale than anywhere else.

First, duration. A backlog recognised over five or more years cannot be compared casually with one recognised over two. Google Cloud disclosed a backlog of five hundred and fourteen billion dollars in its second quarter of 2026, up from one hundred and six billion a year earlier, per reporting by The Motley Fool and The Globe and Mail in August 2026 — but each company's mix of contract lengths differs, and a dollar of RPO deliverable in 2031 is a different object from a dollar deliverable next quarter. The headline figures invite a comparison the footnotes do not support.

Second, conditionality. Contracts of this size are not retail purchases. They contain terms — ramp schedules, milestones, conditions — that the public disclosure does not fully reveal. An obligation can be renegotiated, deferred or, at a price, unwound. The reported OpenAI contract, in particular, was described in the September 2025 coverage as beginning in 2027: at the moment the market added a quarter of a trillion dollars to Oracle's value, the contract that justified it had not yet delivered its first dollar. RPO tells you what has been promised, not what will happen.

Third, delivery. Oracle can only recognise this revenue if the capacity exists to deliver it, which is why the order book and the capital expenditure are the same story read from opposite ends. The backlog is an asset only to the extent that the buildings, power and accelerators arrive on schedule and at the assumed cost.

Fourth — and this is the heart of the chapter — concentration. Of the four hundred and fifty-five billion dollars of RPO disclosed in September 2025, the reported OpenAI contract alone accounted for roughly three hundred billion, per the Wall Street Journal's and subsequent coverage. That is a majority of the order book resting on a single counterparty. Chapter one made the point that a business where four decisions determine three-fifths of revenue must be analysed differently from one with a hundred thousand customers; here, one decision determines more than half of the backlog.

So consider the counterparty. OpenAI is private and publishes no audited accounts. Its annualised revenue run rate was reported at around forty billion dollars as at August 2026 in trade coverage — a monthly figure multiplied by twelve, unaudited, per the caveats set out in chapter five. The Oracle contract, as reported, averages sixty billion dollars a year over its life. Divide one reported number by the other — arithmetic, not analysis — and the customer has contracted to spend, with this one supplier, half as much again as its entire current reported revenue, before its payroll, its training costs and its other infrastructure commitments. Fortune, in September 2025, made exactly this observation under the headline question of whether the deal signalled a bubble. It is not an accusation; both parties presumably signed expecting OpenAI's revenue to be several times larger by 2030 than it was in 2026. OpenAI has so far financed the gap in the capital markets, closing a one-hundred-and-twenty-two-billion-dollar round in March 2026 at an eight-hundred-and-fifty-two-billion-dollar valuation, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes. But the plain reading stands: Oracle's order book is, in substantial part, a claim on money its largest customer does not yet earn and expects to raise or grow into.

That is what "capacity built against contracted future revenue is a bet on the customer's survival" means in the specific. It is not a metaphor. It is the structure of the balance sheet.

## Paying for it

Now read Oracle's own side of the ledger, because the bet had to be financed before a dollar of the backlog could be delivered.

In September 2025 Oracle sold eighteen billion dollars of investment-grade bonds — the market's second-largest corporate deal of the year, issued in six parts including a rare forty-year maturity, with investor demand peaking near eighty-eight billion dollars, per Bloomberg's coverage of the sale. In December 2025, CNBC reported that Oracle's AI-fuelled debt load had investors on edge ahead of earnings. On the second-quarter call that month, the company disclosed quarterly capital expenditure of twelve billion dollars and raised its full-year capital spending expectation by about fifteen billion above what it had forecast a quarter earlier, per the earnings call transcript published by The Motley Fool in December 2025. In February 2026, Axios reported that Oracle planned to raise a further forty-five to fifty billion dollars across debt and equity during the calendar year.

The full-year picture, once fiscal 2026 closed on 31 May 2026, looked like this:

Fourth-quarter OCI revenue grew ninety-three per cent to 5.8 billion dollars, per the June 2026 release — the staircase from the September projection was, so far, being climbed. And still the market flinched: the stock fell ten per cent in extended trading on the day of those record results, per CNBC on 10 June 2026, because alongside them the company set out plans to raise yet more money, with capital expenditure for fiscal 2027 planned at around seventy billion dollars per the same 2026 reporting. Later that month, CNBC reported that Oracle's shares had just had their worst week since the dot-com unwind of 2001, as investors dwelt on the financing. Mid-2026 coverage put the company's total borrowings above one hundred billion dollars — around one hundred and thirty billion in some compilations — and reported that the cost of insuring Oracle's debt against default in the credit derivatives market had reached record highs for the company, per Yahoo Finance and Nasdaq reporting in 2026. By August 2026 the market capitalisation stood at roughly four hundred and fourteen billion dollars on companiesmarketcap — a figure that moves daily, and less than half the roughly nine hundred and twenty-two billion touched on the euphoric day the previous September.

Notice what did and did not change between those two prices. The backlog grew. The cloud revenue grew, at the promised rate. What changed was the market's assessment of what it costs, in capital and in risk, to convert the one into the other. Both prices were photographs of the same company; neither was a fact about its future.

One further disclosure from the June 2026 release deserves attention, because it shows the risk being consciously re-shared. Oracle said that most of the RPO added in its third and fourth quarters came from large AI contracts in which the customer either prepaid Oracle for the accelerators or bought the accelerators itself and supplied them to Oracle, and that these prepaid and customer-supplied portions totalled seventy-five billion dollars. Read as an accountant: Oracle was moving part of the hardware risk off its own balance sheet and onto its customers'. A contract where the customer owns the chips is a smaller bet for Oracle — and a correspondingly larger one for the customer. The risk does not disappear. It is assigned a seat.

## The shape of the bet

Strip the names away and look at the mechanism, because it will recur in the next chapter in a purer form.

Take an illustrative operator, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It borrows forty billion dollars and spends it on accelerators, buildings and power. It signs a ten-year contract with a single customer worth eight billion dollars a year. On signing day, its order book shows eighty billion — twice what it borrowed. The lenders are comforted; the press is dazzled.

Now run the two futures. In the first, the customer thrives, pays for ten years, and the operator's only real risks are execution and the useful life of the hardware — whether accelerators bought in year one are still worth renting in year six, an estimate that chapter four showed can swing reported profit by billions in either direction. In the second future, the customer fails, or shrinks, or renegotiates from distress in year three. The debt remains exactly as contracted. The buildings and chips remain. But the order book — the thing the market valued — evaporates, and what the operator actually owns is a warehouse of ageing, specialised hardware that must be re-let at whatever the market then pays, in a market quite possibly glutted by every other operator's identical misfortune. The asset was never really the contract. The asset was the customer's ability to perform it, and that never appeared on anyone's balance sheet.

Oracle is not the illustrative operator: it has a profitable, cash-generating software business underneath the build, decades of enterprise relationships, more than one large AI customer, and the seventy-five-billion-dollar risk-sharing described above. Nothing here alleges that anything is wrong; every figure in this chapter comes from the company's own disclosures or from named reporting, which is the system working. But the structure is the structure. A reader who wants to track it does not need anyone's opinion — the disclosures to watch are: the rate at which RPO converts into recognised revenue, quarter by quarter; capital expenditure against cash from operations; the customer-concentration language; the mix of prepaid and customer-supplied capacity; and the terms and volume of each new borrowing. Every one of those is published. None requires a subscription to anyone's convictions, and — as this book says wherever it matters — nothing here is investment advice or a view on any share price.

## The second act, honestly stated

The story the market fell in and out of love with inside twelve months is, read calmly, a single coherent one. A database incumbent, having missed the first cloud era by a decade, used the freedom of having nothing to lose to make itself the specialist landlord of the AI build-out — accepting customer concentration, negative free cash flow of twenty-three point seven billion dollars in fiscal 2026 per its June 2026 results, and a debt pile reported above one hundred billion dollars in 2026, in exchange for the largest disclosed order book in the industry's history at six hundred and thirty-eight billion dollars as at May 2026. Revenue for fiscal 2026 came in at sixty-seven point four billion dollars, up seventeen per cent, with cloud revenue up thirty-nine per cent, per the company's release — growth the Oracle of 2020 was not thought capable of.

Whether the second act ends in vindication depends on questions this book does not pretend to answer: whether frontier-model revenue grows into the contracts written against it, whether the hardware lasts as long as the depreciation schedules assume, and whether a company can keep borrowing at acceptable prices through the years in which the cash goes out before the revenue comes in. What can be said is where to look while it plays out. The answer, as usual, is not in the keynote. It is in the filings — dated, audited, and updated every quarter, long after this snapshot of August 2026 has gone stale.

The next chapter follows the same bet one step further out on the risk curve, to the companies that were built from nothing to make it — with rented buildings, borrowed money, and the accelerators themselves pledged as the collateral.
