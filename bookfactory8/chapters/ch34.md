# OpenAI I: 2015 to 2022

On 11 December 2015, a short blog post announced a new research laboratory in San Francisco. It was a non-profit. Its stated goal was to build artificial general intelligence for the benefit of humanity as a whole, unconstrained by any need to generate financial return. Its backers pledged a billion US dollars. Ten years and a few months later, in March 2026, the organisation that grew out of that post closed a one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar funding round at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes.

No company in this book travelled a stranger path between those two points. The story is usually told as a drama of personalities, and the personalities were real enough. But the drama sits on top of something more mundane and more instructive: a financing problem. The founders chose a legal form in 2015 that could not pay for what they later discovered they needed to build, and every structural convulsion that followed — the departures, the capped-profit invention, the Microsoft dependence, the governance crisis that belongs to a later chapter — traces back to that mismatch between the form and the bill.

An accountant reads this history differently from a journalist. The journalist asks who fell out with whom. The accountant asks a duller and more useful question: where was the money supposed to come from, and what did each new structure promise to whoever supplied it? Answer that question at each stage and the whole sequence stops looking like a betrayal of ideals and starts looking like what it was — a series of increasingly expensive attempts to keep a mission attached to a balance sheet that could carry it.

## A non-profit with a billion-dollar press release

Begin with the founding announcement itself, because its most quoted number is also its most misleading.

The launch was reported on 11 December 2015: a non-profit research laboratory, with Elon Musk and Sam Altman as co-chairs, and a founding team that included Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever, Wojciech Zaremba and John Schulman. The backers — Musk, Altman, Brockman, Reid Hoffman, Jessica Livingston, Peter Thiel, Amazon Web Services and Infosys among them — pledged a combined one billion US dollars, per the announcement as covered at the time and in CNBC's December 2025 retrospective on the founding.

Pledged. Not paid. The distinction is the first lesson of the chapter, and it is a lesson straight out of charity accounting. A pledge is a statement of intent; a donation is cash received; and a non-profit's accounts record the second, not the first. What actually arrived was a small fraction of the headline. In its March 2024 public account of the dispute with Musk, OpenAI stated that Musk contributed less than forty-five million dollars in total, with more than ninety million raised from other donors — as reported by TechCrunch in March 2024. Set that against the billion-dollar press release. The gap between what was announced and what was banked was roughly ninety per cent, and nobody had behaved improperly: that is simply what pledges are. Anyone who has audited a charity has seen the pattern, usually at smaller scale. Headlines report intentions; ledgers record receipts.

The mission, as stated at the founding, had two components worth separating. The first was a goal: build artificial general intelligence safely. The second was a distributional claim: ensure its benefits accrue to everyone, which in 2015 was expressed partly through a commitment to openness — publish the research, share the results. The name was a promise. The organisational form was chosen to fit the promise: a non-profit has no shareholders, no profit motive to corrupt the research agenda, and no investor with a claim on whatever gets built. In 2015, that logic was coherent. The laboratory's outputs were papers. Papers are cheap.

What the founders had not fully priced — what almost nobody had priced in 2015 — was that the cost of producing frontier research in this particular field was about to leave the range that philanthropy has ever reliably funded.

## The cost curve that broke the form

In May 2018, OpenAI published an analysis of its own industry's economics that, read with hindsight, is the pivot of this entire history. The analysis found that the compute used in the largest published AI training runs had been growing exponentially since 2012, doubling roughly every three and a half months — the precise figure given was a 3.4-month doubling time — against the two-year doubling of Moore's Law. Between AlexNet, the 2012 image-recognition network that started the deep-learning era, and AlphaGo Zero in 2017, the compute consumed by the largest training runs had grown by a factor of about three hundred thousand, per that May 2018 OpenAI analysis.

Sit with that number the way you would sit with a cost projection in any other business. A cost that doubles every few months is not a cost you budget for; it is a cost that restructures you. If the frontier of the field is defined by the size of the training run, and the training run's cost is compounding at that rate, then the organisations that remain at the frontier will be the organisations that can keep writing larger cheques indefinitely. A non-profit funded by donations — actual receipts in the low hundreds of millions, as above — cannot do that. Philanthropy simply does not scale on that curve. Governments could in principle, but none was volunteering. That left the capital markets, which the 2015 structure had been explicitly designed to keep out.

The people inside the organisation could see this earlier than the public could. Two events from 2018 mark the internal reckoning.

The first was the departure of Elon Musk from the board in February 2018. The public explanation, given in OpenAI's own blog post at the time and reported by CNBC in February 2018, was a potential conflict of interest as Tesla's own AI work expanded. Later accounts complicate that. Reporting by Semafor in March 2023, and OpenAI's own March 2024 statement, describe a dispute over control: Musk, on these accounts, had proposed taking charge of the organisation or attaching it to Tesla, was rebuffed, and left. Musk has contested OpenAI's characterisation, and the two sides subsequently ended up in litigation, so treat every version as a party's version. What no account disputes is the financial consequence: the largest single donor stopped donating, at the moment the cost curve was steepening.

The second was the publication, in April 2018, of the OpenAI Charter — a document that repays reading as closely as any set of accounts. Two clauses matter here. One committed the organisation, should a value-aligned safety-conscious project get close to building general intelligence first, to stop competing and start assisting it. The other stated plainly that the organisation expected safety and security concerns to reduce its traditional publishing in future. Read as a disclosure document, the Charter is the moment the openness promise of 2015 was formally qualified: the organisation was telling its stakeholders, in advance, that "open" was going to mean less than it had. Very few readers noticed at the time. Disclosure usually works that way — the important sentence is published years before anyone has a reason to quote it.

By early 2019, then, the position was this: a mission that required staying at a frontier whose cost compounded monthly; a donor base that had delivered a tenth of its headline pledge and lost its largest contributor; and a legal form that barred the obvious alternative source of capital. Something had to give, and it was the form.

## The capped-profit invention

In March 2019, OpenAI announced a structure that had no established name because nothing quite like it existed: OpenAI LP, a limited partnership the organisation itself described as a capped-profit company, sitting beneath the original non-profit. The same month, Sam Altman stepped back from the presidency of Y Combinator, the start-up accelerator he had led since 2014, to run OpenAI full-time as chief executive.

The mechanics, per the March 2019 announcement and contemporaneous coverage in TechCrunch, were as follows. Investors and employees could hold interests in the limited partnership and receive returns — but capped. For first-round investors, the cap was one hundred times the investment: put in one dollar, and your maximum lifetime return was one hundred dollars. OpenAI said it expected the multiple to be lower for later rounds. Every dollar of return above the cap belonged to the original non-profit, whose board controlled the partnership's general partner. The mission, in other words, kept legal control; the capital got a bounded claim on the upside; and the unbounded tail — the scenario in which the laboratory actually built the transformative technology it was founded to build — remained dedicated to the public benefit.

It is worth being fair to this design before examining its weaknesses, because it was solving a genuinely hard problem. The organisation needed billions; billions come from investors; investors need a return; the mission forbade an ordinary equity claim. The capped-profit structure threaded the needle by, in effect, selling a slice of the outcome distribution. An investor bought the region between zero and one hundred times their money. The non-profit kept everything beyond it. If you believe the technology's value in the success case is effectively unbounded, then a hundred-times cap gives away almost nothing that matters while raising everything that does. That was the theory, and as a piece of financial engineering it was clever.

Now read it as an accountant, and three observations follow.

First, a cap of one hundred times is a cap in roughly the sense that the sky is a ceiling. Venture investors as a class dream of hundred-times returns and almost never achieve them; a fund that returns five times its capital is celebrated. A structure that limits investors to a multiple beyond nearly all realised venture outcomes has, in practical expectation, limited very little — while purchasing, in exchange, the language of restraint. The cap's real function was less to constrain returns than to make investment in the entity narratively compatible with its charitable parent. That is not an allegation of bad faith; it is a description of what the instrument does. Structures should be read by their cash flows, not their names.

Second, the structure created a governance fault line and then built the company on top of it. The non-profit board's duty ran to the mission, not to the investors. The investors' capital sat in an entity controlled by people with no fiduciary duty to them. In sunny weather this is a curiosity. In a crisis it is a conflict with no tie-breaker: one body holding control, another holding the economic exposure, and no mechanism obliging the first to weigh the second. Every reader now knows which crisis eventually tested this seam, and it belongs to a later chapter; the point here is that the fault line was visible in the 2019 documents, in plain language, to anyone who read them the way one reads a loan covenant.

Third — and this is the observation that recurs throughout this book's model-layer chapters — the new entity was private, and would stay private. No listed equity, no statutory filings of the kind a public company makes, no audited numbers in the public domain. From March 2019 onward, everything the world would learn about this organisation's finances would arrive through what it chose to announce, what its investors chose to say, and what reporters could extract from people with reasons to talk. The reporting standard set out earlier in this book applies in full: what is known is what someone decided to disclose.

## Microsoft's first billion

The capped-profit structure was announced in March 2019. Its purpose arrived four months later. On 22 July 2019, Microsoft announced a one billion dollar investment in OpenAI, reported by CNBC that day, alongside a partnership under which Microsoft became OpenAI's exclusive cloud provider and the two companies would jointly develop supercomputing capability on Azure.

The full anatomy of this relationship — what each side supplied, how the exclusivity worked, and why a partnership between a supplier and a competitor is inherently unstable — is the subject of the next chapter. For now, note only what the 2019 deal reveals about the problem it solved, because the shape of the payment is the tell.

A billion dollars invested in a laboratory whose defining cost was compute, paired with an obligation to buy that compute exclusively from the investor, is not a passive financial investment. It is closer to a customer prepayment arranged as equity. Money flowed from Microsoft to OpenAI; a large fraction of it was always going to flow straight back to Microsoft as Azure spending. Contemporaneous coverage noted that the investment was structured to be substantially consumed as cloud services. From Microsoft's side, the deal simultaneously seeded a strategic partner, guaranteed that partner's enormous future compute bill would be paid to Azure rather than to a rival, and bought a position in whatever the laboratory produced. From OpenAI's side, it converted an unfundable cost curve into a fundable one — at the price of binding the mission's physical substrate to a single supplier's data centres.

Circular flows of this kind — investor as vendor, investment as future revenue — recur throughout this industry and this book, at steadily larger scale. They are not improper. They are, however, a reason to read any headline investment figure twice: the first time for its size, the second time for its round trip.

## Renting out intelligence

While the structure was being rebuilt, the research programme was producing the results that justified it — and, almost by accident, discovering the business model.

In February 2019, OpenAI announced GPT-2, a language model of one and a half billion parameters, and declined to release the full version, citing the risk of misuse for synthetic text at scale. It released progressively larger versions over nine months, publishing the full model in November 2019 after the feared harms had not materialised, as covered by The Register in November 2019 among others. The staged release was mocked at the time as either alarmism or marketing, and the mockery missed what the episode actually demonstrated: the organisation had begun treating a trained model as a controlled asset rather than a publication. Whatever the motive, withholding is a precondition of charging. An organisation that publishes everything has nothing to sell. The 2018 Charter had reserved the right to stop publishing; GPT-2 was the right being exercised.

The commercial consequence arrived the following June. GPT-3, a model of one hundred and seventy-five billion parameters described in a paper released in May 2020, was made available on 11 June 2020 not as a download but as a beta API — a metered interface through which approved developers sent text in and got text out, as covered by VentureBeat at the time. This was the founding act of the model layer as a business. The chapter before this one described what a model company sells; the API was where that revenue line was invented. Intelligence became a utility service: consumed by the unit, billed by the token, delivered from someone else's machine. The model itself never left the building — which meant the enormous cost of training it could, in principle, be recovered across every customer who rented access, while no customer could copy the asset.

Then, on 22 September 2020, came the arrangement that showed how much the new structure had changed the organisation's relationship with its own output: Microsoft announced an exclusive licence to GPT-3's underlying model for its own products, announced by both companies in September 2020, with the public API continuing to serve other developers. The laboratory founded five years earlier on the promise that its research would belong to everyone had licensed its flagship model exclusively to the world's largest software company. One can regard that as pragmatism, apostasy or the inevitable consequence of the 2019 financing — but as a matter of structure it was simply the capped-profit entity doing what it had been built to do: converting research into consideration.

It is worth recording what is not known about this period, because the silence is itself informative. The training cost of GPT-3 was never disclosed by OpenAI; figures that circulated at the time were third-party estimates, and this book does not repeat undisclosed numbers as facts. Revenue from the API in its first years was likewise undisclosed. A public company of comparable strategic importance would have filed segment revenue, cost of sales and capital commitments every quarter. The model layer's defining companies have never had to, and 2020 is the year that asymmetry began to matter.

## The consumer moment

Through 2021 and most of 2022, the organisation's public releases — image generation among them — kept it prominent inside the technology industry and largely invisible outside it. The API business grew quietly. Nothing about the period suggested that the company's next release would reset the clock speed of the entire industry, least of all to the company itself.

On 30 November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT — a conversational interface wrapped around its latest model, launched free of charge as a research preview. The launch was, by the standards of corporate history's turning points, remarkably casual: no pricing, no revenue model attached, a product assembled quickly on top of existing components.

What happened next has entered the general culture, so this book will simply record the numbers with their sources. The service reached one million users within five days of launch, per Altman's own contemporaneous statement, widely reported in early December 2022. By January 2023, analysts at UBS estimated it had reached one hundred million monthly active users, making it the fastest-growing consumer application in history to that point — a threshold that had taken TikTok about nine months and Instagram roughly two and a half years, per the UBS analysis as reported by Reuters in February 2023. These are estimates of usage by a third party, not audited disclosures, and should be held accordingly; but no one, including the company's competitors, disputed the shape of the curve.

Consider what that launch did to each of the four layers of the stack this book is organised on, because the effects ran through all of them at once.

At the application layer, it created the category. Twenty dollars a month for a chat assistant — the subscription came later, but the behaviour started here — became the first mass-market consumer purchase in the technology's history, and every incumbent application company suddenly had to answer a question its roadmap had not contained.

At the model layer, it converted an infrastructure business into a brand. Before the launch, a language model was something developers rented through an interface; afterwards, it was something several hundred million people had a direct relationship with. The strategic value of owning that relationship — rather than supplying a component into someone else's — reorganised every model company's ambitions, this one's most of all.

At the compute layer, it detonated a demand forecast. Serving conversational answers to a hundred-million-user consumer product is an inference workload of a kind no capacity plan had provided for, and the capital-expenditure escalation that dominates this book's compute chapters — spending that reached hundreds of billions of dollars a year across the large cloud operators by 2026, as set out in chapter one — dates its acceleration from this launch.

And at the silicon layer, it did what demand shocks always do to the bottom of a supply chain with multi-year lead times: it turned an allocation into a shortage. The scramble for accelerators that defines the industry's subsequent years begins, on any sensible reading, in the weeks after 30 November 2022.

One further effect belongs in this ledger. The launch reset the tempo of every competitor. Release cycles measured in years compressed into months; research organisations that had treated products as a distraction stood up product teams; the industry's centre of gravity moved from the conference paper to the shipped feature. That is what the clock-speed change means in practice, and it was the decision of no committee. It was the emergent result of a free research preview meeting a hundred million people.

## What the structure had become

Step back to the end of 2022 and audit the transformation, because the entity that ended the period bore almost no operational resemblance to the one that began it.

In December 2015: a non-profit, funded by donations that would total roughly a tenth of their headline pledge, committed to open publication, with no products, no revenue and no dominant supplier.

In December 2022: a capped-profit company controlled by a non-profit board; its research increasingly unpublished, its flagship models licensed or metered; its compute bought exclusively from a single supplier that was also its largest investor; and, suddenly, a consumer product growing faster than any in the history of the industry — with the pricing, the costs and the accounts of that product all invisible to the public, because nothing obliged their disclosure.

Every element of that end state traces to the financing problem identified in 2018. The compute curve made donations inadequate; inadequacy forced the capped-profit invention; the invention admitted Microsoft; Microsoft's capital bought the training runs; the training runs produced models valuable enough to withhold; and withholding — the conversion of research into a controlled, rentable asset — is what made a consumer product possible at all. The mission did not so much erode as get progressively collateralised: each stage pledged a little more of the original openness in exchange for the capital the next stage required.

Whether the structure that resulted could bear the weight now landing on it — a governance design with a fault line at its centre, a supplier relationship binding two companies that were becoming competitors, and a valuation that would within four years approach a trillion dollars on the reporting cited at the top of this chapter — is the subject of the next two chapters. The accountant's summary of the period is shorter. Between 2015 and 2022, OpenAI discovered the true cost of its mission, and rebuilt itself, twice, around the only parties willing to pay it. Everything difficult that happened later was priced into those documents. It usually is.
