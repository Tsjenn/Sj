# OpenAI II: The Microsoft Relationship

On 22 July 2019, Microsoft announced that it would invest one billion US dollars in OpenAI and become its exclusive cloud provider, with the two companies jointly building supercomputing systems on Azure in pursuit of artificial general intelligence, per the companies' announcements and the contemporaneous reporting of CNBC and TechCrunch in July 2019. One party to that agreement was a research laboratory that had been a pure non-profit until a few months earlier, and had invented a capped-profit subsidiary partly so that it could legally receive the money. The other was a forty-four-year-old software company then fighting to establish its cloud as a credible second to Amazon's.

Six and a half years later, on 27 April 2026, the same two companies announced that Microsoft's licence to OpenAI's intellectual property was now non-exclusive, that OpenAI was free to sell its products on any cloud, that Microsoft would stop paying OpenAI a share of the revenue from reselling its models, and that OpenAI's own revenue-share payments to Microsoft would be capped at thirty-eight billion dollars and run only to 2030, per CNBC and Forbes, April 2026. The most consequential commercial relationship of the period had been, formally and by mutual consent, taken apart.

Most accounts of what happened in between are told as a drama: personalities, a boardroom coup, betrayals. This chapter reads it instead the way you would read any large contract that crossed your desk. Who supplied what. Who received what. What the consideration actually was, as opposed to what the press release said it was. And why the terms had to keep being rewritten — because a partnership between a supplier and a competitor has a known failure mode, and this one followed it almost exactly on schedule. Nothing in what follows is investment advice; it is an exercise in reading a deal.

## What each side supplied

Start with the consideration, because it was odder than it looked.

OpenAI's side of the exchange is easy to state. It supplied models — the frontier systems Microsoft could not, at that point, build in-house in time to matter — and it supplied a brand. After the consumer launch of late 2022, described in the previous chapter, that brand was arguably the most recognised name in software; a share of its glow was worth real money to a cloud trying to persuade enterprise buyers that the future ran on Azure.

Microsoft's side came in three forms, and the first needs careful handling. **Capital**, first: but not a cheque. The 2019 billion was structured to be delivered over years rather than up front, in instalments, with a substantial part taking the form of Azure compute rather than cash, as reported at the time of the 2019 announcement. The same was true of the later, larger commitments: in November 2023, Semafor reported that OpenAI had by then received only a fraction of the ten-billion-dollar investment announced that January, and that a significant portion was being delivered as compute credits. An investor whose contribution is largely its own product is a very particular kind of investor. Part of every dollar Microsoft "invested" was destined to come straight back to Microsoft as Azure revenue — a circularity this book returns to properly in the chapter on circular revenue, and one worth holding in mind every time the headline investment figure is quoted.

**Azure capacity**, second: not merely credits against a price list, but purpose-built infrastructure — clusters designed around one customer's training runs, at a scale no other tenant was asking for. This was the part of the bargain OpenAI could not have bought elsewhere in 2019 at any price a research laboratory could pay.

**Distribution**, third: the asset examined at length in chapter twenty-four. Microsoft sells software to essentially every large organisation on earth, through contracts and compliance arrangements accumulated over four decades. OpenAI's models, wrapped in Microsoft's products and sold through Microsoft's channel, could reach enterprise customers years before OpenAI could build a sales organisation of its own.

Set out that way, the exchange looks clean: capital, capacity and distribution flowing one way; models and brand flowing the other. What the clean version omits is the sixth element, the one that ended up governing everything: each side was building a dependency on the other that neither could fully control. Microsoft's AI products depended on a research roadmap it did not direct. OpenAI's existence depended on machines it did not own. The rest of this chapter is the story of both sides discovering how uncomfortable that is, and paying to reduce it.

## The terms, as far as they are public

The standard caveat first, and it matters more here than anywhere. OpenAI is private. Nothing about this relationship has ever been disclosed in an audited filing by OpenAI itself; what is known is what the parties have chosen to announce and what reporters have extracted from people familiar with the terms. The figures below are the reported record, not the contract.

| Phase | Date | What was reported | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| First investment | July 2019 | One billion dollars; Microsoft becomes exclusive cloud provider | Company announcements; CNBC, July 2019 |
| Second round | 2021 | Further funding; size not formally disclosed | Contemporaneous and later reporting |
| Third round | January 2023 | "Multi-year, multibillion-dollar"; reported at around ten billion dollars, at a valuation reported at twenty-nine billion | CNBC and Bloomberg, January 2023; Semafor, January 2023 |
| Cumulative | 2023 onwards | Total commitment generally reported at roughly thirteen billion dollars | CNBC coverage of the partnership, repeated through October 2025 |

The structure underneath the January 2023 round was stranger than the headline. Semafor, in January 2023, reported the arrangement as a waterfall: Microsoft would receive seventy-five per cent of OpenAI's profits until its investment was recouped, after which the split would revert to something reflecting ownership — Microsoft at forty-nine per cent, other investors at forty-nine, the non-profit parent at two — with profit caps at each level, in keeping with the capped-profit structure described in the previous chapter. Note what that is not. It is not equity in the ordinary sense. Microsoft held profit-participation rights in an entity controlled by a non-profit board on which it had no seat — a fact that became briefly and spectacularly relevant in November 2023, and which explains why, when OpenAI restructured in October 2025, Microsoft's position had to be converted into conventional shares at all.

Money flowed in the other direction too, on terms that surfaced only when they changed. When the arrangement was rewritten in April 2026, CNBC reported — citing a person with knowledge of the deal — that OpenAI had been paying Microsoft twenty per cent of its revenue, a rate that would continue but now under a cap; and that Microsoft, for its part, had until then been paying OpenAI a share of the revenue from OpenAI models resold through Azure, which it would now stop. Two revenue shares, running in opposite directions, between a supplier and its customer who were also each other's competitor and investor and investee. An auditor mapping related-party flows here would want a larger sheet of paper.

## What exclusivity meant while it held

"Exclusive" covered three distinct things, and they aged at different speeds.

The first was compute exclusivity: OpenAI trained and ran its models on Azure and nowhere else. For Microsoft this guaranteed that the most demanding AI workload in existence — and the demand curve it dragged behind it — landed on its cloud. For OpenAI it was simply the price of the machines.

The second was route-to-market exclusivity: other businesses reached OpenAI's models through Microsoft. The Azure OpenAI Service, which reached general availability in January 2023 per Microsoft's own announcement, put GPT-class models behind Azure's enterprise contracting, compliance and billing. A company that wanted OpenAI's capability with Microsoft's paperwork bought it from Microsoft — and Microsoft kept the customer relationship.

The third was product exclusivity: Microsoft's licence to build OpenAI's models into its own products, which no rival platform enjoyed. The visible results arrived quickly in 2023: the relaunched Bing with OpenAI's models built in, announced on 7 February 2023 per TechCrunch and CNBC's coverage of the event, and then the Copilot line across the product estate. For roughly two years, the fastest way for the world's largest enterprise software vendor to ship frontier AI was a licence, and the fastest way for the frontier laboratory to reach the enterprise was a partner. Each side was renting what the other could not quickly build.

It is worth being precise about why both sides accepted terms this entangling, because the answer explains the later unwinding. In 2019 and even in early 2023, OpenAI had no alternative source of compute at the required scale on any terms it could fund, and Microsoft had no alternative source of frontier models. Exclusivity was not a preference; it was the deal's load-bearing wall. Every subsequent renegotiation happened because one side or the other had built, bought or been offered an alternative — and once an alternative exists, exclusivity stops being a wall and becomes a price one side is charging the other.

## Five days in November

On Friday 17 November 2023, OpenAI's non-profit board dismissed its chief executive, stating that he had not been "consistently candid in his communications with the board", per the company's announcement and the universal contemporaneous coverage. Microsoft — thirteen billion dollars committed, its flagship products built on the partnership — was reported to have been informed only minutes before the public, and its chief executive was widely reported to have been blindsided; Fortune's November 2023 coverage described his position bluntly, and at a later trial he testified that he was never given clarity on the reasons, per 2025 court reporting. By the Monday, Microsoft had announced it would hire the ousted chief executive and OpenAI's president to lead a new in-house AI unit. More than seven hundred and thirty of OpenAI's employees signed a letter threatening to follow them, per reporting of the letter that week. By 21 November the chief executive was returning to OpenAI, with a reconstructed board chaired by Bret Taylor and including Larry Summers and Adam D'Angelo, and Microsoft took a seat as a non-voting observer, per the Washington Post and PBS, November 2023.

The drama is well known. The accounting lesson is less discussed, and it is the reason those five days belong in this chapter. Microsoft had committed thirteen billion dollars to a counterparty over which it held no board seat, no voting control, and no veto — because the capped-profit structure had been designed, deliberately, to keep control with a non-profit board that owed investors nothing. A weekend was enough to demonstrate what that meant: the entire commercial relationship could be upended by five people Microsoft had never contracted with, for reasons it was not told. Its improvised response — hire the leadership, invite the staff — was itself a revealing valuation exercise: pressed to identify what it had actually been paying for, Microsoft's answer was the people, who were free to walk across the street. The observer seat it obtained afterwards bought information, not control. From that weekend onward, both the hedging described later in this chapter and Microsoft's insistence, in every subsequent renegotiation, on long-dated contractual rights rather than goodwill, read as direct consequences.

## The loosening

The exclusivity did not end in one announcement. It came apart in stages, each one traded rather than conceded.

| Date | What changed | Source |
|---|---|---|
| January 2025 | OpenAI announces Stargate with SoftBank, Oracle and MGX as equity funders, intending five hundred billion dollars of United States AI infrastructure over four years; Microsoft is a technology partner, and its cloud exclusivity is replaced by a right of first refusal over OpenAI's new capacity | OpenAI announcement; CNBC and TechCrunch, January 2025 |
| May–June 2025 | OpenAI agrees to buy capacity from Google Cloud, its direct model-layer rival's parent; the deal, finalised in May, is reported in June | Reuters, as carried by CNBC, June 2025 |
| March–September 2025 | Contracts with CoreWeave grow from an initial agreement of up to eleven point nine billion dollars to roughly twenty-two point four billion in total | CoreWeave's announcements, 2025 |
| September 2025 | An Oracle contract reported at roughly three hundred billion dollars over about five years beginning 2027 | 2025 press reporting, including Built In's compilation of OpenAI's cloud agreements |
| October 2025 | OpenAI completes its restructuring as a public benefit corporation. Microsoft receives roughly twenty-seven per cent, valued around one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars; OpenAI commits to an incremental two hundred and fifty billion dollars of Azure purchases; Microsoft's right of first refusal ends; its model rights run to 2032 | GeekWire, Data Centre Dynamics and CNBC, October 2025 |
| November 2025 | A thirty-eight billion dollar, multi-year agreement with Amazon Web Services for capacity including hundreds of thousands of Nvidia GB200 and GB300 accelerators | Data Centre Dynamics, November 2025 |
| April 2026 | Microsoft's IP licence becomes non-exclusive; OpenAI may sell on any cloud; Microsoft stops paying revenue share to OpenAI; OpenAI's revenue share to Microsoft is capped at thirty-eight billion dollars through 2030 | CNBC and Forbes, April 2026 |

The hinge was January 2025, and the mechanism was capacity. Stargate was OpenAI stating in public that its compute appetite exceeded what any single supplier would build for it — and Microsoft, notably, did not fight to keep the obligation. Building at that scale for one loss-making customer at preferential terms was a commitment Microsoft's own shareholders had reason to resist. Exclusivity was not so much lost as declined: each party had arrived, from opposite directions, at the conclusion that the other could not be its only counterparty.

Notice, in the table, that every loosening was paid for. In January 2025 Microsoft surrendered exclusivity but took a right of first refusal. In October 2025 it surrendered the right of first refusal but took a two-hundred-and-fifty-billion-dollar purchase commitment, a twenty-seven per cent shareholding in the restructured company, and model rights running to 2032. In April 2026 it surrendered the exclusivity of its licence, but stopped paying its own revenue share out while keeping a capped stream coming in. This is what an orderly unwinding of a dependency looks like: neither side ever gave up control without converting it into contract.

One clause deserves separate mention, because nothing like it exists in ordinary commercial drafting. Microsoft's rights had been tied, from the early agreements, to the arrival of artificial general intelligence: OpenAI's board could in effect end the arrangement by declaring AGI achieved — a contract terminating on one party's own philosophical judgement, with tens of billions of dollars attached to an undefined term. The October 2025 rewrite converted theology into procedure: any such declaration would now be verified by an independent expert panel, and Microsoft's rights to models were extended through 2032, including models after such a declaration, with its rights to research methods running until the panel verifies AGI or 2030, whichever comes first, per OpenAI's own announcement of the new terms and the October 2025 coverage in TechRadar and the trade press. From an accountant's chair the original clause was a contingency that could not be modelled, priced or audited; the revision is what happens when lawyers are finally asked to make a mission statement bankable.

## Why it could not stay still

Step back from the sequence and ask why so much renegotiation was necessary. The answer is structural, and it generalises beyond these two companies.

A partnership between a supplier and a competitor is unstable because the thing being supplied changes the balance of power between them every year it exists. Consider the specific conflicts this deal contained from the beginning. The same enterprise customer could buy OpenAI's models through Azure, where Microsoft kept the relationship and the margin, or directly from OpenAI, where Microsoft's take was a revenue share — so every sale involved a quiet contest over whose customer this was. The same knowledge worker could be a ChatGPT subscription or a Copilot seat, and could not easily be both. Every unit of capacity Microsoft built for OpenAI on favourable terms was capacity not sold to other tenants at market rates. And each party's product plans were, necessarily, partially visible to the other: Microsoft's roadmap depended on models it did not design, OpenAI's costs on infrastructure it did not price.

Any one of those tensions is manageable. What made the structure unstable was the direction of travel. In 2019 OpenAI needed Microsoft absolutely and Microsoft needed OpenAI not at all; by 2023 the dependence was mutual; by 2025 capital and capacity were available to OpenAI from SoftBank, Oracle, Google and Amazon, and frontier-quality models were available to Microsoft from elsewhere and, increasingly, from itself. By March 2026 OpenAI could close a one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, with Amazon — Microsoft's oldest cloud rival — reported as investing fifty billion dollars of it, per valueaddvc and Forbes, 2026. When a supplier's fiercest competitor is funding the supplier's biggest customer, the original exclusivity has not merely expired; the world that made it rational has gone.

Meanwhile the shareholding, the part of the relationship that survives, imported OpenAI's economics into Microsoft's accounts. In October 2025 Microsoft disclosed that its share of the losses of the company it does not control had reduced quarterly net income by three point one billion dollars, per CNBC, October 2025 — the equity-accounting mechanism examined in chapter twenty-four — and by August 2026 CNBC was reporting that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the earnings picture of the companies holding them. Microsoft, roughly a three point six six trillion dollar company as at mid-August 2026 per the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings, reports within its own income statement the burn rate of a competitor whose strategy it cannot direct. That is not a partnership metaphor. It is a line item.

## How both sides hedged

Both parties behaved, from 2024 onwards, like counterparties who expected the relationship to narrow — which is itself evidence of how each read the contract.

Microsoft's first hedge was capability. In March 2024 it paid Inflection AI a six hundred and fifty million dollar licensing fee — reported by Bloomberg and Reuters as six hundred and twenty million for a non-exclusive licence to Inflection's models plus thirty million connected to the hiring — while taking on most of the start-up's roughly seventy staff, including co-founder Mustafa Suleyman, who became head of a new consumer unit, Microsoft AI, per the March 2024 reporting and Microsoft's own announcement. The structure was itself a comment on the times: not an acquisition, but people and a licence, priced to make the start-up's investors whole. Its purpose was equally legible — a frontier-model capability inside the house, so that Copilot need not depend on any single external laboratory.

The second hedge was the other laboratory. On 18 November 2025 Microsoft announced an investment of up to five billion dollars in Anthropic, alongside Nvidia's up to ten billion, in a deal reported as valuing Anthropic in the range of three hundred and fifty billion dollars — while Anthropic committed to purchase thirty billion dollars of Azure capacity, per CNBC, Bloomberg and Microsoft's own announcement of November 2025. One transaction hedged both exposures at once: the model supply for Microsoft's products, and the demand for Microsoft's data centres, each made less dependent on OpenAI.

OpenAI's hedges mirror them exactly. Against compute dependence: the multi-cloud programme in the table above — Google, Oracle, CoreWeave, Amazon — and, through Stargate, part-ownership of its own infrastructure. Against distribution dependence: its own consumer subscriptions and a direct enterprise sales effort, competing for the very seats Copilot is sold into. Against capital dependence: investors — SoftBank, MGX, Amazon among those reported — who are not Microsoft. Each hedge was individually sensible, and each one justified the counterparty's next hedge. That ratchet, more than any falling-out, is why the April 2026 agreement reads less like a divorce than like two companies finally writing down the relationship they already had.

## What the deal teaches

Score it as a deal, since that is what this chapter promised.

On the reported record, Microsoft converted a commitment generally reported at roughly thirteen billion dollars into a shareholding valued around one hundred and thirty-five billion at the October 2025 restructuring, plus an incremental two-hundred-and-fifty-billion-dollar contracted Azure pipeline, plus a capped revenue stream to 2030, plus model rights to 2032, plus several years of product lead bought with someone else's research. Few counterparties in commercial history have extracted more from a single relationship. The caveats are the ones this book keeps insisting on: the stake is valued off private-market rounds, not audited earnings; the pipeline is only as strong as the customer's own financing; and part of the consideration Microsoft supplied — compute at preferential terms — trained the most capable competitor its software business now faces. OpenAI, for its part, got the only thing that existed nowhere else in 2019: computing at frontier scale, on credit, from the one company willing to build it — and then, once alternatives existed, bought its freedom back with purchase commitments.

For the reader, the transferable lesson is about method. A strategic partnership in this industry is not a press release; it is a set of exposures, and the exposures are legible in the accounts long after the adjectives fade. Ask who can leave, and what leaving costs each side. Ask which direction the money actually flows, net of the round trips. Then watch the line items that answer those questions quarterly: purchase commitments and remaining performance obligations, where promises either convert to revenue or do not; the equity-method line, where a private partner's losses surface; and the terms disclosed each time the agreement is amended — which, on the record above, has been roughly once a year since 2023. Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot from the reporting cited beside it, and several will have moved by the time you read this; the amendments, on current form, will not have stopped.
