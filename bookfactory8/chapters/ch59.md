# SoftBank

In February 2019 a Tokyo-based fund reported that it had sold every share it owned in Nvidia, realising about three point three billion US dollars for a profit of roughly two point six billion, as reported by CNBC in February 2019. The decision was defensible, the profit was real, and the fund's investors were pleased. Six and a half years later the same group sold a second, rebuilt Nvidia position, and used the proceeds to buy something else entirely.

Chapter nineteen followed Arm — the asset, the instruction set, the royalty machine underneath it. This chapter follows the investor. SoftBank Group is the only capital allocator in this book that has held a significant position on every floor of the stack at once: the silicon layer through Arm and later Ampere, the compute layer through the Stargate data-centre programme, the model layer through OpenAI, and the application layer through several hundred Vision Fund portfolio companies. No sovereign fund, no venture firm and no strategic buyer has been so consistently present at every level, for so long, with so much money.

That makes SoftBank the most instructive single case study available on how capital behaves in this industry. Not because its judgement was better than anyone else's — the record contains both the best and among the worst outcomes in modern venture investing — but because its judgement is unusually well documented, and because it is a listed company. A private fund shows you a return. A listed holding company shows you the accounts underneath the return: how the position was financed, what the mark did to reported profit, and what the market thought the whole collection was worth on any given day.

## The investor as a set of accounts

Begin with the structural point, because it changes how every number in this chapter should be read.

A venture fund is a closed pool. Investors commit capital, the manager calls it down, positions are held at fair value, and gains are realised on exit. The fund does not borrow against its holdings, its performance is reported to a limited audience, and the manager's own balance sheet is barely involved.

SoftBank Group is not that. It is a listed Japanese company with a share price, a bond market that lends to it, a telecommunications business that generates cash, and a portfolio of investments held partly directly and partly through the Vision Funds. It funds investments through a combination of operating cash, asset sales and borrowing. Its investment marks flow through its own income statement. Its shares are held by ordinary investors who never signed a limited-partnership agreement.

Three consequences follow, and they run through the rest of the chapter.

The first is that unrealised gains are reported profit. Under the fair-value accounting that applies to this kind of investment portfolio, changes in the carrying value of holdings pass through the income statement. When a private company in the portfolio raises money at a higher valuation, SoftBank's reported earnings rise, even though nothing has been sold and no cash has moved.

The second is that the funding is borrowed rather than called. A fund that wants to make a large investment asks its investors for the money. A listed holding company that wants to make a large investment can sell assets, issue equity, or borrow — and borrowing against a volatile listed holding introduces a risk that a fund structure does not have.

The third is that the market prices the whole thing continuously, and usually at less than the sum of its parts. SoftBank publishes its own calculation of net asset value per share, precisely because the gap between that figure and its share price is the central fact of its existence as a listed entity.

Hold those three, and the record becomes readable.

Two observations before the analysis. First, the amounts escalate by an order of magnitude across the sequence: four billion into Nvidia in 2017, thirty-two billion for Arm in 2016, sixty-four and a half billion committed to a single private company by 2026. Second, everything after November 2025 is funded substantially by debt rather than by proceeds. That is the material change in the story, and it arrives late.

## Early is not the same as right

The founding legend of this investor is an early cheque. Masayoshi Son put about twenty million US dollars into Alibaba in 2000. By 2024 the position was being wound down: the South China Morning Post reported that SoftBank expected to book about eight and a half billion dollars in its 2024 financial year from divesting more than five hundred million Alibaba shares, ending its run as the company's largest shareholder.

That is the outcome everyone remembers, and it has shaped how the investor is described — visionary, early, willing to write a cheque before the thesis is obvious. All of that is true. It is also the wrong lesson, because the Alibaba return did not come from being early. It came from holding for roughly two decades.

Set the two semiconductor episodes against it.

In May 2017, Bloomberg reported a Nvidia stake of about four billion dollars, roughly four point nine per cent. That was early in the sense that matters: before the data-centre business became what it became, before the accelerator was the scarce industrial good. The entry was excellent. The holding period was under two years. The exit in January and February 2019 realised about three point three billion for a profit of roughly two point six billion, per CNBC in February 2019.

Now the crucial correction, because several popular accounts get it wrong and the error flatters everyone involved: SoftBank did not back Nvidia at the beginning. Nvidia's early backers in 1993 were Sequoia Capital and Sutter Hill Ventures. SoftBank bought shares in a large listed company in 2017, twenty-four years after founding and eighteen years after the initial public offering. It was early relative to the AI period, not early relative to the company. The distinction matters for what the case study teaches. This is not a story about spotting a start-up before anyone else. It is a story about buying an already-public company at what turned out to be a remarkable price, and then not keeping it.

The pattern generalises into a simple statement that is worth more than most investment writing: entry timing is the part of the record that gets discussed, and holding period is the part that produces the return.

Take an illustrative pair of investors, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real person or fund. Investor A buys at ten and sells at twenty-five, having held two years. Investor B buys three years later, at forty, and holds for eight. If the asset reaches four hundred, A has made two and a half times their money on a brilliant entry and B has made ten times theirs on a late and unremarkable one. A's entry was the better decision by every measure available at the time. B's outcome is four times larger. Nothing in that arithmetic requires B to be cleverer than A; it requires B to have been able to keep holding, which is a different property altogether — and, as the next two sections show, largely a property of the balance sheet rather than the brain.

The record also contains the counter-case, and it should be stated plainly rather than skipped. SoftBank invested heavily in WeWork through the Vision Fund from 2017 onward. WeWork filed for bankruptcy in November 2023; CNBC reported a quarterly loss of about six point two billion dollars at SoftBank in the quarter that followed, and Commercial Observer reported in November 2023 that the total hit on WeWork ran to about fourteen billion dollars. There, the investor held for years and the holding did not save it, because the asset itself did not compound. Duration multiplies whatever the asset does. It is not a strategy on its own.

## What the accounts can see, and what sets them

This is where the accountant's reading adds something the narrative version misses.

Because SoftBank carries its investments at fair value with changes running through profit or loss, its reported earnings are, to a first approximation, a report on other people's valuation rounds. Watch the sequence across five quarters.

| Reported | What was reported | Source |
|---|---|---|
| November 2025 | Vision Fund gain of about nineteen billion dollars for the quarter, lifted by OpenAI | CNBC, November 2025 |
| February 2026 | A gain of about four point two billion dollars on the OpenAI holding | CNBC, February 2026 |
| May 2026 | Vision Fund gain of about forty-six billion dollars for the year to March 2026; OpenAI holding valued at 79.6 billion against a cumulative cost of 34.6 billion, a valuation gain of 45.0 billion in the year; group net profit of about five trillion yen | CNBC, May 2026 |
| August 2026 | Record net asset value of 72.3 trillion yen at 30 June 2026; cumulative OpenAI investment of more than forty-four billion dollars carried at 89.6 billion; no investment gain or loss recorded on OpenAI in the quarter, the valuation being unchanged from the fiscal year end | SoftBank's Q1 FY2026 presentation of 6 August 2026, as summarised by Investing.com |

Read that last row twice. In a quarter in which SoftBank's largest single holding did nothing at all in accounting terms, the reason was not that the business had stalled. It was that no new financing round had reset the mark. The carrying value of an unlisted holding changes when a transaction gives it a new reference price. Between transactions it sits still, and a company whose profits depend on it reports nothing.

So the reported profit of one of Japan's largest companies is, in substantial part, a function of when a private American company chooses to raise money and at what price. In the year to March 2026 that mechanism produced a forty-five billion dollar gain on OpenAI and a five trillion yen net profit. In the following quarter the same mechanism produced nothing. Neither number tells you much about how the underlying business performed in the period.

This is not a peculiarity of one company. Chapter after chapter of the 2026 reporting made the same observation about the wider industry: Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic have been reported as distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them, as CNBC noted in August 2026. SoftBank is simply the purest instance, because it holds less else. The general rule for the reader is this. When a company's earnings contain large fair-value movements on private holdings, separate them before drawing any conclusion. Ask three questions of every such line: what is the cost, what is the carrying value, and what transaction set the carrying value. All three are usually disclosed. Very few readers look.

There is a second, quieter reading in the same figures. The cost of the OpenAI position was disclosed at 34.6 billion dollars against a 79.6 billion carrying value at March 2026, per CNBC's report in May 2026. That is a large unrealised gain by any standard. It is also, precisely, unrealised. It has not been tested by a sale, it was set by a primary financing round rather than by daily trading, and it can be reset downward by the same mechanism that set it upward. The book has said this about every private valuation it has quoted and says it again here: a mark is an opinion with a date on it.

## The financing layer

Now the part that distinguishes SoftBank from every fund it is usually compared to, and the part that a chartered accountant would look at first.

By 2026, SoftBank's commitments to a single private company exceeded what its asset sales could cover. Set the two against each other. In November 2025 it sold its entire remaining Nvidia stake for five point eight three billion dollars and part of its T-Mobile holding for nine point one seven billion, directing the proceeds at OpenAI and Stargate, as reported by CNBC in November 2025. Together, about fifteen billion. Against a forty billion dollar OpenAI commitment completed the following month, and a further thirty billion agreed in February 2026.

The gap was closed with debt. In March 2026, SoftBank signed an unsecured forty billion dollar bridge facility with a twelve-month maturity, underwritten by JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, Mizuho Bank, Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation and MUFG Bank, to fund the thirty billion dollar follow-on investment and related costs, as reported by Bloomberg in March 2026. Alongside it, the group arranged a twenty billion dollar margin loan secured against its Arm shares, and in April 2026 the Japan Times reported that it was seeking at least ten billion dollars of borrowing secured against its OpenAI holding — an effort reported to have been held up by lenders' caution about lending against a private company's shares. By August 2026, Reuters reported that SoftBank faced roughly thirty billion dollars of obligations in the second half of the year.

It is worth being careful and precise about what this structure is, because the phrase "borrowing against shares" is used loosely and the mechanism is specific.

Take an illustrative margin loan, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real borrower. A holder owns listed shares worth one hundred. A bank lends fifty against them, taking the shares as collateral, and sets a maintenance threshold: if the collateral value falls below, say, seventy-five, the borrower must post more collateral or repay part of the loan. The shares fall forty per cent. Collateral is now sixty. The borrower must find fifteen — in cash, or by pledging more shares, or by selling some of the position into the very market that has just fallen. Nothing improper has occurred, no covenant has been broken in bad faith, and the lender has behaved exactly as documented. But the borrower's freedom of action has been transferred, quietly, to the share price of the collateral.

That is the structure to understand, and it is not unique to this borrower; it is standard practice among large holders of concentrated listed positions. What makes it worth setting out here is the combination. The collateral is a listed semiconductor asset whose price has been among the more volatile in the market. The use of proceeds is an illiquid private holding whose value is reset only when someone raises a round. Volatile collateral, illiquid application, fixed maturities. None of that alleges anything about anyone's conduct or solvency, and this book makes no prediction about the outcome. It simply identifies what a reader should watch: the loan-to-value ratio and how it is measured, the maturity schedule against the liquidity available to meet it, and whether the collateral can be sold in size without moving its own price.

The measurement point deserves emphasis, because it is where reasonable people disagree in public. Reuters reported in August 2026 that S&P Global had put SoftBank's loan-to-value ratio at thirty-three per cent in March, against the seventeen per cent the company prefers to cite, with the agency expecting the figure to have fallen to between twenty and twenty-five per cent by June. Two credible parties, the same balance sheet, ratios that differ by roughly a factor of two. Neither is lying. They are including different things in the numerator and valuing the denominator differently. Whenever you meet a leverage ratio for a holding company, find out what it counts before you compare it to anything.

## The discount

One more line item completes the picture of the investor as a set of accounts.

SoftBank's own first-quarter presentation of 6 August 2026 reported a record net asset value of seventy-two point three trillion yen as at 30 June 2026, with a market capitalisation of about thirty-seven trillion yen, as summarised by Investing.com. On the company's own numbers, the market was valuing the collection at roughly half of what the company calculated its parts were worth.

That gap — the holding-company discount — is not an anomaly to be explained away. It is the market's standing price for four things at once: the tax and friction of getting value out of the holdings, the debt sitting in front of the equity, the risk that the stated marks on private holdings are wrong, and the possibility that the next allocation decision destroys value rather than creating it. The discount widens when investors doubt the allocator and narrows when they do not. Market commentary through 2026 tracked exactly that: reporting in August 2026 noted estimates of the discount falling from above fifty per cent earlier in the cycle toward the mid-thirties and, on some measures, tighter still.

The reader who wants the current figure should not take it from a book. SoftBank publishes its net asset value per share on its own investor-relations pages, and the share price is public. Both move daily. Every figure in this chapter is a photograph of a moving thing, and this one moves faster than most.

## Selling the shovel to buy the mine

Now put the November 2025 decision in the frame this book has been building.

The argument of the whole book is that value has been moving down the stack — away from applications, toward the silicon that makes them possible. Nvidia was the world's most valuable company at roughly five point three to five point four trillion US dollars as at mid-August 2026, on the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings. That is the down-the-stack thesis in a single number.

In November 2025, SoftBank sold its entire remaining position in that company for five point eight three billion dollars and directed the proceeds at OpenAI and at Stargate, as reported by CNBC in November 2025. It sold the silicon layer to buy the model layer and the compute layer. It bet, deliberately and at scale, against the direction this book has documented.

The compute half of that bet is a physical programme, not a share certificate. Stargate was announced in January 2025 with a stated ambition of five hundred billion dollars of investment over four years. In September 2025, OpenAI, Oracle and SoftBank announced five additional US data-centre sites, which the announcement said brought planned capacity to nearly seven gigawatts and over four hundred billion dollars of investment over three years. Those are stated plans, not outturns, and the distinction is the whole difference between an announcement and a set of accounts; chapters in the compute part of this book deal at length with how such programmes actually get funded and built.

The model half is the OpenAI position: forty billion dollars committed in March 2025 and fully funded by December 2025 for about eleven per cent, per CNBC's reporting in December 2025, with a further thirty billion agreed in February 2026 to take cumulative investment to an expected sixty-four point six billion for roughly thirteen per cent, per SoftBank's own announcement of 27 February 2026.

Whether that is the trade of the decade or its most expensive mistake is not knowable now, and this book does not guess. It gives no price target, recommends nothing, and takes no view on the outcome. What can be said with confidence is what the trade *is*: an exchange of a liquid, daily-priced claim on the most profitable company in the industry for an illiquid, periodically-marked claim on the most valuable private one, financed substantially with borrowed money and secured partly against a third holding.

Every element of that sentence is a decision the reader can evaluate on its own terms. Liquidity given up. Pricing frequency reduced. Leverage introduced. Concentration increased. None of those are judgements about OpenAI's prospects. They are judgements about structure, and structure is the thing an accountant can assess without having to forecast the technology.

## What conviction actually costs

The usual moral drawn from this record is that SoftBank should have held Nvidia. It is a comfortable moral, and it is nearly useless, because it treats conviction as a virtue that can simply be chosen.

Look at what actually happened, and a different explanation appears. The asset SoftBank held longest was the one it could not sell. Arm was bought outright in 2016, taken private, and thereby made illiquid. When a strategic sale to Nvidia was agreed in 2020, regulators blocked it. When the company finally listed in September 2023, only about ten per cent was sold, per CNBC's coverage of the debut. Seven years of enforced holding, followed by a partial exit, produced the best outcome in the portfolio.

The assets it sold were the liquid ones. The Nvidia stakes were listed shares, sellable any morning, in a market that quoted a price every few seconds and asked, implicitly, whether the holder still wanted them. Twice, the answer was no.

That is the real finding of the case study, and it is a structural finding, not a moral one. Conviction is not primarily a character trait. It is a capacity created or destroyed by three things: the liquidity of the asset, the reporting cycle you are judged on, and the funding you have used. An investor who has borrowed against a position on a one-year maturity has less capacity to hold it than one who owns it outright, no matter how strongly either of them believes. An investor whose interim marks are published every quarter has less capacity than one whose are not. An investor holding an illiquid asset has, in effect, had the capacity imposed on them.

Which produces an uncomfortable corollary. If you want to hold something for a decade, the decision that determines whether you can is not made at the moment of doubt. It is made at the moment of purchase, in the choice of structure — how it was funded, what it was pledged against, who has to be told what it is worth, and how quickly it could be sold if someone wanted to. By the time the drawdown arrives, the capacity to hold has already been fixed.

## Reading an investor's record

Generalise it away from this company, because the technique is the point.

When you are handed any investor's record in this industry — a fund's, a listed conglomerate's, a friend's — the presented version will consist of realised gains and current marks, because those are what the accounting can produce. Four things it will not show you are worth reconstructing yourself.

| What to look for | Why it matters | Where it usually is |
|---|---|---|
| Cost against carrying value on each large holding | Distinguishes what has been earned from what has been marked | Disclosed in results presentations for listed holders; often not disclosed at all otherwise |
| What transaction set the latest mark, and when | A carrying value with no recent transaction behind it is stale, not stable | Results notes; press coverage of the last financing round |
| How each position was funded | Borrowed positions have a maturity; owned positions do not | Debt disclosures, facility announcements, collateral arrangements |
| Positions closed early | The only record of what the discipline actually cost | Nowhere. It must be reconstructed by hand |

The last row is the one nobody produces voluntarily, and it applies to every investor including the reader. Chapter nineteen made the accounting point about it in the context of Arm; the practical instruction here is simpler. Keep your own list of what you sold and when. No statement will ever show it to you.

And apply the same scepticism in both directions. A record that looks brilliant may be a record of one asset that compounded while its owner was unable to sell. A record that looks poor may contain excellent decisions taken with the information then available. In February 2019, selling a position that had just halved, at a multi-billion-dollar profit, into a market that had lost confidence in the entire category, was not obviously wrong. It was the textbook answer. The textbook was written for a world in which assets do not increase in value fifty-fold, and most of the time it is right.

## The unfinished part

The case study ends in the middle, which is the honest place for it to end.

As at August 2026, SoftBank holds the majority of a listed semiconductor company that is by some distance its largest asset, roughly thirteen per cent of the most valuable private company in the AI industry once the February 2026 follow-on completes, an equity interest in a data-centre programme measured in gigawatts, a wholly owned server-processor business in Ampere acquired for six point five billion dollars and completed in November 2025 as reported by Bloomberg, and a set of borrowings that must be refinanced or repaid on dates that are already in the calendar.

It sold the most valuable company in the world twice. It bought the language underneath every phone and could not sell it. It backed the largest private company in the industry with money it partly borrowed against the first asset. Whether that arrangement is read, in ten years, as the most decisive capital allocation of the period or as the moment leverage met concentration, will depend on figures that do not yet exist. This book will not pretend to know them, and nothing in this chapter is investment advice.

What can be taken from it now is the method. Read the cost, not the headline. Read the carrying value and find out what set it. Read the funding before you admire the position. Ask what the holder would have to do if the collateral fell by half, and whether they could choose not to. Those questions can be answered from published documents, on any investor, in an afternoon.

The record itself will keep updating, quarter by quarter, in documents anyone can read. That is the useful thing about a listed investor. The reasoning has to be shown.
