# Reading a Cloud Company's Accounts

A lorry pulls up to a shed in rural Louisiana, or eastern Oregon, or south of Dublin. Inside are racks of servers, each one holding accelerators that cost more than the vehicle carrying them. The money for those racks left the buyer's bank account weeks ago. On the day they are wheeled through the door, not one cent of that spending appears as a cost in the buyer's profit and loss account.

That is not a trick. It is the ordinary operation of accrual accounting, and it is the single most important thing to understand about the compute layer of this industry. A chip company sells a product and books the sale. A cloud company buys a product, keeps it, and then spends the next several years deciding — through an estimate, revisited annually, disclosed in a footnote — how much of it to charge against profit each quarter.

Cloud businesses are, in a specific and unavoidable sense, accounting businesses. Their reported margins are the output of an assumption. Change the assumption and the margin changes, while the cash, the buildings, the servers and the customers all stay exactly where they were. This chapter is about that assumption: where it lives, what it does, who has changed it and when, and what to watch now that the hardware being bought may not behave the way the assumption says it does.

## The day the money leaves is not the day the cost arrives

Start with the three statements and the fact that they are meant to disagree.

The cash flow statement records what happened to money. When a cloud operator buys servers, land, transformers, chillers and fibre, the money shows up under investing activities as purchases of property and equipment. It is a real outflow. It reduces the bank balance.

The balance sheet records what the company now owns. The same purchase appears as an increase in property, plant and equipment. Nothing has been lost; cash has been converted into a productive asset.

The income statement records the cost of using that asset to earn revenue. It does not record the purchase. It records depreciation — the systematic allocation of the asset's cost across the periods that benefit from it. The purchase touches the income statement only in slices, spread over years, long after the cheque cleared.

So a cloud company can report record operating profit in a year in which it consumed vastly more cash than it generated. It can also report shrinking margins in a year in which it did nothing wrong, simply because assets bought three years ago are now being charged against revenue at full rate. Neither is deceptive. Both are consequences of the mechanism.

The gap between cash out and cost recognised is wider in cloud than in almost any other industry, because the assets are enormous, the lives are long, and the spending is growing faster than the revenue it eventually supports. In the second quarter of 2026, Alphabet reported capital expenditure of 44.9 billion US dollars in a single quarter, roughly double the year-ago quarter, and raised its full-year 2026 guidance to 195 to 205 billion US dollars (Alphabet Q2 2026 results and CFO commentary, July 2026). Amazon raised its full-year 2026 capital expenditure to about 220 billion US dollars, citing higher memory costs among other factors (CNBC, July 2026). Microsoft indicated calendar-year 2026 capital spending of roughly 175 billion US dollars (company commentary on its fourth-quarter fiscal 2026 call, July 2026). These are snapshots. They were revised upward more than once during 2026 alone, and they will have moved again by the time you read this; the current numbers are in each company's most recent quarterly release.

Almost none of that spending hit those companies' 2026 profits. Most of it is sitting on their balance sheets, waiting to be charged against revenue that has not yet been earned.

## What actually goes onto the balance sheet

"A data centre" is not one asset. It is a stack of assets with very different economics, and the accounts treat them differently. Understanding the split is the first defence against the most common error in this debate, which is arguing about a single average life for a pile of things that plainly do not age at the same rate.

| Component | What it is | Character of the asset |
|---|---|---|
| Land | The site itself | Not depreciated at all |
| Shell and buildings | Structure, roof, floor | Very long-lived; decades |
| Power and cooling | Substations, switchgear, generators, chillers, water loops | Long-lived; tied to the building |
| Servers and accelerators | CPUs, GPUs, custom accelerators, memory | Short-lived; the contested item |
| Network equipment | Switches, optics, cabling | Medium-lived |
| Capitalised software | Internal tooling that meets the recognition criteria | Short-lived, amortised |
| Leased capacity | Space and power taken from third parties | Lease accounting, not ownership |

Only costs that meet the recognition criteria get capitalised: the asset must be controlled by the company and expected to produce future economic benefit, and the cost must be measurable. Directly attributable costs of getting it ready for use go in too — installation, testing, and in the right circumstances the interest cost of borrowing during construction. Repairs and routine maintenance do not; they are expensed as incurred. The line between a repair and an improvement is a judgement, and in a business rebuilding its own halls at speed it is not a trivial one.

The mix matters enormously. Microsoft said in July 2026 that roughly two-thirds of its recent capital expenditure had gone to short-lived assets, principally CPUs and GPUs, rather than to buildings and infrastructure (company commentary, fourth-quarter fiscal 2026 earnings call, July 2026). That single disclosure tells you where the accounting risk sits. The concrete is not the question. The silicon is.

## The estimate that moves profit without moving cash

Depreciation under a straight-line policy is arithmetic of the plainest kind: cost, less any expected residual value, divided by useful life. Cost is a fact. Residual value in this industry is usually assumed to be small or nil, because there is no deep second-hand market for a used AI accelerator that anyone outside the company can observe. Useful life is an estimate made by management, reviewed at least annually, and signed off by auditors as reasonable rather than as correct — because there is no such thing as correct here.

Take an illustrative cloud operator, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Suppose it places six billion dollars of servers into service at the start of a year, assumes no residual value, and depreciates on a straight line. Assume for simplicity that the assets are in service for the full year.

| Assumed useful life | Annual depreciation | Cash spent in the year |
|---|---|---|
| Four years | 1,500 million | 6,000 million |
| Five years | 1,200 million | 6,000 million |
| Six years | 1,000 million | 6,000 million |

The right-hand column never moves. The left-hand column moves by 500 million dollars a year between the four-year and six-year assumptions — money that appears as operating profit under one estimate and does not exist under the other. Continue the illustration: if the segment earns ten billion dollars of revenue and incurs 6,500 million of other costs, operating income is 2,500 million on a four-year life and 3,000 million on a six-year life. The reported operating margin is 25 per cent or 30 per cent. Same racks, same customers, same electricity bill, same bank balance. Five percentage points of margin, produced entirely by a footnote.

That is the whole mechanism. Everything else in this chapter is a consequence of it.

Two features of the accounting rules make the effect harder to see than you might expect. First, a change in useful life is a change in accounting estimate, not a change in accounting policy or the correction of an error. It is applied prospectively. Prior years are not restated, so the comparative figures printed alongside the new ones were prepared on the old basis, and the growth rate between them is not like-for-like unless you adjust it yourself. Second, there is no catch-up charge. The remaining carrying amount of the existing fleet is simply spread over the new remaining life from the date of change. The benefit lands quietly, in the current year, inside a line that also contains a dozen other moving parts.

To their credit, the large operators disclose the effect in money. That disclosure is the most valuable paragraph in the filing, and it is usually one sentence long.

## Ten years of extensions, and one reversal

Between roughly 2020 and 2024 the entire industry moved in one direction. Each change was announced, quantified, audited and individually defensible. Taken together they lifted reported profits across the sector by an amount that, in any other industry, would have been the story of the year.

| Company | Change | Effective from | Disclosed effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microsoft | Server and network equipment from four years to six | Fiscal 2023, assessed July 2022 | Operating income increased by 885 million US dollars in the three months to 31 March 2023 and by 2.9 billion in the nine months to that date (Microsoft Form 10-Q, filed April 2023) |
| Alphabet | Servers from four to six years; certain network equipment from five to six | Fiscal 2023, announced February 2023 | Expected reduction in depreciation of about 3.4 billion US dollars when announced; actual effect a 3.9 billion reduction in depreciation and a 3.0 billion increase in net income for the year to 31 December 2023 (Alphabet Form 10-K for 2023) |
| Meta | Certain servers and network assets to 5.5 years, after earlier extensions in 2022 | Fiscal 2025, announced January 2025 | Expected to reduce full-year 2025 depreciation by approximately 2.9 billion US dollars (Meta disclosure, January 2025) |
| Amazon | A subset of servers and network equipment from six years back to five, citing the increased pace of technology development, particularly in artificial intelligence and machine learning | 1 January 2025 | Expected to decrease 2025 operating income by approximately 700 million US dollars; separately, early retirement of a subset of equipment produced a charge of about 920 million US dollars (Amazon disclosures, 2025) |

Note the shape of the Alphabet row. The company guided to roughly 3.4 billion and delivered 3.9 billion, because the fleet grew during the year and the benefit grows with it. That is not an error. It is the arithmetic doing what arithmetic does.

Note also that Amazon is the company that went both ways. It had lengthened server life from five years to six in February 2024 (Amazon disclosure, 2024) and then, less than a year later, shortened a subset back to five and took a charge for kit it retired early. Two adjacent changes, opposite directions, both disclosed, both with a stated reason. Whatever else it is, that is a company revisiting an estimate rather than defending one.

And the direction of travel has not stopped. In July 2026 Microsoft said that from the start of its fiscal 2027 it would extend the estimated useful life of its data centre and office buildings from fifteen years to twenty-five (company commentary reported July 2026). The company was explicit that this concerns buildings and not servers, and that the larger consequence is on reported capital expenditure rather than on profit, because more future data centre leases would sit as operating leases rather than finance leases — and finance leases count in capex while operating leases do not. It is worth pausing on that. A change in an estimate about concrete altered the headline capital expenditure number that the whole market was watching.

## Why an extension flatters a growing company most

Here is the part that is easy to miss. The profit benefit of a longer life is not a one-off. It compounds with the asset base.

Continue the illustration, again with invented round numbers belonging to no real company. Suppose the operator buys six billion dollars of servers in year one, nine billion in year two and 13,500 million in year three — fifty per cent growth, roughly the shape of this industry in the middle of the decade. Assets are assumed in service for the full year, straight line, no residual value.

| Year | Cumulative fleet at cost | Depreciation on a four-year life | Depreciation on a six-year life | Difference in reported profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6,000 | 1,500 | 1,000 | 500 |
| 2 | 15,000 | 3,750 | 2,500 | 1,250 |
| 3 | 28,500 | 7,125 | 4,750 | 2,375 |

The gap does not merely persist; it widens every year the company keeps buying. An assumption chosen when the fleet was small is doing far more work by the time the fleet is large. And the reverse is equally true: if the assumption is eventually shortened, the correction lands on the whole grown fleet at once, not on the small one where the decision was made.

It is worth being precise about what a life estimate does not touch. It does not change cash — the cash left when the kit was bought. It does not change tax in most jurisdictions, because tax depreciation follows statutory capital allowance rules that are set by the tax authority, not by management's estimate; the difference between the two shows up as deferred tax and not as a cheque. It does not change the physical asset, its performance, its power draw, or the revenue a customer will pay for it. It changes reported profit, reported margin, earnings per share, and every multiple calculated from them.

That last clause is why the argument became loud.

## Accounting life and economic life are different questions

There are two distinct questions hiding inside the phrase "how long does a GPU last", and most of the public argument conflates them.

The first is physical and technical: how long will the machine keep working at acceptable failure rates? The second is economic: how long will the machine keep earning a price that justifies the space, the power and the cooling it occupies? The second question is the one that matters for accounting, and it is decided as much by the building as by the chip.

The evidence on both sides is real, and an honest chapter gives both.

On the side of shorter lives: the accelerator design cadence has compressed to roughly annual, so each generation faces a successor much sooner than the four-to-six-year schedules imply. Utilisation on training clusters is punishing in a way that ordinary web servers never were. And a company that keeps announcing new capacity while retiring old capacity early — as Amazon did, taking a charge of about 920 million US dollars — is telling you something about the pace of turnover in its own fleet.

On the side of longer lives: old accelerators appear to keep earning. In August 2026, on CoreWeave's second-quarter earnings call, chief executive Mike Intrator said the company had contracted 2020-vintage Nvidia A100 capacity out to 2029 "at full freight" (reported by Tom's Hardware and 24/7 Wall St., August 2026). That is nine years of revenue from a single architecture generation, contracted beyond even the six-year schedules under debate. Part of the reason is unglamorous and physical: an air-cooled A100 system draws a few kilowatts, while current-generation rack-scale systems draw well over a hundred kilowatts and require direct liquid cooling, so the halls built for the old chips frequently cannot host the new ones without being rebuilt. The old silicon is not competing with the new silicon for space. It is occupying space the new silicon cannot use.

That is the crux, and it deserves stating plainly. The binding constraint in 2026 was power and the buildings that deliver it, not the arithmetic capability of the newest part. In a world short of energised, cooled floor space, a five-year-old accelerator sitting in a hall that already has power is not obsolete. It is supply.

None of which proves that six years is right. It shows that the question is empirical, that the answer will differ by asset class and by site, and that the disclosure needed to settle it — utilisation, revenue and retirement by hardware vintage — is not published by anybody. Which brings us to the argument itself.

## The argument in public

In November 2025 the investor Michael Burry publicly criticised the large cloud operators for what he described as understating depreciation by extending useful lives, and estimated the effect at about 176 billion US dollars across 2026 to 2028, singling out Oracle and Meta as, in his estimate, having profits overstated by roughly 27 per cent and 21 per cent respectively by 2028 (reported by CNBC, 11 November 2025). His argument was that hardware on a two-to-three-year product cycle should not be depreciated over five or six years.

Handle this carefully, because the book's method requires it. What is verifiable is that the criticism was made, publicly, by a named investor, on those dates, with those figures attached. What is not established is the conclusion. The estimate is a projection built on assumptions about future economic lives that no outside party can observe; the companies concerned disclosed their changes in their filings, gave reasons, and had those judgements audited. This book does not allege improper conduct by any company named in it, and does not have the information that would be needed to do so. Nor does any of this constitute investment advice, or a view on any share price; the purpose here is to teach you where the estimate sits and how to test it yourself.

What the episode did achieve is worth more than its arithmetic. It moved a footnote into the main text. For most of the past decade the useful-life paragraph was read by auditors, a handful of analysts and almost nobody else. By 2026 it had become a line of questioning on earnings calls, and companies were addressing it directly in prepared remarks. That is a healthy development, and it is the reason this chapter exists.

The reasonable position for a reader is neither of the two loud ones. It is this: the useful life is an estimate that materially determines reported profit; it has been revised in both directions by companies with the best information available; the evidence on economic life is genuinely mixed; and therefore you should read the number as an estimate and not as a measurement, and you should look at the cash separately.

## Where to look, in order

Open a cloud company's annual report and go to these places, in this sequence. It takes about twenty minutes and it is the most useful twenty minutes in this book.

| The question | Where it is answered |
|---|---|
| How much did they actually spend? | Cash flow statement, investing activities: purchases of property and equipment |
| What do they own, and how worn is it? | Property and equipment note: cost, accumulated depreciation and net book value, split by class |
| What lives are they using? | Significant accounting policies note, usually the sentence beginning "estimated useful lives" |
| Did they change an estimate this year? | Change in accounting estimate paragraph, and the critical accounting estimates discussion in the management commentary |
| How big is the charge now? | Depreciation and amortisation line in the cash flow statement, and the operating expense note |
| What have they promised to spend? | Commitments and contingencies note: purchase obligations, and leases signed but not yet commenced |
| What is the cloud business earning? | Segment note: segment revenue and segment operating income |
| Is there revenue they have booked obligations for? | Remaining performance obligations disclosure in the revenue note |

Two derived measures are worth calculating yourself, because they are rarely printed.

The first is capital expenditure divided by depreciation. A ratio near one describes a company replacing what it wears out. A ratio of three or four describes a company building something much larger than it currently operates — and it tells you that the depreciation charge you are reading reflects a company that no longer exists, because the asset base has grown enormously since most of those assets were bought. The reported margin is flattered by that lag whether or not anybody chose the life aggressively.

The second is accumulated depreciation divided by gross property and equipment. That gives you a crude sense of fleet age. A number that falls year after year tells you the fleet is getting younger fast, which is what a build-out looks like — and also warns you that a great deal of the eventual depreciation is still in front of the company rather than behind it.

## What a cloud margin is actually made of

Segment reporting is where the estimate meets the number everybody quotes.

Amazon Web Services generated 128.7 billion US dollars of net sales and 45.6 billion of operating income in its 2025 financial year, an operating margin of about 35 per cent (Amazon 2025 results). In the second quarter of 2026, AWS reported segment sales of 42.2 billion US dollars and segment operating income of 16.6 billion, against 10.2 billion in the same quarter of 2025 (Amazon second-quarter 2026 results, July 2026). Alphabet's Google Cloud grew 82 per cent year on year to 24.8 billion US dollars of revenue in the second quarter of 2026 (Alphabet Q2 2026 results). Oracle reported that its cloud infrastructure revenue grew 93 per cent in the fourth quarter of its 2026 financial year, with remaining performance obligations of 638 billion US dollars (Oracle Q4 fiscal 2026 results, 2026). All of these are snapshots and all of them will have moved.

Now look at that 35 per cent again. It is a residual: segment revenue less allocated costs, of which depreciation on an enormous and rapidly growing asset base is among the largest. The depreciation component rests on the useful life estimate. Shorten the life and the same physical business reports a lower margin without a single customer leaving or a single price changing.

This is not a reason to distrust the figure. It is a reason to know what kind of figure it is. A cloud operating margin is a statement about accounting allocation as much as about competitive position, and comparing two cloud businesses' margins without checking that their useful-life assumptions match is comparing two different questions and calling it one.

A related trap: rising depreciation is often described as margin pressure, as though something had gone wrong. Frequently it is the opposite. It is the sound of assets bought two and three years ago finally being charged against the revenue they were bought to produce. A company whose depreciation is not rising after a build-out of this size would be the one worth asking about.

## The number that cannot be re-estimated

Depreciation policy cannot touch cash. This is why free cash flow — operating cash flow less capital expenditure — is the discipline that sits underneath all of it.

In the twelve months to the second quarter of 2026, Amazon's trailing free cash flow was an outflow of about 7.6 billion US dollars, against an inflow of about 18.2 billion a year earlier, as trailing capital expenditure rose to roughly 173 billion (Amazon second-quarter 2026 results and accompanying commentary, July 2026). Alphabet reported negative free cash flow of about 5.9 billion US dollars in the second quarter of 2026 (Alphabet Q2 2026 results, July 2026). Microsoft's total lease commitments for leases that had not yet commenced stood at 329.1 billion US dollars as at 30 June 2026, up from 196.6 billion in the prior comparative, with those leases scheduled to begin between fiscal 2027 and fiscal 2033 (Microsoft fiscal 2026 filing, reported July 2026).

Resist the obvious conclusion in both directions. Negative free cash flow is not evidence of a company in trouble; it is what building capacity ahead of revenue looks like, and a company that refused to build in a market that was capacity-constrained would be making a different and possibly worse mistake. Nor is it evidence that everything is fine. It is simply the one figure in the accounts that no estimate can improve. If reported profits are rising while free cash flow is deeply negative for several consecutive years, you do not need to accuse anybody of anything. You need only observe that the profit is a claim about the future and the cash outflow is a fact about the present, and hold both in view.

## What is no longer on the balance sheet

The final development a reader of these accounts must know about is that a growing share of the build-out is being financed in ways that keep the assets, and more importantly the debt, out of the operator's own balance sheet.

In October 2025, Meta announced a joint venture with funds managed by Blue Owl Capital to develop its Hyperion data centre campus in Richland Parish, Louisiana. The Blue Owl funds took an eighty per cent interest and Meta twenty per cent, with Meta providing construction and property management services; the Blue Owl funds made a cash contribution of approximately seven billion US dollars and Meta received a one-time distribution of approximately three billion, with the project financed by roughly 27 billion US dollars of debt raised through a special purpose vehicle (Meta press release and CNBC, October 2025). Oracle, meanwhile, indicated it expected to raise in the region of forty billion US dollars of debt and equity in its 2027 financial year to fund its infrastructure commitments (Oracle Q4 fiscal 2026 commentary, 2026).

There is nothing improper about any of this. Joint ventures and project finance are ordinary instruments, the arrangements were announced publicly, and the accounting for them follows consolidation rules that are neither new nor obscure. The point for a reader is narrower and entirely practical: the headline capital expenditure figure is becoming a less complete description of what a cloud operator has committed to. Some capacity arrives as owned assets, some as finance leases inside capex, some as operating leases outside it, and some through vehicles in which the operator holds a minority interest and the debt sits with somebody else.

Which means the commitments note has been promoted. It used to be the dullest page in the filing. It is now where a material part of the obligation lives, and if you read only the cash flow statement you will understate what a cloud company has undertaken to build and to pay for.

## Five questions, and a warning about dates

If you take nothing else from this chapter, take these five questions and ask them of any cloud business, in any year.

What useful lives are assumed, for which classes of asset, and have any of them changed in the last three years? If one changed, how much profit did the change produce, and was that disclosed in money? What is capital expenditure as a multiple of depreciation, and therefore how much of the eventual charge is still ahead? What did free cash flow do while reported profit was doing whatever it did? And what is in the commitments note that is not in the capital expenditure line?

Answer those and you will understand a cloud company better than most of the commentary you will read about it.

Every figure in this chapter carries a date because every figure in this chapter is already ageing. Capital expenditure guidance for 2026 was revised upward repeatedly during 2026 itself. Useful-life assumptions changed at four major operators within four years, in both directions. The current numbers are always in the most recent quarterly release and the most recent annual report, and the relevant paragraphs are the ones this chapter has just taught you to find. None of this is a recommendation to buy or sell anything, and this book never offers one.

The reason this particular estimate deserves the attention is that it is the rare place where an accounting judgement and a physical fact must eventually meet. A useful life is a forecast about machines. Machines do not negotiate. Whatever the footnote says, the racks will run for as long as they earn their power and their floor space and not one quarter longer, and the accounts will find out in due course which estimate was closer. Your job as a reader is not to know the answer in advance. It is to know that the question is being asked inside the profit figure, and to read that figure accordingly.
