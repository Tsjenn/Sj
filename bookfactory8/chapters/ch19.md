# Arm and SoftBank

There is a story in circulation that SoftBank was an early backer of Nvidia — that Masayoshi Son saw the future of the GPU before anyone else and planted money in the company when it was young. It is repeated at conferences and in comment sections, and it is wrong. Nvidia's backers at its founding in 1993 were Sequoia Capital and Sutter Hill Ventures, two Silicon Valley venture firms. SoftBank was nowhere near it. SoftBank's involvement with Nvidia came a quarter of a century later, lasted less than two years the first time, and ended — both times — with a sale that looked sensible on the day and expensive in hindsight.

The record matters because the correction points at a better story. SoftBank's foundational semiconductor bet was not a stake in a chip company at all. It was the outright purchase, in 2016, of a company that has never manufactured a chip in its life and never intends to: Arm. Understanding why that purchase was a different kind of bet — and why SoftBank held it through a collapsed sale, a public listing and two separate exits from Nvidia — is the subject of this chapter. It is also, for an accountant, one of the cleanest case studies available in the difference between owning an asset and owning shares, and in what an exit actually costs.

## Twelve engineers and a turkey barn

Arm was incorporated in November 1990 as Advanced RISC Machines Ltd, a joint venture between three companies with three very different contributions. Acorn Computers, the British maker of the BBC Micro, supplied the twelve engineers who had designed its RISC processor. Apple supplied the cash — it wanted a low-power chip for the Newton handheld. VLSI Technology supplied the design tools. The company's own official history records the arrangement in exactly those terms: Apple the money, VLSI the tools, Acorn the people. The first chairman and chief executive, Robin Saxby, joined in 1991, and the early team worked out of a converted turkey barn in Cambridgeshire.

The barn is a good detail, but the founding structure is the important one. Arm began life with no factory, no product it could sell in a box, and a customer base consisting largely of its own shareholders. What it had was an instruction set — the vocabulary of operations a processor understands — and a set of processor designs that implemented it with unusually low power consumption. Because the company was too small to manufacture anything, it did the only thing available to it: it licensed the designs to anyone who would pay, and took a small royalty on every chip that shipped.

Necessity became the business model, and the business model became the moat. Low power mattered little on the desktop, where Intel's x86 ruled and the electricity came out of a wall. It mattered enormously in anything that ran on a battery. When the mobile phone arrived, and then the smartphone, the industry standardised on Arm's architecture, and the standard fed on itself: software written for Arm needed Arm-compatible chips, which produced more Arm software. By the company's own figures, reported by Tom's Hardware in 2025 on the fortieth anniversary of the original ARM1 processor, more than two hundred and fifty billion Arm-based chips had shipped cumulatively, and Arm's designs were in over ninety-nine per cent of the world's smartphones per the company's figures cited in 2025 trade coverage. There is no meaningful second architecture in mobile. There is Arm, and there is the open-source RISC-V project trying to become what Arm already is.

## What it means to own an instruction set

It is worth pausing on why this asset is different in kind from the assets that dominate the rest of this part of the book.

TSMC's asset is capacity: fabs that cost tens of billions and depreciate from the day they open. Nvidia's asset is a designed product that must be re-earned every generation, plus the software ecosystem around it. Arm's asset is closer to a language. A company that licenses the Arm architecture and builds a chip around it has committed its software, its toolchains, its engineers' habits and its customers' expectations to that vocabulary. Leaving is not a purchasing decision; it is a migration, measured in years and risk. The switching cost sits with the customer, permanently, and grows with every product the customer ships.

The accounts that follow from this are unlike anything else in semiconductors. Arm's revenue for the year ended 31 March 2025 was four billion and seven million US dollars — the first time the company had crossed four billion in a year, per its results announced in May 2025. Of that, roughly two point one seven billion was royalty revenue, up twenty per cent year on year, and roughly one point eight four billion was licensing and other revenue. Set that four billion against the scale of what sits on top of it: every smartphone, most of the embedded world, and a growing share of the data centre. Arm captures a sliver of the value of each chip built on its architecture — and a chip is itself a sliver of the value of the device around it. The result is a company whose economic footprint is colossal and whose revenue is, by the standards of this book, almost quaint. Nvidia recognises more revenue in a fortnight than Arm does in a year, on the fiscal 2026 figures both companies have published.

The two revenue lines behave differently enough that they deserve a table.

|---|---|---|
| What is sold | The right to use an architecture or a specific design | A payment per chip actually shipped |
| When it is paid | Up front or over the licence term, before any product exists | Years later, when the customer's product succeeds |
| What drives it | Customers starting new design projects | Customers' unit volumes and chip prices |
| What it tells you | Demand for future products, two to four years out | The installed success of decisions made years ago |
| Character | Lumpy, negotiated, forward-looking | Recurring, formulaic, backward-looking |

For a reader trained on the earlier chapters, the royalty line is the interesting one. It is revenue with almost no associated cost of sale — the design work was expensed years earlier — so it behaves less like product revenue and more like a toll. It is also a uniquely honest indicator. A licence can be signed out of optimism. A royalty is paid only because somebody, somewhere, actually shipped a chip. When Arm's royalty revenue accelerates, as it did in fiscal 2025 on the adoption of the Armv9 architecture and the company's compute subsystems, that is the sound of old decisions paying off, per the results coverage of May 2025.

The overall shape of the accounts follows. Arm's gross margin for fiscal 2025 was about ninety-seven per cent, per its results for the year ended 31 March 2025 — a figure that would be a misprint at a chip manufacturer, and at Arm is simply a description of the business. There is almost nothing in cost of revenue because there is almost nothing to make. The money is spent one line further down: research and development expense of roughly two point zero seven billion dollars in fiscal 2025, per the same results — more than half of revenue, and about twice the company's selling, general and administrative costs of roughly nine hundred and eighty-four million. Read as a machine, Arm is an R&D department with a toll booth attached. The tolls fund the next generation of designs; the designs, if they are adopted, become the next decade's tolls. The company's real capital expenditure is salaries, and its real inventory is engineers' time — neither of which appears on the balance sheet, for reasons chapter three explained.

### Three ways to buy the language

"Licensing" covers three quite different transactions, and telling them apart helps with everything else in this chapter.

At the simplest tier, a customer licenses a finished processor design — a core — and drops it into their chip much as they would any purchased component. Most of the world's microcontrollers and mid-range devices are built this way. One tier up, a customer takes Arm's designs and works with the company to tailor them, and more recently buys the pre-integrated compute subsystems that bundle cores, interconnect and supporting blocks into something close to a ready-made chip floorplan. At the top sits the architecture licence: the customer buys the right to the instruction set itself and designs its own cores from scratch, compatible with Arm software but owing nothing to Arm's implementations. Apple, whose custom silicon has led the industry in performance per watt for years, is the canonical architecture licensee — a status with roots in its position as one of Arm's three founding shareholders in 1990.

The tiers matter because they define who owes what to whom. A core licensee depends on Arm's engineering every generation. An architecture licensee depends only on the language — and pays for the privilege of independence. Notice the elegance of the trap: the better a customer becomes at designing its own Arm-compatible cores, the more its software estate commits it to the architecture, and the harder it becomes to leave the language it no longer needs help speaking. Arm's most independent customers are, in the way that counts, its most captive.

The corresponding weakness is just as structural. A toll on everyone is a fortune only if the toll is meaningful, and Arm's historic royalty rates — set in an era when it was a small British firm negotiating with giants — were thin. Much of the company's story since 2016 has been the attempt to raise the take: newer architectures carry higher royalty rates, and the compute-subsystem strategy sells customers a larger, pre-integrated block of design in exchange for a larger slice of the chip's value. Whether the customers who benefit from Arm's neutrality will tolerate a more expensive Arm is one of the open questions the industry gets to answer this decade.

## The 2016 purchase

On 18 July 2016, SoftBank announced an agreed offer for Arm Holdings at seventeen pounds per share in cash — twenty-four point three billion pounds, about thirty-two billion US dollars, a forty-three per cent premium to the previous close, as reported by Forbes and Bloomberg at the time. The acquisition completed on 5 September 2016, per SoftBank's own announcement, and the largest technology company on the London market became a private, wholly owned subsidiary of a Japanese conglomerate.

Two things about the deal repay attention.

The first is the timing. July 2016 was three weeks after the Brexit referendum, with sterling at multi-decade lows against the dollar and yen; a yen-funded buyer was purchasing a dollar-earning business through a discounted currency. Son's stated rationale was not currency but the "Internet of Things" — the thesis that tens of billions of connected devices would all need low-power processors, and that the company whose architecture they defaulted to would sit under all of them. Read a decade later, the thesis was directionally right and specifically wrong. The connected devices came, but the money came from somewhere else entirely: the data centre, where Arm-based server chips and Nvidia's Arm-based Grace processors gave the architecture its second act. It is a recurring pattern in this industry that the asset outperforms the thesis it was bought on. Buying the bottleneck can work even when the story attached to it does not.

An accountant will also notice what a thirty-two billion dollar purchase of a company like Arm does to a balance sheet. Arm's value sits in its architecture, its licence contracts and its engineers — assets that, under the accounting rules chapter three set out, largely do not appear on Arm's own books, because internally generated intangibles are expensed as they are created. When an acquirer pays thirty-two billion for a business like that, the gap between the price and the identifiable net assets lands on the acquirer's balance sheet as goodwill and acquired intangibles, to be carried and tested rather than depreciated away on any natural schedule. The purchase converts an invisible asset into a very visible one — and makes the buyer's balance sheet, for the first time, state an opinion about what the instruction set is worth. Every year such a carrying value survives its impairment test, the accounts quietly reassert the acquisition thesis; a write-down is how they would confess it had failed. Given where Arm listed in 2023 and traded afterwards, the 2016 opinion has aged unusually well.

The second is what taking Arm private did to its visibility. From September 2016 to September 2023, one of the most strategically important companies in the world published no quarterly accounts of its own. Its numbers surfaced only as fragments inside SoftBank's disclosures. Chapter five argued that a company with no published accounts must be read through whoever reports on it; for seven years, that was true of the company underneath essentially every phone on earth.

## The Nvidia episode, told properly

Now the thread this chapter exists to straighten out.

In May 2017, Bloomberg reported that SoftBank had built a stake in Nvidia of about four billion US dollars — roughly four point nine per cent of the company, making it the fourth-largest shareholder. The size was not an accident of conviction. A five per cent holding in a US-listed company triggers a public disclosure; four point nine per cent does not. That August, SoftBank transferred the position into the newly raised Vision Fund, the largest venture-capital vehicle ever assembled — ninety-three billion dollars at its first close in May 2017, including forty-five billion from Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund and twenty-eight billion from SoftBank itself, per SoftBank's announcement of the close.

Then, in January and February 2019, the Vision Fund sold the entire position — about three point three billion dollars realised, for a profit of roughly two point six billion, as reported by CNBC in February 2019. The context makes the decision comprehensible. Nvidia's share price had roughly halved in late 2018 when the cryptocurrency-mining boom collapsed and left the channel stuffed with unsold gaming cards. The fund crystallised a large, fast profit on a position that had just demonstrated how volatile it could be. On any conventional venture-fund scorecard, a multi-billion-dollar gain inside two years is a triumph, and it was booked as one.

What it became in hindsight is a different number. By June 2024, Fortune reported that the stake the Vision Fund sold in early 2019 would have been worth on the order of one hundred and sixty billion dollars at Nvidia's then price. That figure has kept moving with the share price and will have moved again by the time you read this; the point is its scale, not its precision. The realised profit and the foregone one differ by roughly a factor of sixty.

Sit with the accounting of that for a moment, because it is the chapter's first lesson. The two point six billion dollar profit appeared in the Vision Fund's results, was reported, and was praised. The hundred-and-sixty-billion-dollar absence appears nowhere. There is no line item for it. No accounting framework on earth records the gain you did not stay for, which is precisely why exits look cheaper than they are. The only place an exit's true cost ever shows up is in a comparison nobody is obliged to publish.

SoftBank, it should be said, was not finished with Nvidia — but the second act belongs a few pages further on, because between the two Nvidia episodes came the strangest passage in Arm's corporate life.

## The sale that regulators stopped

In September 2020, SoftBank agreed to sell Arm to Nvidia for about forty billion dollars in cash and Nvidia shares, per Nvidia's announcement — at the time, the largest semiconductor deal ever attempted. For SoftBank it promised a profit on the 2016 purchase plus continuing exposure through the share component. For Nvidia it promised the instruction set underneath the whole mobile world, joined to the dominant position in AI accelerators.

Which is roughly why it could not be allowed to happen. Arm's value rests on a specific and unusual property: neutrality. It licenses to everyone — to Apple and Qualcomm, to MediaTek and Samsung, to Amazon designing its own server chips — and competes with none of them. An Arm owned by Nvidia would have put every one of those licensees in the position of sourcing their architecture from a direct competitor. The US Federal Trade Commission sued to block the acquisition in December 2021, European and British regulators raised their own objections, and on 8 February 2022 Nvidia and SoftBank announced the deal's termination, citing significant regulatory challenges, per Nvidia's announcement and the FTC's statement. SoftBank kept a one point two five billion dollar break fee, which it recorded in earnings, as reported by Forbes in February 2022.

The price deserves a dry footnote in the main text. The deal was struck at about forty billion dollars in September 2020; nineteen months of regulatory failure later, Arm listed at a valuation of about fifty-four and a half billion, and the retained stake was subsequently marked far higher still. The regulators who blocked SoftBank's exit did its shareholders a considerable favour, which is not what either party said at the time.

The episode is worth filing next to the licensing table above. The property that made Arm valuable enough for Nvidia to want — its position underneath everyone — is the same property that made it impossible for Nvidia to have. Some assets are worth most precisely because no strategic buyer is permitted to own them. For such an asset there is really only one exit, and SoftBank promptly took it.

## The second listing and the second exit

Arm returned to the public markets on 14 September 2023, listing on Nasdaq — not London, to the loudly reported disappointment of the UK government — at fifty-one dollars per share, a valuation of about fifty-four and a half billion dollars. The offering raised about four point eight seven billion dollars for SoftBank, which sold only around ten per cent of the company and retained roughly ninety per cent, per CNBC's coverage of the debut. The shares closed their first day near sixty-four dollars.

Note what kind of exit this was: a partial one, priced above the 2016 purchase but structured to keep the asset. The market then re-priced the retained ninety per cent in SoftBank's favour. By August 2026 Arm's market capitalisation stood at roughly two hundred and sixty to three hundred billion US dollars depending on the day and the tracker — companiesmarketcap showed about two hundred and sixty billion in August 2026, with other services recording higher marks earlier in the summer. Every figure in that sentence is a photograph, and Arm's photographs move more than most; the stock has been among the more violently re-rated names of the AI period. But on any reading, the thirty-two billion dollar purchase of 2016 sits under a listed position worth several multiples of that a decade later, with the royalty machine still attached.

Meanwhile SoftBank had quietly walked back into Nvidia. Having exited in 2019, the company rebuilt a position over the following years that reached thirty-two point one million shares — worth about three billion dollars as of the March 2025 quarter, per CNBC. In October 2025 it sold all of it, for five point eight three billion dollars, announcing the sale in November 2025 alongside the sale of part of its T-Mobile stake for nine point one seven billion, with the proceeds directed at its investment in OpenAI and the Stargate data-centre project, as reported by CNBC in November 2025. SoftBank had agreed in March 2025 to invest up to forty billion dollars in OpenAI, and CNBC reported in December 2025 that the final tranche had been funded, completing the largest private financing commitment the AI industry had then seen. Stargate itself — the data-centre programme SoftBank backs alongside OpenAI and Oracle — was announced in January 2025 with a stated ambition of five hundred billion dollars of investment over four years.

So the two Nvidia episodes rhyme. In 2019, SoftBank sold Nvidia to shore up a strategy; in 2025 it sold Nvidia to fund one. Both sales were profitable. Both converted the industry's most explosive equity into cash for something SoftBank believed in more. And note the layer transition in the second one: SoftBank sold shares in the semiconductor layer to buy exposure to the model layer and the compute layer. This book has argued that value has been flowing down the stack. The most aggressive capital allocator in the industry spent 2025 betting the other way. One of those positions will look, in time, the way the 2019 sale looks now; this book does not predict which, and is not investment advice.

## What an exit costs

Strip the personalities out and the chapter reduces to a comparison between two ways of holding the semiconductor layer.

Holding shares in a chip company is a claim on a price. The price is set daily by other people, it embeds a forecast, and it hands you a permanent, liquid invitation to leave. Every trading day asks the question: still sure? SoftBank answered that question honestly twice, and both answers were "no — there is something better to do with the money." Liquidity is usually described as an advantage, and for most purposes it is. But liquidity is also temptation with a settlement date. The Vision Fund's 2019 sale was not a blunder by the standards of any information available in 2019; it was a rational response to a halved share price and a fund with other uses for capital. The cost only materialised because the asset later did something almost no asset has ever done. That is the uncomfortable honest version of the lesson: conviction is easy to praise in hindsight and expensive to hold in real time, and the cost of an exit is unknowable at the moment of exit — which is exactly when the decision must be made.

It is worth seeing why a professional fund is built to make exactly this kind of sale. Take an illustrative fund, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real fund: it buys a stake for four billion, and two years later the stake is worth six and a half billion after a stomach-churning drawdown along the way. Selling books a two-and-a-half-billion gain and an annualised return above twenty-five per cent — the kind of number that raises the next fund. Holding adds risk to the current fund's reported performance for a payoff that, if it comes at all, may arrive after the people who made the decision have moved on. Every incentive in the structure — management fees on new funds, carried interest crystallised on realised gains, investors who judge interim marks — points toward taking the profit. The illustrative fund sells, correctly by its own rules. If the asset then rises fifty-fold, the rules do not record a mistake, because the rules cannot see one. Nothing in that example is a criticism of any real fund; it is a description of what fund accounting is able to measure, and the reader should assume every fund they encounter is governed by some version of it.

There is a second cost of exiting that the fund structure hides even better: position. A holder of four point nine per cent of a company is a phone call that gets returned, a potential partner in whatever the company does next. SoftBank in 2019 did not just give up Nvidia's future share price; it gave up being a significant shareholder of what became the most valuable company on earth during the years when that company was deciding whose data centres to fill, whose start-ups to back and whose projects to join. What that seat would have been worth to a conglomerate assembling an AI portfolio is not calculable, which is the point. The intangible costs of an exit are even more invisible than the financial ones.

Owning Arm outright was the opposite structure. No daily price, no liquid exit, no invitation. When the one strategic sale available was forbidden by regulators, SoftBank was, in effect, forced to keep the asset — and the forced holding turned out to be the best trade of the lot. There is a genuinely humbling accounting point buried in that. The discipline SoftBank could not apply to its Nvidia shares — never selling — was applied to Arm by circumstance: first by the illiquidity of private ownership, then by regulators, and even at the IPO the structure sold a tenth and kept nine-tenths. The instruction set stayed; the shares went. If you want a one-line summary of SoftBank's quarter-century in semiconductors, that is it.

The general form of the lesson belongs to the reader, not to SoftBank. When you evaluate any investor's record in this industry — a fund's, a company's, your own — the published numbers will show realised gains, because realised gains are what accounting can see. The record that matters also contains the positions closed early, and that record is invisible unless someone reconstructs it. Reported profit is a fact. Opportunity cost is a counterfactual. The whole art of reading an exit is refusing to let the fact obscure the counterfactual — while remembering, fairly, that the person who made the decision could see neither.

And the asset itself rolls on, indifferent to who owns it. Somewhere in the time it took to read this chapter, several million more Arm-based chips shipped, each one paying its small toll to a company started by twelve engineers in a barn. Whoever holds the shares, the language underneath the industry keeps collecting.
