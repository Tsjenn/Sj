# Amazon

In the last week of July 2026, Amazon put two facts about artificial intelligence into the public record within days of each other. The first, reported by The Next Web and Techstrong.ai in late July 2026, was that the company was winding down most of its own Nova model family — Premier, Omni, Reel and Canvas — to concentrate its researchers and its scarce compute on a single frontier model, expected at its re:Invent conference in the autumn. The second, contained in a filing on 31 July 2026 and reported by PYMNTS, was that it had completed a fifty-billion-dollar investment in OpenAI: fifteen billion dollars funded in the first quarter of 2026, thirteen point seven billion in the second, and the remaining twenty-one point three billion after 30 June.

Set those side by side and you have the whole chapter. In the same month, the company retreated at the model layer and paid a sum larger than most national defence budgets to own a piece of somebody else's model layer. That is not incoherence. It is a strategy, and it is the only one of its kind in this book: Amazon holds more rentable computing than any company on earth, has the least distinguished record at the research frontier of the five giants in this part, and has responded by buying its way into the two laboratories that matter most. It is now a shareholder in both.

The position the market puts on this arrangement, as at 21 August 2026, was a market capitalisation of roughly two point eight trillion US dollars, per companiesmarketcap and the Motley Fool ranking of the largest companies for that month — fifth in the world, behind Nvidia at roughly five point three to five point four trillion, Apple at roughly four point four one trillion, Alphabet at roughly four point two trillion and Microsoft at roughly three point six six trillion, same period, same sources. Every one of those numbers is a photograph and will have moved. The interesting question is not the level. It is that the company holding the industry's largest physical estate is priced below three companies that hold less of it.

An earlier chapter, in the compute part of this book, read Amazon Web Services on its own terms: the 2006 launch, the price cuts, the segment table, the margin machinery. This chapter reads the company from the top of the stack down — from the place where a shopper, a Prime member or an advertiser actually parts with money — and takes up the two matters that chapter deliberately left standing: what it means to own every floor except the one everybody is talking about, and what it means to be a large shareholder in both leading laboratories at once.

## The application layer, where the money actually arrives

Start where a user pays, because in Amazon's case that is a shop.

In the second quarter of 2026, Amazon's net sales rose twenty per cent to two hundred point six billion dollars — the first time the company had crossed two hundred billion in a quarter — against one hundred and sixty-seven point seven billion in the second quarter of 2025, per the company's results of 30 July 2026 and the coverage of them. The North America segment alone produced one hundred and sixteen point two billion of that, up sixteen per cent. Group operating income was twenty-seven point five billion against nineteen point two billion a year earlier. For the full year 2025, the company had reported seven hundred and sixteen point nine billion dollars of net sales and seventy-seven point seven billion of operating income, per its fourth-quarter 2025 results published in February 2026.

Those numbers make one point immediately, and it is a point about the application layer generally. The overwhelming majority of Amazon's revenue comes from selling physical objects and delivering them, at margins that would embarrass a software company. The overwhelming majority of its profit comes from renting computers. In 2025, AWS supplied forty-five point six billion of the group's seventy-seven point seven billion of operating income on one hundred and twenty-eight point seven billion of the group's seven hundred and sixteen point nine billion of sales — roughly three-fifths of the profit from under a fifth of the revenue, per the same February 2026 results. In the second quarter of 2026 the split sharpened: sixteen point six billion of AWS operating income out of twenty-seven point five billion group total, on forty-two point two billion of AWS sales, per the July 2026 results.

So Amazon's application layer is enormous, famous, and — in profit terms — the junior partner. With one exception, and the exception is the most interesting line in the company.

### Advertising: the application-layer business that already works

Amazon's advertising services revenue reached nineteen point eight billion dollars in the second quarter of 2026, up twenty-six per cent from fifteen point seven billion in the same quarter of 2025, per the company's July 2026 disclosure as reported by PPC Land, MediaPost and Variety. For the full year 2025 the line passed sixty-eight billion dollars, with the fourth quarter alone at twenty-one point three billion, up twenty-two per cent, per Marketing Dive's reporting of the February 2026 results.

An accountant looks at that line differently from a marketer. Sixty-eight billion dollars of advertising revenue arriving inside a retailer is not really retail revenue at all. It is a fee charged to suppliers for position — for appearing higher in a search result on a site where the customer has already arrived with a credit card and an intention. Its cost of delivery is close to nil, because the traffic was going to be there anyway. Amazon does not break out the segment's profitability, so no outsider can state its margin, and this book will not estimate one. But note the structure: the company sells the ranking of its own shelves, on its own property, to the people who want to be on those shelves. Whatever margin that carries, it is not the margin of a warehouse.

It is also the part of Amazon where machine learning has been earning money quietly for a decade, long before anyone used the word "AI" in an earnings call. Ranking, recommendation, demand forecasting, fraud screening, the routing of a parcel through a sorting centre — these are all prediction problems, and they were being solved with statistical models when the frontier laboratories were research groups. That history is worth holding on to, because it explains a strategic choice that otherwise looks like a failure of nerve. Amazon has never needed a chat assistant to make money from artificial intelligence. It has needed better predictions inside a machine that already converts predictions into cash.

### Alexa, and the price of nothing

Which brings us to the consumer assistant, and to a pricing decision that says more about the application layer than any product launch of the period.

In early February 2026 Amazon began the nationwide United States rollout of Alexa+, its rebuilt conversational assistant, free with a Prime membership, per Bloomberg's report of 4 February 2026 and GeekWire's coverage of the same rollout. By 20 August 2026 it had been extended free to all compatible Fire TV devices in the United States, per the device trade press that month. Amazon has not disclosed how many people use it, how much it costs to serve, or what revenue it produces, and this book will not guess at any of the three.

The absence of a price is the fact. Compare the two other giants in this part of the book. Microsoft attaches a stated thirty-dollar-per-seat monthly surcharge to an existing enterprise subscription. Alphabet defends a search business that monetises a query through an auction. Amazon has taken the largest installed base of voice-controlled devices in the world, put a frontier-class assistant behind it, and charged nothing incremental for it.

That is rational for exactly one kind of company. If your assistant's job is to make it easier to buy things from you, to watch the video you sell, to reorder the consumable you stock and to justify the annual membership fee that binds the customer to all three, then the assistant is not a product. It is a conversion mechanism, and charging for it would be charging admission to your own shop. The revenue shows up, if it shows up, in the retail line, the advertising line and the subscription line — mixed in, unattributable, and invisible to anyone outside the company.

This is the sharpest illustration of a difficulty this book keeps running into at the application layer: for the companies best placed to deploy AI to consumers, the return on it is structurally unmeasurable from outside. Microsoft can be held to a seat count. Amazon cannot be held to anything, because it has arranged for the benefit to arrive as slightly better performance in businesses that were already very large. A reader who wants to know whether Alexa+ is working will not find the answer in a line item. They will find it, years late and heavily confounded, in the growth rate of retail sales per Prime member — which Amazon does not disclose either.

## The floor Amazon does not own

Now the gap. Map the company onto this book's four floors and it holds three of them convincingly. Applications: the largest e-commerce operation in the West, an advertising business past sixty-eight billion dollars a year, Prime, devices, video. Compute: AWS, at roughly twenty-eight per cent of a global cloud infrastructure market worth about one hundred and forty-three billion dollars in the second quarter of 2026 alone, per Synergy Research Group. Silicon: Annapurna Labs, acquired in January 2015 for a reported three hundred and fifty million dollars per Bloomberg and GeekWire at the time, and now the source of Inferentia, announced November 2018 per CNBC, Trainium, announced December 2020 per TechCrunch, and Trainium3, announced on a three-nanometre process at re:Invent in December 2025 per the data-centre trade press.

The model floor is the thin one. Amazon announced the Nova family at re:Invent in December 2024, per TechCrunch's report of 3 December 2024, positioning it on price-performance rather than on frontier capability. Through 2025 it added services around the family — a customisation offering and an agent tool among them. Then came the retrenchment reported in July 2026: most of the flagship Nova models discontinued, Nova 2 Lite, Nova 2 Sonic, the Nova Forge customisation service and the Nova Act agent tool retained, and resources concentrated on a single frontier model under a research group led by Pieter Abbeel, who had joined Amazon through its 2024 acquisition of the robotics company Covariant, per The Next Web and Techstrong.ai in July 2026. The same reporting described a leadership consolidation: Rohit Prasad departed at the end of 2025 and Peter DeSantis took over a unified AI group in December.

Read that as an engineering decision and it is defensible, even overdue — a portfolio spread thin across text, image and video, narrowed to one serious attempt. Read it as a strategic position and it is stark. In the year when the model layer became the most valuable private asset class in the world, the company with the most computing on earth publicly reduced the number of models it was building.

The accounts, incidentally, will not help you judge this. Amazon does not report a research and development line in the conventional sense. Its income statement carries "technology and infrastructure", a heading that mixes the cost of running the world's largest cloud estate with the cost of the scientists inside it. An outsider therefore cannot separate what Amazon spends on inventing models from what it spends on keeping servers cool. This is not a criticism of the presentation, which follows how the business is actually managed. It is a warning about a common error: anyone who quotes Amazon's technology spending as a research figure, or compares it with another company's R&D line, is comparing two different things. If you want a company's frontier research effort, that number is generally not disclosed by anyone, and saying so is more honest than modelling it.

So: three floors owned, one rented. The rest of this chapter is about how Amazon paid for the fourth.

## Buying the position, part one: Anthropic

The Anthropic relationship was assembled in tranches, each larger than the last.

| Date | Commitment | Running total | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| September 2023 | 1.25bn USD initial, up to 4bn agreed | up to 4bn USD | CNBC, September 2023 |
| March 2024 | 2.75bn USD, completing the first agreement | 4bn USD | CNBC, March 2024 |
| November 2024 | additional 4bn USD; AWS named primary cloud and training partner | 8bn USD | CNBC and Amazon's announcement, November 2024 |
| April 2026 | up to a further 25bn USD in an AI infrastructure agreement | up to 33bn USD | CNBC, April 2026 |

At the March 2024 tranche, CNBC noted it was the largest outside investment in Amazon's three-decade history. It has since been dwarfed twice over by the same company's own subsequent cheques. The November 2024 language mattered as much as the money: "primary cloud and training partner" converts an investee into an anchor tenant, and the physical expression of that phrase went live on 29 October 2025, when AWS activated Project Rainier — a purpose-built cluster of nearly half a million Trainium2 chips on an Indiana campus, dedicated to training and serving Anthropic's models, with Anthropic planning to expand beyond a million chips by the end of 2025, per AWS's announcement and the data-centre trade press that October.

Then the investment ate the income statement. Anthropic closed a sixty-five-billion-dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five-billion-dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, per CNBC that month. Amazon's holding had to be remeasured to reflect it. In the second quarter of 2026, Amazon reported net income of sixty-two point six billion dollars — including fifty-three point four billion of non-operating pre-tax other income, primarily from its Anthropic investments, per the company's July 2026 results and the coverage of them. Earnings per share came in at five dollars seventy-five against analyst expectations of roughly one dollar eighty-one, per CNBC in July 2026.

Look at what that means arithmetically. The mark-up on a stake in a private company was nearly twice the twenty-seven point five billion dollars that the entire operating business — every warehouse, every advertisement, every server in every region — earned in the same three months. The gain is real in the sense that accounting standards require its recognition. It is also non-cash, unrealised, and priced by the last shares sold in a private negotiation, on terms outsiders cannot see. CNBC reported in August 2026 that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them; Amazon's second quarter is the exhibit that proves it.

## Buying the position, part two: OpenAI

The second purchase is stranger, because it is a purchase of a stake in the chief competitor of the company Amazon had just spent thirty-three billion dollars supporting.

It began as a supply relationship. On 3 November 2025, AWS and OpenAI announced a multi-year compute agreement worth thirty-eight billion dollars, giving OpenAI access to hundreds of thousands of Nvidia accelerators on AWS, with capacity to be deployed through 2026; Amazon's shares closed at a record that day, per CNBC's report of 3 November 2025 and Data Centre Dynamics' coverage of the technical terms. The commercial significance was that Microsoft's right of first refusal over OpenAI's compute had lapsed, and OpenAI immediately signed with Microsoft's largest cloud rival.

Four months later the relationship changed character. On 27 February 2026, OpenAI announced new funding that included fifty billion dollars from Amazon, per PYMNTS reporting in July 2026; the round has been reported at one hundred and twenty-two billion dollars, at an eight hundred and fifty-two-billion-dollar valuation, by valueaddvc and Forbes in 2026. The funding schedule, disclosed in Amazon's filing of 31 July 2026 and reported by PYMNTS and The Information, was fifteen billion in the first quarter, thirteen point seven billion in the second, and the remaining twenty-one point three billion after 30 June — with the later tranche reported as contingent on OpenAI reaching defined milestones. The same reporting described AWS becoming the exclusive third-party cloud provider for OpenAI's frontier programme, and OpenAI expanding its prior infrastructure agreements with AWS to a total that could reach one hundred billion dollars over eight years.

Notice the shape, because it is the same shape as the Anthropic arrangement, executed at greater speed. Money goes out as investment. A related stream comes back as contracted cloud revenue from a company the investor part-owns. Part Six of this book examines circular structures across the industry as a structural feature rather than a scandal, and the same discipline applies here: the disclosures are public, the logic is defensible on both sides, and no accusation is being made. But the reader acquires a duty. When AWS reports accelerating growth — thirty-seven per cent in the second quarter of 2026, its fastest in eighteen quarters, per CNBC in July 2026 — the question that must be asked is how much of that acceleration is arm's-length demand and how much is two heavily funded counterparties spending money their landlord helped provide. From outside, the public record does not separate the two, and anyone who tells you it does is filling in a gap with confidence.

## What it means to own both

No other company in this book holds material reported positions in both leading independent laboratories. Set the three giants' model-layer holdings against each other, as reported:

| Company | Position at the model layer | As reported |
|---|---|---|
| Microsoft | Roughly 27 per cent of OpenAI, model access contracted to 2032; up to 5bn USD into Anthropic announced November 2025 | CNBC, October and November 2025 |
| Alphabet | Approximately 14 per cent of Anthropic, capped at 15 per cent, no board seat or voting rights; committed investment reported at up to 43bn USD | Court filings reported by The New York Times, 2025; 2026 reporting |
| Amazon | Up to 33bn USD committed to Anthropic; 50bn USD invested in OpenAI | CNBC, April 2026; PYMNTS and The Information, July 2026 |

Four consequences follow, and they are worth separating carefully, because the position is easy to admire and easy to misread.

The first is that neutrality becomes a product. AWS's model strategy has always been the merchant's: Bedrock sells access to many laboratories' models the way a supermarket stocks many brands. A merchant who owns a piece of two of the leading suppliers can credibly promise customers that it will not steer them, because it is paid either way. Where Microsoft's customers must wonder how hard it is pushing its partner's models, and Alphabet's must wonder about Gemini, Amazon can plausibly say that it profits from whichever model wins. That is a genuine commercial asset, and it is the clearest answer to the question of what fifty billion dollars bought beyond a security.

The second is that the earnings statement now moves with private valuations rather than with trade. The second quarter of 2026 showed what that looks like on the way up. The mechanism runs in both directions: a stake carried at fair value is marked down when the reference price falls, and the reference price is set by whoever last agreed to buy shares in a private negotiation. A reader comparing Amazon's net income year on year without stripping the other-income line out is not analysing the company; they are reading a valuation report on two businesses Amazon does not control.

The third is an accounting point that this book's profession exists to make. The way these stakes hit the accounts differs by holder, and the difference is not cosmetic. Microsoft's stake in OpenAI has been reported as running through the equity method — its share of OpenAI's losses reduced its September-quarter 2025 net income by three point one billion dollars, per CNBC in October 2025 — while Amazon's Anthropic gain arrived as non-operating other income, the signature of a holding measured at fair value. Two companies, two comparable-sounding stakes in frontier laboratories, two entirely different effects on reported profit: one that falls when the laboratory loses money, one that rises when the laboratory raises money at a higher price. It is perfectly possible for a laboratory to be burning cash furiously and for its shareholder's earnings to rise sharply because of it. Anyone comparing the headline profits of these companies without first reading the basis of measurement in the notes is comparing two things that only look alike. Check the note. It is usually one paragraph and it changes everything.

The fourth is what the money did not buy. It did not buy control — Amazon's holdings are reported as investments, not consolidations, and there is no public suggestion of governance rights over either laboratory. It did not buy exclusivity: OpenAI's freedom to sell from any cloud is the very reason AWS could sign it. And it did not buy research capability. A shareholding in a laboratory produces a claim on that laboratory's economics; it does not put frontier researchers on your own payroll, and the thing being retrenched in July 2026 was precisely the in-house effort that a stake cannot substitute for. Amazon has bought a position in the model layer. It has not bought a model.

## The silicon answer, and why it is the same answer

The custom chip programme deserves a paragraph here even though the compute chapter covered it, because from the top of the stack it is visibly the same move made downward instead of upward.

Every dollar of accelerator spending paid to a merchant chip vendor carries that vendor's gross margin. Nvidia's data-centre business booked one hundred and ninety-seven point three billion dollars of revenue in the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026, per the company's February 2026 results — revenue that is, from its customers' side of the ledger, cost. Trainium and Inferentia attack that cost; the investments in Anthropic and OpenAI secure the demand that pays it. Amazon has bought into the layer above to fill its machines and built the layer below to make filling them cheaper. Both moves protect a compute business, which is the only floor the company has ever been dominant on.

By the middle of 2026 the chip bet had disclosed numbers attached: Amazon said its AI business and its custom silicon operation had each passed a twenty-five-billion-dollar annualised revenue run-rate, both growing at triple-digit percentage rates, per its second-quarter 2026 results coverage on CNBC in July 2026. Run-rate means a recent period multiplied out — not audited annual revenue — and should be read as a direction rather than a total.

## The bill

All of this is paid for out of the same capital expenditure line, and that line is now the most consequential number Amazon publishes. Purchases of property and equipment reached one hundred and sixty-nine billion dollars on a trailing twelve-month basis as at the second quarter of 2026, up sixty-four per cent year on year, per CNBC's July 2026 coverage; compilations of 2026 guidance reported by CNBC in February 2026 put the year's planned spending around two hundred billion dollars, within a total for the four largest cloud builders approaching seven hundred billion.

Two hundred billion dollars of capital spending against seventy-seven point seven billion dollars of group operating income in 2025 is a company committing more to concrete, steel and silicon in one year than its entire operations earn. The machinery by which that becomes future profit or future pain was set out earlier in this book, and it reduces to one sentence: spending capitalised today becomes depreciation charged against tomorrow's margin over an assumed useful life, and the assumption is management's judgement. In an era when accelerators are obsoleted by the arrival of a better model rather than by wear, that judgement deserves an annual sceptical reading. This is a description of where the soft joints are in any cloud's accounts. It is not an allegation about anyone's, and every large cloud has the same joints.

## What to watch

The threads of this chapter meet in five disclosures, all public, all quarterly.

The AWS segment note, for whether growth and margin move together — and, with the OpenAI and Anthropic commitments now flowing through it, for how concentrated that growth has become in counterparties Amazon has financed. The advertising line, because it is the application layer's clearest profit engine at this company and the place where better prediction converts into money without needing a chat interface. The other-income line, where two private valuations now sit and can double or halve reported profit without a single additional customer. The capital expenditure line against the depreciation charge, for the squeeze that arrives on a lag. And whatever Amazon eventually says about its own frontier model — because the July 2026 retrenchment set up a public test, and the difference between a company that rents intelligence and one that makes it will show up first in what it charges for it.

What none of this supports is a view on the shares, and this book does not offer one: no price target, no recommendation, no prediction. Every figure above carries the date and the source it came from, and every one will be superseded.

The durable judgement is about shape. Amazon reached the AI period holding the largest computing estate in the world and the weakest hand at the research frontier of any giant in this part of the book. Rather than out-research two laboratories it could not out-research, it bought pieces of both, contracted them as tenants, and set its own chip division to lowering the cost of housing them. It is an approach available only to a company with a profit engine large enough to pay for it — which is to say, only to a company that had already won the layer below. Whether owning the landlord's position and the shareholder's position in the same industry proves to be the strongest place to stand, or merely the most exposed, will be settled in the other-income line and the capital expenditure line, quarter by quarter, in public.
