# Memory II: HBM and the Supercycle

Year to date in 2026, as at mid-year, Samsung Electronics' shares were reported up about one hundred and fourteen per cent, SK Hynix's about one hundred and eighty-six per cent, and Micron's about one hundred and forty-one per cent, per IG and industry reporting in mid-2026. Those are not the share prices of a commodity business. They are the share prices of a business the market has decided is something else entirely.

The previous chapter made the case that memory was, for most of its history, the worst business in semiconductors: a true commodity, sold at whatever the spot market would bear, in a cycle that reliably destroyed the capital that built it. Nothing about that history has been repealed. What happened instead is narrower and stranger: one product within memory stopped behaving like a commodity at all, and it happened to be the product that artificial intelligence cannot run without.

That product is high-bandwidth memory — HBM. This chapter explains what it is, why AI accelerators need it more than they need anything else, why it is brutally hard to make, and how those three facts together turned an interchangeable part into a qualified, contracted, sold-out component with a queue of customers stretching years ahead. That transformation, not any general enthusiasm for chips, is why the memory share prices moved the way they did.

A caution before the mechanics: every figure in this chapter is a snapshot with a date on it, and memory figures go stale faster than most. Prices, market shares and order books in this business have historically swung harder than anywhere else in semiconductors. Check the current quarter before repeating any number you read here.

## The constraint nobody advertises

Start with the problem HBM exists to solve, because it is the opposite of the problem most people assume.

The marketing around AI accelerators is about arithmetic: how many trillions of operations per second a chip can perform. The engineering reality is that for the workload that matters most commercially — running a trained model to answer queries, known as inference — the chip's arithmetic units spend much of their time waiting. What they are waiting for is data. Specifically, they are waiting for the model's parameters — the billions of numbers that constitute the trained model — to arrive from memory.

The reason is a mismatch that has been widening for decades and is sometimes called the memory wall. Processor arithmetic has scaled with transistor counts, which is to say relentlessly. The speed at which data can be moved from memory chips into the processor has scaled far more slowly, because it depends not on transistors but on wires: the physical connections between the memory package and the processor package. A conventional memory module talks to a processor over a bus a few dozen bits wide. You can clock that bus faster, but signalling at high speed over centimetres of circuit board costs power and eventually stops working. The result is that a modern accelerator can perform arithmetic far faster than any conventional memory system can feed it.

The constraint binds differently across the two halves of the AI workload. Training a model is comparatively kind to memory: the work can be arranged so that large blocks of arithmetic are performed on each batch of data fetched, keeping the arithmetic units usefully busy. Inference — serving the trained model to paying users — is the unkind half, because each generated word requires a fresh pass through the model's weights with comparatively little arithmetic per byte moved. And inference is where the industry's revenue lives: it is the metered, repeated, billable act. The commercial product of the entire AI stack, in other words, is the memory-bound workload.

For model inference the constraint is decisive, and it is worth seeing why with actual arithmetic. Take an illustrative model, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real product: seventy billion parameters, stored at two bytes each, which is one hundred and forty gigabytes of weights. To generate a single token of output, the hardware must in essence read every one of those weights once. One hundred and forty gigabytes read per token, over and over, for as long as the model is answering. If your memory system delivers three terabytes a second, the ceiling is roughly twenty tokens a second for a single stream of output, no matter how fast the arithmetic units are — they could be infinitely fast and the answer would arrive no sooner. Serving many users at once softens the constraint by sharing each read across a batch of queries, but it never removes it. The arithmetic in that example is the point; the numbers are invented. The conclusion is general: inference is rationed by memory bandwidth, and the accelerator business is therefore hostage to the memory business.

The specifications of real machines say the same thing without any invention. Nvidia's H100 accelerator carries eighty gigabytes of HBM3 with about three point three five terabytes per second of memory bandwidth, per the company's published specifications. Its successor, the H200, is — instructively — the same processor with more and faster memory: one hundred and forty-one gigabytes of HBM3E at four point eight terabytes per second, per Nvidia's datasheet. The Blackwell-generation B200 is quoted in vendor and trade specifications at around one hundred and eighty to one hundred and ninety-two gigabytes of HBM3E and roughly eight terabytes a second, with the exact figure varying by configuration. Read that progression as a statement of priorities. When Nvidia wanted a mid-cycle upgrade, it did not add arithmetic. It added memory.

|---|---|---|---|
| Nvidia H100 | 80 GB HBM3 | ~3.35 TB/s | Nvidia published specifications |
| Nvidia H200 | 141 GB HBM3E | ~4.8 TB/s | Nvidia datasheet |

A component that sets the ceiling on what the five-trillion-dollar company's product can do is not a commodity, whatever its accounting classification says. The market spent 2024 to 2026 working that out.

## What high-bandwidth memory actually is

If the problem is wires, the solution is more wires, shorter. That is essentially all HBM is, and the elegance is in how literally the industry took the idea.

Instead of spreading memory chips across a circuit board and connecting them to the processor through a narrow bus, HBM stacks the memory dies vertically — eight, twelve, and in the newest generation up to sixteen thin DRAM dies, one on top of another — and connects them through the stack itself. Thousands of microscopic holes are etched clean through each die and filled with conductor, so that signals travel vertically through the silicon rather than out to the edge of a package. These are called through-silicon vias, TSVs. The completed stack sits on a base logic die, and the whole tower is placed millimetres from the processor on a slice of silicon called an interposer, which carries a connection about a thousand and twenty-four bits wide in the HBM3 generation — versus the few dozen bits of a conventional module — and two thousand and forty-eight bits wide in HBM4, per the JEDEC standards and technical documentation from Rambus and Siemens published up to 2026. Because the distances are tiny, each wire can run efficiently; because there are thousands of them, the aggregate is enormous. A single HBM3E stack delivers over one point two terabytes a second, and a twelve-high HBM3E stack packages thirty-six gigabytes; HBM4 stacks are specified at roughly two terabytes a second each, per the same technical documentation. Put six or eight such towers around a processor and you get the bandwidth figures in the table above.

The idea is older than the AI boom, and its history rewards attention. The architecture was driven in the late 2000s by AMD, a chip designer drowning in memory power consumption, working with SK Hynix, then the perennial second player in Korean memory. JEDEC, the industry's memory standards body, adopted HBM as a standard in October 2013; SK Hynix built the first devices the same year; and the first commercial product to ship with HBM was AMD's Fiji graphics chip in 2015, per the standard histories of the technology. For years afterwards HBM was a niche — technically admired, commercially marginal, too expensive for graphics cards that could make do with cheaper memory. SK Hynix kept investing anyway. When the transformer boom arrived and suddenly every accelerator needed the widest memory interface physics allowed, the company that had spent a decade on the unfashionable technology was the only one ready at scale. Chapter twenty-two returns to what that head start has been worth; the point here is that the supercycle's winner was decided years before the cycle began, by a bet that looked mediocre the whole time it was being made.

## Why it is hard to make, and why an accountant cares

Stacking chips sounds like packaging. It is closer to performing surgery twelve times in a row on the same patient, where any slip kills.

Begin with the arithmetic of stacked yield, using an illustrative calculation with round numbers belonging to no real company. Suppose each die in a stack is good ninety-five per cent of the time, and suppose a single bad die condemns the stack. Twelve dies at ninety-five per cent each compounds to about fifty-four per cent — before counting the yield of drilling thousands of holes through each die, thinning wafers to fractions of a millimetre without cracking them, bonding the layers with micron precision, and getting heat out of a tower of silicon that sits beside the hottest processor in the building. Real manufacturers fight this with known-good-die testing — screening each die before it is committed to a stack — and with repair schemes, and real yields are closely guarded. The structural point survives the secrecy: every layer added multiplies the ways a nearly finished, expensive part can die, and the industry has been adding layers with every generation.

Scarce capacity compounds the difficulty. HBM consumes roughly three times the wafer capacity of standard DDR5 memory per gigabyte of output, per Tom's Hardware reporting in 2026 — the dies are laid out generously for bandwidth and thermals, and the stacking process discards more of what is made. Every wafer a memory maker allocates to HBM is a wafer that no longer produces roughly three wafers' worth of conventional memory. That is why the supercycle spilled over into ordinary DRAM: with capacity diverted to HBM, conventional memory tightened too, and Gartner was forecasting DRAM prices to rise about forty-seven per cent in 2026, per Network World's reporting of the forecast. The same reporting noted Samsung raising module prices sharply in late 2025. Anyone who bought a laptop in 2026 paid a little of the AI industry's memory bill.

Then comes the step that changes the business model rather than the cost structure: qualification. An HBM stack is not dropped into a socket; it is bonded permanently beside a specific processor, inside that processor's power and heat budget, and it must work at speed, at temperature, for years, with no possibility of replacement. So the accelerator designer tests each supplier's parts exhaustively before committing, and a part that fails is simply not bought, at any price. This is not hypothetical. Through 2024, Reuters reported — in May of that year — that Samsung's HBM3 and HBM3E parts were struggling in Nvidia's qualification tests over heat and power consumption; Samsung publicly disputed the characterisation; and it was August 2024 before Reuters reported that Samsung's eight-layer HBM3E had cleared Nvidia's tests for use, with the twelve-layer version still pending at that time. Draw the accounting moral, not the engineering one: the world's largest memory maker, with the deepest pockets in the industry, spent the better part of two years unable to sell its newest product to the customer that mattered most. In a commodity, capacity is market share. In a qualified component, capacity is nothing until the customer signs.

That is the sentence in which the old memory business ends. A commodity is defined by the interchangeability of its suppliers — chapter twenty rested on it, because interchangeability is what handed all pricing power to the buyer the moment supply exceeded demand. HBM is not interchangeable. Each supplier's part is qualified individually, on a specific accelerator, over months. Switching suppliers mid-generation is a re-qualification project, not a purchase order. The buyer still has the money; the seller now has something the spot market never gave it — a moat around each design win, measured in engineering time.

### The bottleneck behind the bottleneck

One more manufacturing fact completes the picture, because it explains why the shortage could not be relieved simply by building more memory. An HBM stack is useless on its own. It has to be joined to the processor across that thousand-wire interface, and the joining is itself a scarce industrial process. The dominant version is TSMC's CoWoS — chip-on-wafer-on-substrate — in which the processor die and its surrounding memory stacks are mounted together on the silicon interposer that carries the connections between them. Nearly every high-end AI accelerator passes through this step or one of its close relatives; a leading-edge processor wafer that cannot get a packaging slot is not a product, it is inventory.

Through the boom, packaging was repeatedly the binding constraint. TSMC roughly doubled its CoWoS output in 2024 and set out to double it again in 2025, and its chief executive C. C. Wei still described the capacity as very tight and sold out through 2025 and into 2026, per trade reporting of the company's statements. Epoch AI's 2025 analysis of the accelerator supply chain concluded that advanced packaging and HBM — not the logic wafers themselves — were the bottlenecks on AI chip production in that period. So the memory supercycle is really a story about two interlocking scarcities: the stacks, and the seats at the table where stacks are married to processors. A buyer who wants more accelerators must reserve both, from different companies, years ahead, and a failure in either queue voids the other reservation. This is what it looks like when an industry's constraint migrates from how many transistors can be printed to how many finished towers of silicon can be assembled — and it is why relief from the shortage was always going to be measured in years, whatever any individual supplier spent.

## From spot price to order book

Follow what qualification does to the commercial terms, because this is where the share prices come from.

A commodity trades on spot and short-term contract prices, which is why memory makers historically had almost no revenue visibility: the price of everything they would sell next year was unknowable. A qualified, supply-constrained component trades the other way around. The accelerator designers cannot risk building machines they cannot supply with memory, so they contract for HBM far in advance, at negotiated prices, sometimes with prepayments. The suppliers, in turn, know what they will ship and roughly what they will be paid before the year begins.

Set the two business models side by side and almost every line of the ledger changes character.

| | Commodity DRAM, the old model | HBM, the new model |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Standard part, any supplier's will do | Qualified per customer, per accelerator design |
| Price | Spot and short-term contract, set by the market | Negotiated in advance, set in a shortage |
| Revenue visibility | Weeks | A year or more of contracted volume |
| Customer's switching cost | A phone call | A re-qualification programme measured in months |
| Failure mode in a downturn | Price collapses below cash cost | Volumes and terms renegotiated at the next generation |
| What capacity is worth | Market share | Nothing, until the customer's tests are passed |

The table is a simplification — the suppliers still sell plenty of ordinary memory, and chapter twenty-two weighs how much of each company sits on each side of it — but it explains why the market began valuing these businesses differently. The right-hand column describes something closer to an aerospace supplier than to a commodity producer.

By late 2025 this had reached its logical extreme. SK Hynix's head of DRAM marketing said the company had sold out its DRAM, NAND and HBM capacity for the following year, with customers reserving manufacturing slots into 2026, per TechSpot's reporting of the remarks. Micron was reported over the same period as having its HBM capacity for 2025 and 2026 fully booked, with new capacity not meaningfully improving availability until 2027. And by January 2026, trade coverage reported that all three major suppliers — SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron — had pre-sold their entire HBM capacity for the 2026 calendar year. The anchor statistic for the market as a whole: Bank of America estimated the 2026 HBM market at about fifty-four point six billion US dollars, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, per 2026 industry reporting of the estimate.

To a reader of accounts, "sold out through next year" is not one fact but several, and they are worth separating.

First, revenue visibility. A memory maker with a contracted order book can be analysed, for a year or two, almost like an industrial company with a backlog rather than a commodity producer — which is precisely how the market began pricing these companies, and a large part of why the shares re-rated. Where customers pay in advance to reserve capacity, the cash arrives before the revenue may be recognised, and sits meanwhile on the balance sheet as a contract liability — so a growing pile of customer prepayments in a memory maker's accounts is, counter-intuitively, one of the most bullish lines in the statements, and a shrinking one is worth noticing early. Second, price. Contracted HBM prices are negotiated in a shortage, between a supplier with sold-out capacity and buyers whose own product cannot ship without it. Memory makers were reported as earning three to five times the revenue per wafer from HBM as from conventional DRAM, per SemiAnalysis and trade reporting up to 2026. Third, mix. Every point of production shifted from spot-priced commodity to contracted premium product lifts the blended margin.

The results show what that mix shift does when it lands all at once. SK Hynix reported record results for the second quarter of 2026: revenue of about seventy-nine point three trillion won, up roughly two hundred and fifty-seven per cent year on year, and operating profit of about sixty point five trillion won, up roughly five hundred and fifty-seven per cent — an operating margin around seventy-six per cent, per the company's results announcement of July 2026 as reported by Investing.com and PR Newswire. Pause on that margin. Chapter twenty described an industry that spent whole years selling below cash cost. Seventy-six per cent is not a commodity producer's margin in a good year; it is the margin of a sole-source supplier of a component the richest customers on earth cannot design around. For context from adjacent chapters, it stands above the audited gross margins Nvidia and TSMC reported for their most recent fiscal years — and it is an operating margin, after research and overheads.

Two footnotes to the euphoria, both instructive. The first: on the day those record results were released, SK Hynix's shares were reported falling roughly nine per cent, because the market had priced in even more, per CNBC and Korean press coverage in July 2026. When a share has risen as far as these had, the reported number competes with the imagined one, and the imagined one had won. The second: the honours in the industry changed hands on the strength of all this. Counterpoint Research reported SK Hynix overtaking Samsung in DRAM revenue in the first quarter of 2025 — the first change at the top of that market in decades — and CNBC reported in January 2026 that SK Hynix had overtaken Samsung in annual profit for 2025, the first time the smaller company had out-earned its neighbour across a full year. Counterpoint put SK Hynix's share of HBM shipments at sixty-two per cent as of the second quarter of 2025 and its share at fifty-eight per cent in the first quarter of 2026, still comfortably the largest.

Every line of that table is perishable. Shares of a sold-out market are re-divided at each product generation, and the generational hand-off from HBM3E to HBM4 — with its doubled interface — was precisely the moment competitors were aiming at as this book went to press. ## A supercycle is still a cycle

The word the industry attached to all this was "supercycle", and the prefix is doing a lot of work. It is meant to suggest that this time the up-phase is different: demand structural rather than cyclical, supply disciplined rather than reckless, prices contracted rather than spot. Each claim has something to it. Qualification genuinely slows new supply, because capacity that has not passed a customer's tests is not supply at all. Contracts genuinely dampen the spot swings that used to whipsaw the industry. And the demand is not a forecast; it is written into order books.

But read the response to the shortage with the previous chapter still in mind. SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, as reported by CNBC in August 2026. Samsung and Micron were raising capital spending into the same demand. Every memory boom in history has ended the same way: capacity ordered into a shortage, arriving together, into a market that has meanwhile learned to need less of it than the order books implied. The qualified, contracted structure of HBM may soften that mechanism. It has not repealed the underlying one, because nothing can: fabs are still ordered years ahead of the demand they serve, and every supplier still makes that decision separately, watching the same customers.

The accounts will announce the turn, if it comes, before the press releases do, and it is worth knowing which lines to watch. Inventory is the classic one. In a memory downturn, finished chips that cannot be sold at acceptable prices pile up on the balance sheet, and days of inventory stretch out quarters before management concedes anything in words; in the shortage, the same line runs lean because everything made is already sold. Watch inventory days at the suppliers, and watch them at the customers too — a customer that has over-ordered against a shortage shows it as swelling stock long before it cancels anything. Then watch the gap between capital expenditure and depreciation: when every supplier's capex runs far above its depreciation charge at the same time, the industry as a whole is committing to a step-change in future capacity, and the depreciation on those new plants will arrive as a fixed cost precisely on the schedule the previous chapter described, whether or not the demand does. None of these lines forecasts anything on its own. Together, read quarterly across all three suppliers and their biggest customers, they are as close to an early-warning system as this industry provides.

Beyond the standard lines, there are two things specific to HBM that a careful reader should watch, neither of which requires a prediction. The first is the gap between contracted and merchant exposure. "Sold out through 2026" is a statement about a defined period; the interesting accounting question is what fraction of capacity is committed beyond it, on what terms, and with what cancellation or take-or-pay provisions — details the suppliers largely do not disclose, which is itself worth knowing. The second is the customer list. HBM's buyers are, overwhelmingly, a handful of accelerator designers, and their demand is in turn a derivative of a handful of cloud builders' capital budgets — the customer-concentration chain this book has traced in every layer. A component can be sold out and precarious at the same time, if everyone it is sold to answers to the same underlying spending decision.

None of this is a view on any share price, and this book does not offer one. What the accounts establish is narrower and more durable. For thirty years, memory was the textbook commodity: undifferentiated, spot-priced, cyclical, and cursed to hand its profits to its customers. High-bandwidth memory shows the escape route, and it is the same one every industrial commodity has ever found — become specified. Get designed into the customer's product so deeply that substituting you costs them a year. The memory makers did not find that route by marketing brilliance; they found it because physics moved the constraint on computing from arithmetic to bandwidth, and bandwidth happened to be the thing they sell. Whether the escape is permanent is the next chapter's question, because there are three companies on the route, and they are already spending like it will last forever.
