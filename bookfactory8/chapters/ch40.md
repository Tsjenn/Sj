# Google DeepMind and Gemini

In February 2023, Google published a short promotional video for a chatbot called Bard. In it, Bard answered a question about the James Webb Space Telescope with three bullet points, one of which said the telescope took the very first pictures of a planet outside our solar system. It had not; the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope photographed an exoplanet in 2004, nearly two decades earlier. Reuters spotted the error. On 8 February 2023, Alphabet's shares fell 7.7 per cent, taking roughly one hundred billion US dollars off its market value in a single session, as reported by Reuters and CNN that week.

Hold both halves of that picture. The company whose share price fell a hundred billion dollars over one wrong bullet point was, at that moment, the strongest AI research organisation on earth. It employed the researchers who had invented the architecture underneath every modern chatbot, including the one embarrassing it. It owned the second-largest cloud rival to the machines that trained them, designed its own AI chips, and put software in front of billions of people every day. No other company held every layer of the stack this book is organised on. Yet the market's verdict, expressed in the bluntest instrument available, was that Google was behind.

How a company can own everything and still be late — and what its accounts say about both conditions — is the subject of this chapter. It is also the sharpest available test of the question that runs through this whole part of the book: whether the model layer is won by integration or by focus.

## The endowment

List what Alphabet held when ChatGPT launched in November 2022, layer by layer, and the list reads like a description of the perfect AI company assembled a decade in advance.

At the research layer, it had two of the three or four leading laboratories in the field under one corporate roof: Google Brain, founded inside Google around 2011, and DeepMind, acquired in 2014. Between them these groups produced a remarkable share of the ideas the current era runs on. The most important was a June 2017 paper by eight Google researchers proposing an architecture called the transformer, which dispensed with the recurrent designs that had dominated language processing and replaced them with a mechanism called attention. Every frontier model discussed in this book descends from that paper. The T in GPT stands for transformer. Google did not merely participate in the research that made its competitors possible; it published it.

At the silicon layer, it had its own accelerator. Google began running a custom chip, the tensor processing unit, in its data centres in 2015, and disclosed its existence at its developer conference in May 2016 — the chips had been serving production traffic for a year before anyone outside knew they existed, per Google Cloud's own published history of the programme. No other model developer owned its own AI silicon then. Most still do not.

At the compute layer, it had Google Cloud, one of the three hyperscale providers, with its own data centres, network and power contracts. At the application layer, it had the most valuable distribution machine ever built: search, Android, Chrome, YouTube, Gmail, Maps, each with users counted in the billions.

That is the shape a strategist would draw on a whiteboard: research feeding models, models running on proprietary chips in proprietary data centres, delivered through products the whole world already uses, funded by an advertising business generating extraordinary cash. Vertical integration is usually an aspiration. Here it was a fact. And for the first year of the generative AI era, it conspicuously failed to help.

## Two laboratories

The research endowment came in two parts, and the seam between them matters to the story.

DeepMind was founded in London in 2010 by Demis Hassabis, Shane Legg and Mustafa Suleyman, with a stated mission of building general artificial intelligence. Google bought it in January 2014. Note, as an accountant would, what we know about the price: nothing official. Google never disclosed the consideration, because against its accounts the deal was immaterial. TechCrunch reported at the time that the price exceeded five hundred million US dollars; other outlets reported around four hundred million pounds. The most consequential AI acquisition ever made survives in the record as a spread of press estimates, a small early lesson in the difference between what is reported and what is filed.

DeepMind then spent a decade producing the field's most famous demonstrations. In March 2016 its AlphaGo system beat Lee Sedol, one of the greatest living Go players, four games to one in Seoul — a match DeepMind says was watched by more than two hundred million people. In 2020 its AlphaFold system effectively solved the problem of predicting a protein's structure from its amino-acid sequence, and in October 2024 Hassabis and his colleague John Jumper shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it, alongside David Baker, per the Nobel Foundation's announcement. A corporate research laboratory won a Nobel Prize for work done inside a listed company's cost base. It is worth pausing on how unusual that is.

Google Brain, meanwhile, sat inside Google proper, closer to products, and built much of the machinery of the deep-learning era: the TensorFlow framework, large-scale training infrastructure, and the transformer itself. The two groups were siblings and rivals. They published separately, competed for compute, and duplicated work. For years the arrangement was affordable eccentricity: research was cheap relative to advertising profit, and the field had no commercial clock running.

Then the clock started.

## Where the costs lived

Before following the events of 2022 and 2023, look at where Alphabet put these laboratories in its segment reporting, because the accounting told you precisely what management thought they were.

For years DeepMind sat inside Other Bets — the segment Alphabet uses for ventures that are not the core business, alongside experiments in health and autonomous driving. Google Brain's costs sat within Google Services, absorbed invisibly into the advertising machine. Then, beginning in the first quarter of 2023, Alphabet moved DeepMind out of Other Bets and into unallocated corporate costs, citing its increasing collaboration across the whole company; and when Brain and DeepMind were merged that April, the combined group was reported within unallocated corporate costs from the second quarter of 2023 onward, as Alphabet set out in its 2023 quarterly disclosures.

Read as an accountant reads, that reclassification is a strategy document in miniature. A cost in Other Bets is an option: something that might pay off, ring-fenced so investors can see the core business without it. A cost in unallocated corporate is infrastructure: something that belongs to no segment because every segment depends on it. In moving DeepMind, Alphabet was saying — in the segment note, before saying it anywhere else — that frontier AI research had stopped being a bet and become load-bearing. The filing knew before the press releases did.

The same reclassification removed DeepMind's results from separate visibility. When a company folds a unit into unallocated costs, outsiders lose the ability to track what it spends. From 2023 onward, the true cost of Alphabet's frontier research effort is not separately disclosed, and this book will not guess at it.

## The cost of moving late

Why was the company that invented the transformer not the company that shipped the transformer's defining product?

Part of the answer is arithmetic. Google's core product is search, and search is funded by advertising attached to results. A chatbot that answers the question directly, rather than returning links, threatens the click on which that model depends. A challenger with no advertising business risks nothing by shipping such a product; Google risked its income statement. This is the innovator's dilemma in its purest form, and it is not a psychological failing — it is a rational response to the numbers. The margin being protected was real, enormous and reported every quarter.

Part of the answer is reputational asymmetry. A startup's chatbot that invents facts is a curiosity; Google's chatbot that invents facts is a scandal, as the hundred-billion-dollar Wednesday demonstrated. A company whose brand is built on returning correct answers carries a heavier burden of caution, and between 2020 and 2022 Google repeatedly built capable language models — researchers and journalists documented several — without releasing them as products.

And part of the answer is people. The eight authors of the 2017 transformer paper all eventually left Google, a roll-call so complete it became a standing observation in coverage of the industry — noted again in 2024 reporting when Google paid to bring one of them back, which we will come to. The pattern is the oldest one in this book: the organisation that can afford to fund frontier research is rarely the organisation that lets researchers ship products at speed, and the researchers leave to ship.

The cost of the resulting delay was paid in public. OpenAI released ChatGPT in November 2022. Within weeks, The New York Times reported in December 2022 that Sundar Pichai had declared a "code red" inside Google, reassigning teams across the company to AI products; Forbes reported in January 2023 that founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin, long absent from operations, had returned for product meetings. Bard was demonstrated in February 2023 — the demonstration this chapter opened with — and released in March 2023. The strongest research organisation in the field spent 2023 visibly chasing a startup one-tenth its age.

## The merger

In April 2023 Alphabet did the thing it had declined to do for nine years: it merged its two laboratories. Google Brain and DeepMind became Google DeepMind, under Hassabis as chief executive, with Jeff Dean — who had led Google Research and co-founded Brain — becoming chief scientist, as reported by CNBC and Bloomberg in April 2023. Pichai's stated logic was that combining the talent into one focused team, backed by Google's computational resources, would significantly accelerate progress.

Strip the announcement language and the decision reads as an admission with a price attached. Running two world-class laboratories had been a luxury of the era when research was decoupled from products. In a race measured in months, duplication was no longer eccentric; it was expensive in the only currency that now mattered, which was time. The merger traded the creative friction of rivalry for the speed of a single line of command. Whether that trade was correct cannot be settled from outside, but what followed it can be dated.

The first Gemini model was announced in December 2023, per Google's own launch materials — Alphabet's first frontier model shipped as a unified product of the merged laboratory. In February 2024 the Bard name was retired and the chatbot itself became Gemini, with a paid tier introduced at 19.99 US dollars a month through Google One, as reported by the technology press that month. The renaming mattered more than renamings usually do: it moved the consumer product onto the research brand, tying the thing users touch to the laboratory that makes it — the integrated structure finally expressed in a product name.

One further transaction belongs in this section, because it prices something normally invisible. In August 2024, Google paid a reported 2.7 billion US dollars to Character.AI — a chatbot startup founded by Noam Shazeer, one of the eight transformer authors, after he left Google — in a deal structured as a non-exclusive technology licence. The Wall Street Journal reported that the deal's primary purpose was bringing Shazeer back; he returned and was made a co-lead of the Gemini model effort. Press reports in 2025 and 2026, including Yahoo Finance and Calcalist, said the US Department of Justice examined whether the structure avoided merger review — a question of process that remains, on public reporting, a question. Then in June 2026 Shazeer announced he was leaving again, for OpenAI, per Calcalist's reporting of his own statement. Set aside the drama and note what the episode establishes: at the frontier, a single researcher's presence was priced by a rational, disciplined acquirer in the billions. No line item in any filing will ever show you talent risk more clearly than that.

## The turn

Through 2024 the standard account of Google in AI was still the February 2023 story: strongest researcher, weakest shipper. Across 2025 and into 2026, the dated record stops supporting it.

Start with the model. Gemini 3 was released on 18 November 2025, and TechCrunch reported that it set a record score of 37.4 on Humanity's Last Exam, a broad reasoning benchmark on which the previous best, held by GPT-5 Pro, was 31.64, while also topping the LMArena leaderboard of human preference. Benchmarks are a moving and gameable measure, and this book treats them as weather rather than climate. But the direction of the weather had changed: for the first time in the generative era, Google's flagship was widely treated as the most capable model available at its release.

Then the users, which are harder to game. The trajectory of the Gemini app's monthly active users is unusually well documented, partly because early figures surfaced in United States court proceedings in the search antitrust case rather than in marketing, as reported by TechCrunch.

| Date | Gemini app monthly active users | Source |
|---|---|---|
| October 2024 | 90 million | Court documents, reported by TechCrunch |
| March 2025 | 350 million | Court documents, reported by TechCrunch |
| October 2025 | 650 million | Alphabet Q3 2025 earnings call |
| February 2026 | over 750 million | TechCrunch, February 2026 |
| August 2026 | over 1 billion | Google announcement, 11 August 2026, reported by TechCrunch and Forbes |

When the app passed one billion monthly users in August 2026, Google called it the fastest-growing product in the company's history, per Forbes. Two readings of that table are available and both are true. The first: an elevenfold gain in under two years is the integrated machine finally working — Gemini pre-installed on Android, surfaced in search, threaded through Chrome and Workspace, distribution doing exactly what the whiteboard always said it would. The second: a monthly active user acquired through a default placement is not the same asset as one who sought the product out and pays for it, and Google has not broken out Gemini's paying subscribers. The number that would settle which reading dominates — revenue per Gemini user — is not disclosed.

The financial results carried the same double message. Alphabet's third quarter of 2025 was its first hundred-billion-dollar quarter — revenue of 102.3 billion US dollars, up sixteen per cent year on year, per its results in October 2025. The fourth quarter, reported in February 2026, brought revenue of roughly 113.8 billion, up almost eighteen per cent, and net income of about 34.5 billion, up nearly thirty per cent, per CNBC's coverage. The threat scenario of December 2022 — chatbots strangling the search business — had not, on any line of the income statement then visible, arrived.

But look at what the growth is costing. On that same February 2026 call, Alphabet guided 2026 capital expenditure to between 175 and 185 billion US dollars — the top of that range more than double its 2025 spend, per CNBC, and consistent with the roughly seven-hundred-billion-dollar aggregate for the four biggest spenders that chapter one dated to 2026 guidance. A company that spent most of its life converting a fifth of its revenue or less into capital assets is now building at a rate that rivals nation-state infrastructure programmes. Chapter four's warning applies with full force: that capital will come back through the income statement as depreciation for years, on useful-life assumptions management chooses, and the profitability of the AI era will be decided as much in that estimate as in any model release.

The silicon layer, meanwhile, became an external business rather than an internal convenience. In November 2025 Google made Ironwood, its seventh-generation TPU, generally available — announced the previous April, and offering roughly four times the performance of its predecessor, per CNBC's November 2025 coverage, which framed it plainly as a challenge to Nvidia. Its flagship customer was Anthropic, which announced in October 2025 an expansion giving it access to up to one million TPUs and more than a gigawatt of capacity in 2026. Recall from earlier in this part that Google has also committed up to 43 billion dollars of investment to Anthropic, including ten billion in April 2026, per 2026 reporting. Follow the circle: Google is an investor in a model company that competes with Google's own models, and sells that company the chips it built to reduce its own dependence on Nvidia. Integration, taken far enough, stops being a structure and becomes a set of internal contradictions that must be managed — profitably, so far.

## Integration or focus

So does owning every layer win?

The honest answer, as at the summer of 2026, is that the case for integration looks stronger than at any point since the era began, and still is not proven.

The case for. Alphabet's market capitalisation stood at roughly 4.20 trillion US dollars in mid-August 2026, per companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings — behind only Nvidia at roughly 5.3 to 5.4 trillion and Apple at roughly 4.41 trillion, and ahead of Microsoft at roughly 3.66 trillion, same period, same sources. Those are photographs, not verdicts, and they will have moved by the time you read this; look them up. But the photograph shows a company the market stopped treating as the loser of the AI race some time ago. The integrated structure gives Alphabet things no rival can buy at any price: a frontier laboratory with a Nobel Prize, models trained on its own silicon at cost rather than at a chip vendor's margin, a cloud to rent the surplus, and a distribution system that took a chatbot from ninety million to a billion monthly users in twenty-two months. Every layer feeds a layer it owns.

The case against begins with the same history this chapter told. Integration did not prevent Google from being late; arguably it caused the lateness, because the layer that mattered most — the application — was chained to an advertising margin a focused rival could ignore. It did not hold the transformer's authors. It required, in the end, a merger of laboratories, a hundred-billion-dollar public humiliation and a reported 2.7 billion dollars to re-hire one researcher — who left anyway — before the machine turned. Focus, meanwhile, produced two private companies valued near a trillion dollars each within roughly a decade of existing, as the preceding chapters dated and sourced. A structure that owns everything defends everything, and defends slowly.

An accountant can add one more observation without taking a side. Alphabet's integration means the AI business is nowhere visible as a whole. Its research costs sit unallocated; its model revenue is folded into subscriptions, cloud and advertising; its chips are an internal transfer plus an emerging external business; its capex serves search and Gemini alike. Nobody outside the company — and possibly nobody inside it — can produce a profit and loss account for "Google's AI business", because the structure dissolves the question. OpenAI and Anthropic, whatever their disclosure habits, are at least the thing itself: their revenue is AI revenue, their costs are AI costs, and when they eventually file audited accounts, the model layer will have a visible income statement for the first time. Integration wins many things. Legibility is not one of them.

Whether it wins the era is a question this book will not pretend to answer, and nothing here is a view on any company's shares. What can be said from the record is narrower and more useful. The generative era's first act punished Google's integration, because incumbency set the constraints and a focused challenger set the pace. The act now in progress has rewarded it, because at scale the contest became one of capital, silicon, power and distribution — the layers Google already owned. If the next act is decided on those terms, the integrated player holds most of the board. If it is decided, as the last one was, by whoever is free to break the existing business first, then the most valuable endowment in the industry is also, once again, the heaviest thing to carry.

Both of those have been true within four years of each other. That is the fact to keep.
