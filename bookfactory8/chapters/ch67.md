# Concentration Risk

Buried in Nvidia's quarterly report for the period ended 26 April 2026 is a sentence that no press release repeated. Three direct customers accounted for thirty per cent, eighteen per cent and sixteen per cent of the company's accounts receivable balance at that date, per the filing as reported in 2026 coverage of it. Sixty-four per cent of the money owed to the world's most valuable company was owed by three parties, and the filing does not name them.

That is not an allegation. It is a disclosure, made because an accounting standard requires it, in a company whose results were excellent. It is also the cleanest single illustration of what this chapter is about.

## What concentration means in a set of accounts

Concentration risk is not the risk that something will go wrong. It is the risk that when something goes wrong, there is nothing else to absorb it.

An accountant meets the idea early and formally. Financial reporting standards require disclosure of significant concentrations — of customers, suppliers, geographies, products — precisely because a set of financial statements that reports only totals can conceal the shape of those totals entirely. Two companies can report identical revenue, identical margin and identical receivables. In one, ten thousand customers each contribute a hundredth of a per cent. In the other, three customers contribute two thirds. Every ratio you can calculate from the face of the accounts is the same. The businesses are not remotely the same business.

The disclosure exists because the difference only becomes visible in the notes, and only if someone reads them.

What follows applies that habit to an entire industry rather than a single company. The exercise is descriptive. Nothing here forecasts a failure, names a company as fragile, or suggests that any participant has behaved improperly. The claim is narrower and harder to argue with: the structure that supports the whole of this industry is unusually narrow at almost every level, the narrowness is disclosed in public documents, and most of the people holding exposure to it hold the same exposure several times over without noticing.

Measuring it does not require anything elaborate. Three readings taken from the notes will do for almost any company. What share does the largest single customer represent? What share do the top three represent together? And is the *count* of customers crossing the ten per cent disclosure threshold rising or falling? The third is the one most often misread. A company that discloses four customers above ten per cent this year and two next year has usually not become more diversified; it has usually become more concentrated, with two of the four having grown large enough to absorb the others' share. The same three readings applied to receivables rather than revenue give the balance-sheet version of the answer, and the two do not always agree.

There is one more distinction worth setting up front, because the rest of the chapter turns on it. Concentration comes in two forms. The first is a **single point of failure**: one supplier, one machine, one site, where an interruption has no substitute. The second is **correlated exposure**: many holdings that look different and move together, because underneath they are the same holding. The first is an operational fact. The second is a portfolio fact. This industry has both, and the second is the one people are worse at seeing.

## One foundry

Start at the bottom, where the concentration is most extreme and least disputed.

TSMC's share of the overall foundry market reached seventy-two point three per cent in the first quarter of 2026, up from seventy point four per cent in the preceding quarter, per TrendForce data reported in 2026. That figure covers the whole market, including mature nodes where competition is real. At the leading edge — the nodes that matter for accelerators — the concentration is higher still, and no competitor has demonstrated the ability to manufacture at comparable volume and yield.

Read that as an accountant reads a market share number, which is to say sceptically. Market share is an output, not a cause. What matters is why the share is what it is, and whether anything could change it on a timescale that would help.

The answer is that the leading edge behaves as a natural monopoly for reasons set out earlier in this book: the capital required arrives years before revenue, yield is accumulated process knowledge rather than purchasable equipment, and a fab that runs below capacity destroys its own economics. A second entrant would need to spend tens of billions, wait years, and then win volume from an incumbent whose costs are already down the learning curve. Nothing prevents it. The arithmetic simply discourages it.

The dependency runs in both directions, which is the part usually missed. TSMC's own customer base has concentrated sharply. Nvidia accounted for nineteen per cent of TSMC's revenue across 2025 — about NT$727 billion, or roughly twenty-three point four billion US dollars — overtaking Apple at seventeen per cent, per TSMC's 2025 annual report as reported by CNBC in January 2026 and by other trade coverage. Nvidia's share was twelve per cent in 2024; Apple's was twenty-two per cent. Two customers, on those figures, were more than a third of the revenue of the company that everything else runs through.

| Layer | Concentration | Figure | Period | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Foundry, all nodes | TSMC share of foundry market | 72.3%, from 70.4% | Q1 2026 | TrendForce, reported 2026 |
| Foundry customer base | Nvidia share of TSMC revenue | 19%, from 12% | Full year 2025 | TSMC 2025 annual report, via CNBC Jan 2026 |

So the base of the pyramid is one company, and that company's revenue rests substantially on two of the companies above it. That is not a criticism of anyone's commercial strategy. It is a description of a chain in which each link has fewer alternatives than the link above assumes.

## One machine

Below the foundry sits a narrower point still.

ASML is the only producer of extreme ultraviolet lithography systems. Nikon and Canon exited the technology more than a decade ago and no production-ready alternative exists; 2026 industry analyses put ASML's share of the wider lithography market at roughly ninety per cent and its share of EUV at effectively all of it. The company crossed five hundred billion US dollars in market value for the first time in January 2026, per Reuters and Yahoo Finance reporting at the time.

The important word in that paragraph is *only*. A ninety per cent share is a commercial position; a competitor could take some of it with a better product or a lower price. A hundred per cent share in a machine that took two decades and several near-failures to build is a different category of fact. There is no second source to switch to, no dual-sourcing policy that helps, and no procurement decision at any customer that changes the position.

Behind ASML the narrowing continues. The optics for these systems come from a single specialist supplier relationship of long standing, and the machines depend on export licences granted by governments. This is the general pattern of the bottom of the stack: at each step down, the number of viable participants falls rather than rises, and the substitution options that exist at the top disappear.

Two further chokepoints sit alongside, and both bind in practice rather than in theory.

The first is advanced packaging. The technique used to assemble accelerators — chip-on-wafer-on-substrate, in its several variants — has been a live constraint on supply rather than a technical footnote. Trade reporting through 2026 described TSMC scaling monthly capacity from roughly thirty-five thousand wafers a month in late 2024 towards a figure in the range of a hundred and twenty to a hundred and forty thousand across 2026, with TrendForce reporting in June 2026 that the supply–demand gap was expected to narrow from about twenty per cent to about ten per cent by the end of the year. The same coverage put a single customer at more than seventy per cent of the capacity for the variant used in the largest current designs. A gap of ten per cent is not a shortage in any dramatic sense. It is, however, a market in which the constraint is a process owned by one company.

The second is memory. High-bandwidth memory was estimated at about fifty-four point six billion US dollars for 2026, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, with SK Hynix holding the largest share and capacity reported sold out through 2026 across the major suppliers, per 2026 industry analysis. SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, reported by CNBC in August 2026. Three suppliers, in practice, serve the world for this component, and a sold-out condition means there is no spare capacity anywhere in the system to absorb a problem at any one of them.

## A handful of buyers

Move up a floor and the concentration inverts. At the silicon layer, the scarcity is supply. At the compute layer, the scarcity is customers.

The numbers are disclosed quarterly and they are stark. In the quarter ended October 2025, four direct customers each exceeded ten per cent of Nvidia's total revenue — twenty-two, fifteen, thirteen and eleven per cent — together about sixty-one per cent of roughly fifty-seven billion dollars of quarterly revenue, per the company's filing for that period and coverage of it by The Motley Fool in November 2025. In the quarter before, two customers alone approached forty per cent, per Datacenter Dynamics' reporting of the filing. And as at 26 April 2026, three direct customers held thirty, eighteen and sixteen per cent of the receivables balance.

Note what "direct customer" means, because it is a genuine complication rather than a quibble. Nvidia's direct customers include original equipment manufacturers, system integrators and distributors that build and resell systems. A direct customer is not necessarily the end operator of the hardware, and the concentration disclosed at the invoice level is not identical to the concentration of ultimate demand. It may understate it or overstate it. The filing does not say, and no external analysis can settle it.

What can be said is that the receivables disclosure is the sharper of the two figures. Revenue concentration tells you where the sales came from. Receivables concentration tells you who currently owes the money, and it is the line that determines what happens to the balance sheet if one of them stops paying. An accountant reading these accounts would spend more time on the second number than on the first, and would notice that both have been rising.

The same pattern appears one layer up, from the other side. Microsoft's commercial remaining performance obligation was reported at six hundred and twenty-five billion US dollars for the quarter ended December 2025, of which about forty-five per cent — roughly two hundred and eighty-one billion — was attributed to OpenAI contracts, per the earnings call transcript published by The Motley Fool in January 2026. A backlog is a genuine asset in the sense that it is contracted and disclosed under a standard. It is also only as good as the parties inside it, and nearly half of that one rests on a single private counterparty whose accounts are not published.

Stand further back and the concentration of demand is more striking than the concentration of any one company's sales ledger. Substantially all of the growth at the compute and silicon layers rests on the capital expenditure programmes of a small group of buyers. Compilations of 2026 guidance put the four largest of them on course for something approaching seven hundred billion dollars of capital expenditure for the year, with other compilations of the same guidance arriving nearer seven hundred and twenty-five billion; later in the year, reporting put one company's guidance at a hundred and ninety-five to two hundred and five billion dollars and another's raised to two hundred and twenty billion. Those are secondary compilations of guidance rather than filings, and they should be read as such.

The relevant feature is not the size of the number. It is that the number is the decision of a handful of boards. Ordinary industrial demand is the aggregate of millions of independent choices, which is why it moves gradually. Demand composed of four capital budgets can change direction at four meetings. Nothing suggests it will. But a demand base with four decision-makers behaves differently from one with four million, and every company below them inherits that property whether or not it appears anywhere in their own accounts.

Nvidia's own share of the accelerator market compounds the picture. Estimates for 2026 sit in a wide band depending on what is counted: roughly eighty to eighty-eight per cent of the merchant AI accelerator market by various 2026 analyst estimates, falling into the seventy-five to eighty per cent range when hyperscalers' own custom silicon is included in the denominator. The band is wide because the definition is contested, which is itself informative. But at any point in that band, one designer supplies most of the world's AI compute, buying most of its leading-edge manufacturing from one foundry, using packaging capacity from that same foundry and memory from three suppliers — and selling the result to a group of buyers small enough that four of them cross the ten per cent disclosure threshold in a single quarter.

## Two labs, one interface

The model layer is concentrated in a way that is harder to measure, because most of it is private.

The most-cited public measurement of enterprise usage is Menlo Ventures' periodic survey work. Its 2026 reporting put Anthropic at about forty per cent of enterprise large-language-model API spend, OpenAI at twenty-seven per cent and Google at twenty per cent, with the coding subset more concentrated still — Anthropic at roughly fifty-four per cent by mid-2026 against OpenAI at about twenty-one per cent. Three suppliers, on those figures, account for the overwhelming majority of enterprise spend on the layer.

Treat that measurement with the caution the earlier chapters set out. It is a survey conducted by a venture firm, not an audited market study, and the firms surveyed are not a random sample of world business. It is nonetheless the best regularly published series available, and its direction has been consistent.

The application layer is where measurement gets genuinely poor, and the poverty of the measurement is worth stating plainly rather than papering over. Compilations of web-traffic measurement in 2026 put ChatGPT's share of chatbot traffic at roughly fifty-seven per cent in March 2026, declining to about fifty-three per cent by May; other 2026 compilations, using different definitions, put the figure near eighty per cent. Those cannot both be describing the same thing. Nobody publishes an audited unit of account for this layer, and until somebody does, any precise claim about application-layer share should be treated as an estimate produced by a party with a methodology it has not fully disclosed.

The honest summary of the top two layers is therefore: concentrated, on the best available evidence, but measured by instruments that would not survive an audit. That is a different statement from the silicon layer, where the concentration is visible in filings.

## A single island

Most of the leading edge is manufactured in one place. The next chapter deals with the subject properly and without speculation; what belongs here is only the scale of the dependency and the timescale of any alternative.

Taiwan held about sixty-eight per cent of global capacity in advanced processes — sixteen and fourteen nanometres and below — in 2023, and close to eighty per cent at the EUV-generation nodes, per data compiled in a 2024 supply-chain review published by Taiwanese government sources. An industry projection reported by Manufacturing Asia puts Taiwan's share of sub-ten-nanometre logic capacity declining from sixty-nine per cent in 2022 to forty-seven per cent by 2032 as capacity is built elsewhere.

Both halves of that matter. The dependency is extreme now. It is also being reduced, slowly, and the projections that describe the reduction are measured in years and reach out to the 2030s. A fab announced today produces revenue several years from now; a workforce and a yield curve take longer than the building does. Diversification at this layer is real and it is not fast, and no amount of capital makes it fast.

## A few power markets

The newest concentration is the one that appears in no chip company's accounts at all.

Capacity that cannot be energised is not capacity, and the places able to energise it are few. Northern Virginia — the PJM system's Dominion zone — serves the largest concentration of data centres in the world, reaching roughly twenty point three gigawatts of capacity in 2026, up from about sixteen gigawatts in 2025, and representing on the order of thirteen per cent of global live capacity, per 2026 market analyses. Data centres consumed about a quarter of Virginia's electricity in 2025, per US Energy Information Administration data reported in 2026.

The consequences show up in prices and in queues rather than in shortages. The Dominion zone's price gap against the rest of PJM in the real-time market widened past forty per cent during 2026, per analysis published by Amperon. PJM's 2025 regional transmission expansion process identified a hundred and twenty-two new baseline reliability projects at an estimated eleven point eight billion dollars, driven substantially by data centre growth, per 2026 reporting of the process. Dominion has proposed distinct tariff arrangements for very large load customers, reported by Datacenter Dynamics.

The point is not that power runs out. It is that the industry's physical footprint has clustered into a handful of grids and permitting regimes, each with its own regulator, its own interconnection queue and its own local politics. That is a concentration of a type semiconductor companies have no experience managing, and it is the one constraint in this chapter that binds regardless of who is right about demand.

## The same risk, counted once

Here is the part that concerns anyone holding exposure, and it has nothing to do with any individual company's quality.

Consider a portfolio assembled with ordinary care. It holds the accelerator designer, the foundry, the lithography supplier, a memory maker, two hyperscalers and a broad index fund. Seven positions, four countries, three currencies, several industries as classified by any standard scheme. On paper this is diversified.

Underneath, every position is a claim on the same variable: the rate at which AI capital expenditure continues. The designer sells to the hyperscalers. The foundry sells to the designer. The lithography supplier sells to the foundry. The memory maker sells into the same systems. The hyperscalers are the buyers. And the index fund holds all of them again.

That last point is measurable. Ten companies made up over thirty-six per cent of the S&P 500 by weight in 2026, against about twenty-three per cent in 2000; the Magnificent Seven passed thirty per cent of the index during 2026, per Forbes reporting in June 2026. Nvidia alone was the largest constituent at roughly seven per cent of the index — a single company weighing more than entire sectors — with Apple at about six point three per cent and Microsoft at about four point six per cent, per 2026 compilations of index weights.

An index fund is the standard instrument for *not* taking a concentrated position. In 2026 it delivered one. Nobody chose that; it is the arithmetic of a capitalisation-weighted index doing exactly what it is designed to do. But the effect on a portfolio is the same as if the choice had been made deliberately, and most holders are unaware they have made it.

This is the correlated-exposure form of concentration, and it is why the chapter cannot be confined to supply chains. The operational concentrations described above — one foundry, one machine, three memory makers, four buyers, one island, a few grids — are the reason the financial exposures move together. They are one phenomenon observed at two different levels.

### An illustrative example

Take an illustrative supplier, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It earns a thousand of revenue, sixty per cent of it from a single customer, at a forty per cent gross margin, and carries two hundred of receivables of which a hundred and twenty are owed by that customer.

Now suppose the customer does not fail, does not dispute anything, and simply defers its next order by two quarters while it digests what it already bought. Revenue falls by three hundred. Gross profit falls by a hundred and twenty. But the fixed cost base does not move, the inventory built for that customer stays on the balance sheet and ages, and the receivable — still perfectly collectable — now represents a larger share of a smaller business. If the supplier has debt covenants tied to earnings, they tighten. If it has capacity commitments to *its* suppliers, they do not relax.

Nothing improper occurred. Nobody defaulted. One buyer changed its mind about timing, and a healthy business had a difficult year. That is the entire mechanism of concentration risk, and it requires no villain.

## Where it is disclosed and where it hides

The practical value of this chapter is knowing where to look, because most of it is disclosed and free.

| What you want to know | Where it is disclosed | What to read for |
|---|---|---|
| Customer concentration | Notes to the accounts, concentration of credit risk | Customers above 10% of revenue; the same for receivables |
| Supplier concentration | Risk factors and commitments notes | Sole-source language; purchase obligations by year |
| Geographic concentration | Segment note and property disclosures | Where the assets are, not where the sales are |
| Backlog quality | Remaining performance obligation disclosures | Concentration within the backlog, and conversion timing |
| Capacity commitments | Commitments and contingencies | Amounts committed before demand is contracted |
| Portfolio concentration | Index fact sheets | Top-ten weight; single-name weight |

One derived measure is worth adding to that list, because it costs nothing and turns early. Days sales outstanding — receivables divided by revenue, multiplied by the days in the period — measures how long the company waits to be paid. In a business with a broad customer base it moves slowly and means little. In a business where three parties owe two thirds of the balance, it is a direct reading on those three. A rising trend, with revenue flat, says that concentrated customers have begun paying more slowly, and that shows up a quarter or more before anything appears in revenue. It is a two-minute calculation from figures on the face of the accounts.

Three things hide rather than appear.

The first is *indirect* customer concentration. A supplier that sells through integrators discloses concentration at the invoice level, which may be more or less concentrated than the underlying demand. No filing resolves this, and analysis that claims to has made an assumption.

The second is concentration across a group of holdings. Every company discloses its own position; nobody discloses the overlap between yours. The correlation described above must be assembled by hand from separate documents, which is why so few people assemble it.

The third is the concentration inside a private counterparty. Where a disclosed backlog rests on a company that publishes no audited accounts, the disclosure tells you the amount and not the capacity to pay it. That is not a failure of the filer. It is a limit of the reporting system as it currently stands.

## What this chapter is not saying

It is worth being exact about the claim, because concentration is easily overstated in either direction.

Concentration is not the same as fragility. A single supplier with excellent operations may be more reliable than four mediocre ones. Nor is concentration evidence of wrongdoing: every relationship described here is an arm's-length commercial arrangement between sophisticated parties, disclosed as the standards require. Nor does it predict a failure. Structures like this have persisted for long periods in other industries — commercial aviation, medical devices, certain chemicals — without incident.

What concentration does is remove the margin for error. It converts events that would otherwise be absorbed somewhere in a supply chain into events that propagate through it. A delay at one packaging line, a licence not renewed, a single grid's interconnection queue lengthening, one buyer deciding to pause — in a broad market each of those is noise, and in this structure each of them reaches the accounts of companies several layers away.

That is the honest description, and it neither supports nor refutes either of the arguments in the previous two chapters. Concentration is a feature of the present arrangement, visible now, regardless of what happens next. Nothing in this chapter is investment advice, and it contains no price target and no prediction.

## What to watch

Four things move this picture, and all are public.

**The disclosure thresholds themselves.** Watch the number of customers exceeding ten per cent of revenue at the accelerator layer, and whether the receivables concentration follows revenue concentration up or down. A falling count of ten-per-cent customers means concentration is increasing, not decreasing.

**Second sources becoming real.** Custom silicon from the hyperscalers, competing accelerators, and any credible second foundry at the leading edge all reduce concentration if they ship in volume. Announcements do not; shipments do. The test is whether the market share bands cited above narrow in successive quarters, not whether a product is launched.

**Capacity arriving outside the current cluster.** Fabs, packaging lines and memory plants outside the present geographic concentration change the structure when they produce at yield, which is years after they are announced. Follow completions and qualified output, not ground-breakings.

**Index weights.** The single-name and top-ten weights of the major indices are published monthly and take thirty seconds to check. They tell any holder of a broad fund exactly how concentrated their supposedly diversified position has become.

Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot with a date attached and will have moved. The concentration figures move quarterly with the filings, the index weights move daily, and the capacity figures move as plants complete. The structure they describe moves considerably more slowly, which is the reason it is worth understanding rather than merely tracking.
