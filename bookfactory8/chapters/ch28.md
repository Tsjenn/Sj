# The Neoclouds

In August 2026 a company that had spent 2018 mining Ethereum reported a contracted order book of one hundred and four point two billion US dollars. CoreWeave's second-quarter results, covered by CNBC and Investing.com on 11 August 2026, showed quarterly revenue of about two point six billion dollars, up one hundred and twelve per cent on the year, against a revenue backlog of one hundred and four point two billion as at 30 June 2026 — up from thirty point one billion a year earlier — with a further twenty-five billion in net new commitments signed after the quarter closed. Total debt stood at roughly thirty-five billion dollars. Nine years earlier the company did not exist. Five years earlier almost nobody had heard of it.

CoreWeave is the largest and most visible member of a category that had no name before this cycle and now has an ugly one: the neocloud. Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe, Applied Digital, IREN, TeraWulf and a longer tail of private operators share the same basic shape. They are not software companies, whatever their investor presentations say. They are capital-intensive rental businesses, assembled at speed, that buy one manufacturer's accelerators in bulk, install them in powered buildings, rent the resulting machine time under contract, and borrow against the whole arrangement to buy more.

The previous three chapters dealt with companies that added AI capacity to an existing business. This chapter deals with companies for which the capacity is the entire business. That difference is not cosmetic. It changes what the accounts look like, what the debt is secured on, who the customers are, and what happens if any single assumption in the model stops holding. The neocloud is the purest expression of the compute layer — nothing but the layer, with no search engine, no retail operation and no software franchise standing behind it — which makes it the best place in the book to examine the layer's economics without interference, and the worst place to be standing if those economics turn.

## Where they came from

The origins are less glamorous than the order book. CoreWeave was founded in 2017 by commodities traders — Michael Intrator, Brian Venturo and Brannin McBee among the founders — as a cryptocurrency mining operation called Atlantic Crypto, which by its own account became one of the larger Ethereum miners in North America before the 2018–2019 crypto winter made the activity uneconomic. The pivot, retold across the coverage of its 2025 listing, was straightforwardly practical: the firm was holding thousands of Nvidia graphics processors whose value as mining equipment had collapsed but whose value as rentable computation had not. From 2019 it rented them out; in 2021 it renamed itself CoreWeave; on 28 March 2025 it listed on Nasdaq at forty dollars a share, below the range it had marketed, per CNBC's coverage of the offering.

That lineage matters more than it first appears, because cryptocurrency mining turns out to have been an accidental apprenticeship for AI infrastructure. A miner's competence is acquiring cheap electrical capacity, filling industrial buildings with hot, dense, failure-prone GPU racks, and running them flat out. Those are precisely the scarce skills of this cycle, which is why so much of the neocloud category is converted miners. Applied Digital, IREN and TeraWulf all came out of bitcoin mining. CoreWeave tried to buy one outright: in July 2025 it proposed an all-stock acquisition of Core Scientific, a bitcoin miner and data-centre operator, valued at about nine billion dollars — and was refused, when Core Scientific's shareholders voted the deal down and the agreement was terminated on 30 October 2025, as reported by Bloomberg. The miners' shareholders, note, thought the price too low. The buildings and the power connections, not the chips, were the part worth arguing over. Chapter thirty-one will explain why.

Nebius took a stranger route. It is the corporate remnant of Yandex, the Russian search company, which after Russia's invasion of Ukraine divested its Russian businesses; the Netherlands-incorporated remainder kept the engineering staff, the cash and the Nasdaq listing, resumed trading in October 2024, and rebuilt itself as a GPU cloud, per CNBC's reporting in September 2025. Lambda began as a deep-learning hardware supplier and moved into renting the machines it had previously sold. The routes differ; the destination is the same five-part machine: buildings, accelerators, hourly rental, debt secured on the hardware, and a very short customer list.

## The model, taken apart

Strip the category to its working parts and it is a leasing business with unusual assets. The neocloud secures powered industrial space, own-built or leased. It converts capital into accelerators — overwhelmingly Nvidia's, bought in the tens of thousands, which requires an allocation from Nvidia as much as it requires money. It sells the resulting machine time, some of it on demand by the hour, most of it under multi-year contracts to a handful of enormous counterparties. And it finances the whole structure with debt raised against the hardware and, increasingly, against the contracts the hardware serves.

Each part deserves a paragraph, because each carries a different risk.

The buildings are the slow part. Chips can be bought in months; an energised, cooled data-centre hall takes years, and the grid connection can take longer than the building. This is why converted miners command a premium and why CoreWeave was willing to issue nine billion dollars of stock for one. It is also why the neoclouds exist at all: the hyperscalers of chapters twenty-four to twenty-six, despite combined 2026 capital-expenditure guidance approaching seven hundred billion dollars as reported by CNBC in February 2026, could not build fast enough, and found it rational to rent someone else's halls while their own were being poured.

The accelerators are the fast-depreciating part. A rack of current-generation hardware is simultaneously the most valuable thing the company owns and the thing whose value is least certain three years out. Chapter four set out the useful-life argument; chapter thirty-two returns to it. For a neocloud the argument is not academic, because the hardware is most of the balance sheet.

The rental is the revenue part, and it comes in two very different flavours. Spot and on-demand rates are a genuine market price, and they have behaved the way market prices behave when supply arrives: trade price-trackers such as Silicon Data and IntuitionLabs record that renting an Nvidia H100 fell from above eight dollars per GPU-hour — with some hyperscaler listings above twelve — at the 2023 peak of the shortage to a market median around three dollars by mid-2026, with published rates as low as one dollar forty-nine on marketplace platforms; AWS cut its own on-demand H100 pricing by forty-four per cent in June 2025, per trade coverage of that reduction. A price that falls by more than half in three years is not a detail. It is the residual-value question in its rawest form, and we will come back to it. Contracted revenue, by contrast, is negotiated privately, runs for years, and is the thing the backlog measures.

The financing is the part that makes the rest possible, and it deserves its own section.

### An illustrative cluster

Before the real financings, a worked example — with round numbers chosen for clarity, invented for the purpose, and belonging to no real company.

Take an illustrative operator that buys ten thousand accelerators at forty thousand dollars each: four hundred million dollars of chips, and call it five hundred and fifty million once networking, storage and fit-out are included. It borrows seventy per cent of the cost, three hundred and eighty-five million, at ten per cent, and funds the rest with equity. It depreciates the hardware over five years and signs contracts averaging three dollars per accelerator-hour.

| Illustrative line | Per year |
|---|---|
| Revenue at 90% utilisation, $3.00/hour | $237m |
| Power, staff, facilities | ($35m) |
| Depreciation over five years | ($110m) |
| Interest at 10% on $385m | ($39m) |
| Pre-tax profit | $53m |

On those invented numbers the business works: fifty-three million of pre-tax profit on one hundred and sixty-five million of equity. Now move the two assumptions the operator does not control. At two dollars an hour and eighty per cent utilisation, revenue falls to about one hundred and forty million and the pre-tax line is a loss of roughly forty-four million — while the interest and the depreciation, which between them were one hundred and forty-nine million, do not move at all. The entire equity case lives in the gap between a contracted rental rate and a fixed capital charge. Everything else in this chapter is about who bears that gap, and what they have demanded in exchange.

## Why a lender accepts a chip

The question in the chapter's brief is the right one: why would anyone lend billions against hardware whose spot rental price halved in three years? The answer has three layers, and the order in which lenders discovered them is visible in the sequence of CoreWeave's own facilities.

| Facility | Date | Size | Lead lenders | Notable terms |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First debt facility | August 2023 | $2.3bn | Magnetar, Blackstone | Secured on the Nvidia GPU fleet (company announcement, August 2023) |
| Second facility | May 2024 | $7.5bn | Blackstone, with Magnetar and Coatue; Carlyle, CDPQ, BlackRock funds among participants | Secured against the GPU fleet and contracts (company announcement, 17 May 2024) |
| Investment-grade facility | March 2026 | $8.5bn | Bank-led | Reported at SOFR plus 2.25%, rated A3 by Moody's, maturing 2032 (industry reporting, 2026) |

The first layer of the answer is the chip itself. In 2023 an H100 was a scarce object with a queue behind it. Collateral that can be repossessed and resold above its carrying value is good collateral, whatever it is made of; for a period, accelerators met that test better than most commercial property. Trade analyses of the GPU-collateralised lending market in 2026 describe the standard structure that emerged: advance rates of fifty to seventy per cent against appraised value, senior secured terms of two to four years matched to an assumed useful life, and pricing of several hundred basis points over SOFR. Lenders never believed the chips held value forever. They believed the loan would amortise faster than the chips decayed, and priced accordingly.

The second layer — the one that actually carried the big numbers — is not the chip but the contract wrapped around it. When Blackstone and its co-lenders advanced seven point five billion dollars in May 2024, they were not primarily underwriting resale values in a secondary GPU market. They were underwriting the obligation of an investment-grade counterparty — at that time overwhelmingly Microsoft — to keep paying for capacity for years. The chip secures the loan in form; the customer's covenant secures it in substance. This is why the neocloud financing model is best understood as a cousin of project finance, the discipline used for pipelines and power stations: lend against a contracted revenue stream, take security over the physical asset as a backstop, and size the debt so the contract alone can service it. Bloomberg made the same point from the customer's side in September 2025: Microsoft's arrangement with Nebius lets it expand capacity without the capital expenditure appearing on its own balance sheet, while Nebius finances construction from the contract's cash flow and from debt secured against the agreement itself.

The third layer is the strangest, and it is the manufacturer. In September 2025 a securities filing disclosed — and CNBC reported, on 15 September 2025 — that Nvidia had signed a six point three billion dollar order under which it is obliged to purchase CoreWeave's residual unsold cloud capacity through April 2032, under an agreement originally struck in 2023. Read that carefully. The supplier of the collateral has promised to be the customer of last resort for the capacity the collateral produces. For a lender, this is close to a residual-value guarantee by the one party with the deepest interest in the residual value; 2026 reporting also put Nvidia's equity holding in CoreWeave at about four point seven billion dollars. It is disclosed, it is legal, and it plainly strengthens the credit. It also means the health of the borrower, the collateral, the backstop and the equity all trace to a single company's continued prosperity. Chapter fifty-seven examines circular structures of exactly this kind; here it is enough to note that the circle exists and that lenders lend inside it with their eyes open.

The evolution across the table is the story of the category told in credit terms. In 2023 the debt was expensive and secured on chips. By March 2026 an eight and a half billion dollar facility priced at two and a quarter points over SOFR carried an A3 rating — pricing that treats a nine-year-old former Ethereum miner's paper the way markets treat an established utility's. Whether that migration reflects genuinely transformed risk, or a market extrapolating a contracted backlog into permanence, is the question the last section of this chapter equips you to keep asking. This book takes no position on any security, and nothing here is investment advice.

## The customers, counted on one hand

Every neocloud balance sheet rests on a customer list short enough to memorise. CoreWeave's own listing documents, widely reported at the March 2025 flotation, disclosed 2024 revenue of one point nine two billion dollars, up seven hundred and thirty-seven per cent — with sixty-two per cent of it from Microsoft alone. Full-year 2025 revenue reached five point one billion dollars, per the company's annual report as covered by StockTitan in early 2026. Since then the list has broadened without lengthening: press reporting in 2025 and 2026 described OpenAI commitments to CoreWeave building from an initial contract of up to eleven point nine billion dollars in March 2025 to a total of roughly twenty-two point four billion, a Meta commitment reported at about fourteen point two billion, and the company saying Microsoft would fall below half of future committed revenue.

The same pattern repeats down the category. Nebius signed a five-year agreement announced in September 2025 to supply Microsoft with dedicated capacity from a New Jersey site, worth seventeen point four billion dollars through 2031 and up to nineteen point four billion if options are exercised, per CNBC and Bloomberg; Nebius shares rose nearly fifty per cent on the announcement. Lambda announced its own multibillion-dollar Microsoft agreement in November 2025 — value undisclosed — covering tens of thousands of Nvidia GPUs, per CNBC on 3 November 2025, in the same week that Microsoft agreed terms with IREN, per press reporting of both deals.

Notice who keeps appearing. The neoclouds' largest customers are the hyperscale clouds of the preceding chapters — the companies that are also their most capable competitors, running the same chips in their own buildings at larger scale. This looks stranger than it is. For a hyperscaler, renting a neocloud's hall buys time and preserves optionality: capacity now, without waiting for its own construction, without the capital expenditure on its own balance sheet, and without a permanent commitment to the site. For the neocloud, the same contract is existence itself — the collateral for the next facility and the substance behind the backlog.

An accountant reads a backlog of one hundred and four billion dollars against annual revenue only recently past five billion and asks the unglamorous questions. A backlog is not revenue; it is a schedule of promises stretching years ahead, recognised only as capacity is delivered and consumed. Its value depends on the counterparty still needing the capacity, still being able to pay for it, and not renegotiating when the market price of the underlying service has fallen — and the largest counterparties include private model laboratories whose own accounts, as chapter five established, nobody outside has audited. Oracle's chapter made this point about a much older company; it applies with more force to a younger one. Capacity built against contracted future revenue is a bet on the customer's survival as much as on the demand. When the customer is Microsoft, that bet is about as good as commercial bets get. When the customer is a loss-making private laboratory financing its commitments from successive funding rounds, the quality of the backlog is exactly the quality of the next round — and the neocloud's lenders are, at one remove, exposed to it.

Concentration also cuts the other way, which is easy to miss. A customer that is sixty per cent of revenue holds obvious power over the supplier. But a supplier whose capacity is fully contracted holds power too, in a shortage: the customer cannot quickly replace the hall, the power connection or the delivery slot. The relationship is less like a vendor and a buyer than like two parties chained to the same construction schedule. The chain binds in both directions — until, one day, capacity stops being scarce.

## What happens if the residual falls

Now assemble the risk the whole structure carries. The debt is fixed. The depreciation is fixed the day the hardware is bought. The revenue rests on a small number of contracts and on rental rates that, in the open market, have already fallen by more than half from their 2023 peak. And the collateral is hardware whose resale and re-rental value depends on the pace at which its own manufacturer makes it obsolete — a manufacturer that, as chapter fifteen recorded, has moved to a roughly annual product cadence.

The pessimistic reading writes itself, and by 2026 a commentary industry existed to write it. If accelerator residual values fall faster than loans amortise, advance rates that looked conservative become loans underwater on their security. If contracted rates reset toward spot at renewal, the margin over the fixed capital charge compresses just as the debt matures. Trade coverage in 2026 described a cluster of neocloud maturities falling between 2026 and 2028 — a refinancing wall, in the market's phrase — arriving exactly when the first large cohort of hardware bought in 2023 and 2024 reaches the age at which the useful-life argument stops being theoretical. The cost of carrying the structure is already visible in the income statement: CoreWeave's net interest expense reached six hundred and forty million dollars in the second quarter of 2026 alone, more than double the two hundred and sixty-seven million of a year earlier, per the August 2026 results coverage.

The optimistic reading is also genuinely evidenced, and honesty requires stating it with equal care. Old accelerators have kept earning: on the same August 2026 earnings call, CoreWeave's chief executive said the company had contracted 2020-vintage A100 capacity out to 2029 at full price, as reported by Tom's Hardware and 24/7 Wall St. Chapter four explained the physical reason — the binding constraint in 2026 was energised, cooled floor space, and an old chip in a powered hall is not obsolete, it is supply. On this reading the collateral behind the loans is not really the chip at all but the chip's place in a powered building with a contract attached, and that composite asset has so far held its value far better than the spot price of the silicon alone. The lenders' behaviour suggests they believe it: pricing tightened and ratings rose between 2023 and 2026, which is not how credit markets treat collateral they fear.

Both readings are conditional forecasts, not facts, and this book does not adjudicate between them. What it can do is say precisely what would distinguish them, because the difference is observable, line by line, as the years arrive. If the optimists are right, ageing fleets will keep re-contracting near original rates, backlogs will convert into recognised revenue on schedule, and refinancings will keep pricing tighter. If the pessimists are right, the first signs will be quiet: renewal rates below original rates, utilisation slipping on older vintages, backlog growth outpacing revenue conversion by ever-wider multiples, impairments of hardware carrying values, and new debt costing more than the debt it replaces.

That is the watch list, and it doubles as the chapter's summary. A neocloud is a machine for converting borrowed capital into contracted machine-hours faster than the hyperscalers could do it themselves. It is neither a scandal nor a miracle. It is project finance applied to silicon: a genuinely new category of company built, at extraordinary speed, on four numbers — the rental rate, the utilisation, the useful life and the cost of debt — of which management fully controls none. The figures in this chapter are snapshots, dated for that reason, and the current versions live in each company's latest quarterly results and debt disclosures, which any reader can consult. When you do, read the backlog last. Read the interest expense, the depreciation schedule and the customer names first. They are where this business model actually lives.

The neoclouds, for all their scale, were still built one facility at a time. The next chapter concerns what happened when the industry stopped financing buildings and started financing projects the size of small national economies.
