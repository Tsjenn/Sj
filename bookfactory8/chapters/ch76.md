# A Reader's Watch List

Taiwan's listed companies must publish revenue for the preceding month by the tenth day of the following month, under the Taiwan Stock Exchange's continuing obligations. No other major market demands disclosure at that frequency. So anyone with a browser and ten minutes gets a public read on demand for leading-edge silicon roughly six weeks before a quarterly statement reaches anybody, and it costs nothing.

That is the shape of everything in this chapter. Not privileged information, not a subscription: a published document, on a known schedule, in a place most readers have never looked.

The rest of this book is an argument built on numbers that are already ageing. This chapter is the part that keeps working after they have gone: six indicators, chosen because between them they read all four layers of the stack and the grid underneath it, each published on a schedule you can put in a calendar, each meaning something specific when it moves.

Nothing here is investment advice. Each of these indicators describes commitments the industry has already made. None tells you what any share is worth, because that depends on what the market already expected, and expectations are disclosed nowhere.

## Why these six, and not sixty

Any industry can generate an unlimited number of things to watch; the discipline is in the exclusions. Here are the tests each of the six had to pass.

It must be **published on a schedule**, not on a news cycle: an indicator that appears when somebody decides to write about it is a story. It must be **hard to spin** — a number required by an accounting standard, buried in a note and identical in form from year to year, has not been selected for you the way a slide-deck metric has. It must **lead something**: an order book, a queue position or a purchase commitment tells you what has been paid for and cannot easily be undone, where reported revenue only tells you what already happened. It must **change meaning when it moves**, in a direction you can state in advance. And it must be **checkable by one person in an afternoon**, without a terminal or a professional qualification.

Several important things fail these tests and are absent. Model benchmark results move monthly and mean something different each time the benchmark changes. Token prices are published but are a marketing surface as much as a cost. And the single most interesting number in the industry — what a hyperscaler actually pays for an accelerator — is disclosed by nobody, so no amount of watching will produce it.

## One: capital expenditure guidance, read against revenue

Capital expenditure is the number that commits everything below it. Nothing is built, ordered, powered or racked until somebody has guided to a spending figure, and once the concrete is poured the commitment is real in a way no strategy statement ever is. The four largest buyers of computing hardware — Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta — were on course to spend approaching seven hundred billion US dollars between them on capital expenditure in 2026, as reported by CNBC in February 2026. One floor down, TSMC guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion US dollars, above analyst expectations, in its January 2026 results.

**Where to find it.** Guidance appears in the results announcement and, more usefully, in the question-and-answer section of the earnings call, where the framing is less controlled. The outturn appears in the cash flow statement under investing activities, as purchases of property, plant and equipment — quarterly on Form 10-Q and annually on Form 10-K for a US domestic filer, annually on Form 20-F for a foreign private issuer.

**How often it updates.** Annual guidance is set once and revised at each quarterly result. In this cycle the revisions have run in one direction. Treat the revision, not the original guidance, as the news.

**What a change means.** Read the raw figure against three denominators, because each answers a different question.

Against **revenue** it gives capital intensity — how much must be spent to stand still and grow — which is meaningful against a company's own history and meaningless across business models. Against **operating cash flow** it tells you who is paying: spending funded from money the business generates is a different proposition from the same figure funded by debt, vendor credit, or a joint-venture vehicle whose obligations sit outside the balance sheet. Against **depreciation** it gives the direction of travel: materially above is expansion, materially below is harvesting the existing base. That third ratio is the one to memorise, because it is the industry's cycle written in two numbers.

Take an illustrative operator, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Revenue 100, capital expenditure 30, depreciation 12: intensity of thirty per cent, capex at two and a half times depreciation, a company building far beyond its current consumption of assets. Hold revenue at 100 and cut capital expenditure to 10, and the same company is living off what it already owns. Neither is good or bad. They are opposite bets, and the accounts announce the switch a year or more before the physical consequences arrive.

**What would fool you.** Guidance is a forecast, framed by the people giving it. Definitions of capital expenditure differ, and leases are the usual culprit: finance leases count in headline capex, operating leases do not, which is why an accounting change can move the number the whole market is watching. In July 2026 Microsoft said that from the start of its fiscal 2027 it would extend the estimated useful life of its data centre and office buildings from fifteen years to twenty-five, and was explicit that the larger effect was on reported capital expenditure rather than on profit, because a longer building life changes how future data centre leases are classified (company commentary, reported July 2026). And most disclosures do not separate the building from the machines inside it, though one depreciates over decades and the other over a handful of years.

## Two: the assumed useful lives

One sentence in the accounting policies note decides more reported profit in the compute layer than any pricing decision. It reads something like "servers and network equipment are depreciated on a straight-line basis over estimated useful lives of up to six years". The number in it is an estimate made by management and reviewed by an auditor as reasonable rather than as correct.

**Where to find it.** The property and equipment accounting policy note gives the current assumed lives by asset class, and the note itself gives gross cost against accumulated depreciation, from which the age of the fleet can be worked out. Any change during the year appears as a change in accounting estimate, with its effect in money, in the notes and in management commentary.

**How often it updates.** The assumption is reviewed at least annually and disclosed in every annual report. A change is announced when made, often in a quarterly filing rather than an annual one, and disclosed properly exactly once.

**What a change means.** Lengthening a life reduces the annual charge and raises reported profit, with no effect whatsoever on cash, and the amounts are not small. Microsoft extended server and network equipment from four years to six for fiscal 2023, following an assessment in July 2022, and disclosed operating income higher by eight hundred and eighty-five million US dollars in the three months to 31 March 2023 and by two point nine billion in the nine months to that date (Microsoft Form 10-Q, filed April 2023). Alphabet moved servers from four years to six for 2023, announced that February, and reported a reduction in depreciation of three point nine billion US dollars and an increase in net income of three point zero billion for the year to 31 December 2023 (Alphabet Form 10-K for 2023).

Shortening is the more interesting signal, because it is management conceding that the machines age faster than the schedule assumed. Amazon went both ways within a year: servers extended from five years to six in February 2024, then a subset shortened from six back to five with effect from 1 January 2025, citing the increased pace of technology development particularly in artificial intelligence and machine learning, expected to decrease 2025 operating income by approximately seven hundred million US dollars — alongside a charge of about nine hundred and twenty million dollars for equipment retired early (Amazon disclosures, 2025).

That last item is the one to watch hardest. An early-retirement or abandonment charge is the estimate failing in real time, machine by machine, and it is much harder to present favourably than a change in a note.

Divergence between peers is the other reading. Where two companies in the same trade hold visibly different assumptions — six years against four for near-identical rented fleets, as reported of CoreWeave and Nebius in 2025 — their margins are not comparable until both fleets have been restated to a common life. That is arithmetic you can do yourself in a few minutes, and it is among the most valuable calculations in this book.

**What would fool you.** A change in estimate is applied prospectively. Prior years are not restated, there is no catch-up charge, and the comparatives printed beside the current year were prepared on a different basis. Any growth rate spanning the change is not like-for-like unless you adjust it, and nobody will adjust it for you.

## Three: the customer concentration disclosures

Under ASC 280, a public entity must disclose that revenues from a single external customer amount to ten per cent or more of total revenues, the amount from each such customer, and which segment reports it. Entities under common control count as one customer, and the customer's identity need not be given. What that produces is the plainest picture of bargaining power in the industry, published annually, in a note almost nobody opens. Nvidia's Form 10-K for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026 disclosed four direct customers each above the ten per cent threshold, together accounting for sixty-one per cent of revenue — twenty-two, fifteen, thirteen and eleven per cent respectively, per 2026 reporting of the filing by Daloopa. Four unnamed buyers, three fifths of the revenue of the most valuable company on earth.

**Where to find it.** Three places, and they do not always agree. The segment note carries revenue concentration; the financial instruments or credit risk note carries concentration in accounts receivable, which measures the same relationship at a different moment; and Item 1A, Risk Factors, carries the narrative version, where the company must say in prose that losing one or a few customers would materially affect it.

**How often it updates.** Annually in full, with quarterly updates in the interim filings. The risk-factor language is the highest-yield item on this entire list to compare against last year's edition, line by line: companies do not add or delete a risk factor casually, and each change has been through counsel.

**What a change means.** Rising concentration means the buyers' negotiating position is strengthening, which will show up later in gross margin, in receivable days, and in inventory that may be specific to one customer's design. Falling concentration is ambiguous in a way worth internalising: a customer dropping off the list may have gone, or may simply have fallen below ten per cent because the total grew around it. The percentages alone cannot distinguish those, and one of them is very good news.

The receivables note deserves its own attention: a customer large in receivables but not in revenue has either just accelerated its purchasing or has stopped paying on time. Reading the two notes side by side across two consecutive years is fifteen minutes of work and frequently the most informative fifteen minutes available.

**What would fool you.** The disclosed customer is the entity billed, not necessarily the one that decided to buy. Sales through a distributor or contract manufacturer are attributed to the intermediary, so one disclosed customer may be a dozen unrelated end buyers, or several may all be serving one. The geography table has the same defect, being usually built on ship-to destination — where the goods were delivered for assembly into something else, not where the demand was. Read the basis-of-attribution sentence before drawing any conclusion from a country figure.

And where a customer is also an investor, the question is not whether something improper is occurring. It is narrower and answerable: does the disclosure let you separate the connected revenue from the rest? Where it does not, the honest conclusion is that you cannot tell, and the honest thing to write down is that you cannot tell.

## Four: equipment orders, as the industry's leading signal

Capacity for 2028 is being decided now, in purchase orders. Reported lead times for advanced lithography systems have been in the region of eighteen to twenty-four months, with bookings extending into 2028, per industry reporting of ASML's 2026 earnings commentary, and the gap between an equipment maker's revenue and the wafer output it enables runs to about a year. A fab operator can cancel an order, and does. It cannot conjure capacity it failed to order two years ago.

**Where to find it.** The classic instrument no longer exists: SEMI ceased publishing its monthly North American book-to-bill report in 2017, the December 2016 report being the last, after participants stopped reporting orders. What remains is the Worldwide Semiconductor Equipment Market Statistics report, compiled by SEMI with the Semiconductor Equipment Association of Japan from members' monthly submissions, which reports billings — money invoiced — by segment and region. SEMI reported global equipment billings of thirty-six point five five billion US dollars in the first quarter of 2026, up fourteen per cent year on year and a record quarter, attributing it to leading-edge logic, DRAM and advanced packaging (SEMI, WWSEMS release, 2026).

**How often it updates.** Monthly submissions, quarterly public releases, and forecasts published roughly twice a year, revised in between.

**What a change means.** This series falls first and falls hardest, which is why it is worth the trouble. In 2023, worldwide semiconductor manufacturing equipment sales slipped one point three per cent to one hundred and six point three billion dollars from the 2022 record of one hundred and seven point six billion (SEMI, reporting on 2023 billings) — a modest headline concealing a collapse, with NAND equipment sales forecast to fall about forty-nine per cent to eight point eight billion dollars and capital spending cuts of the order of fifty per cent at SK Hynix and forty-two per cent at Micron. That glut was visible in equipment orders long before it was visible anywhere else, and the shortage that followed was the direct consequence of capacity nobody had ordered.

The opposite condition looked like this. SEMI's forecast published in July 2026 put wafer fab equipment at one hundred and forty-three point nine billion dollars for the year, up twenty-three point one per cent, with the foundry and logic portion up eighteen point nine per cent to seventy-eight billion — raised through the year from the same body's earlier releases. A forecaster revising the current year upward, mid-year, is telling you that the buyers changed their minds during it, which in this layer means they changed their minds about 2028.

**What would fool you.** Billings are invoices, not orders, so the series lags the decision it reports. And the sellers disagree: during 2026, Lam raised its estimate of the wafer fab equipment market to about one hundred and forty billion dollars (company earnings call, April 2026), KLA raised its view to the low one hundred and fifty billion range (company earnings call, July 2026), and SEMI's July 2026 figure sat at one hundred and forty-three point nine billion. That disagreement is not noise to be tidied away. It shows the width of the honest range, and anyone quoting one of the three as the size of the market is quoting an estimate and calling it a fact.

## Five: memory contract prices

Memory prices move faster than anything else in the stack, and they move through everyone. A rise is revenue for three companies and a cost for every company above them, which makes this the one indicator on the list that must be read twice, once from each side.

**Where to find it.** Contract prices — the negotiated prices at which memory actually ships in volume — are surveyed monthly by TrendForce, alongside separate spot-market data through its DRAMeXchange platform, with quarterly outlooks by product category; the companies' own results give the outturn. Spot and contract are different instruments: spot is thin, fast and largely a sentiment gauge, and contract is where the volume is.

**How often it updates.** Monthly for contract prices, continuously for spot, quarterly for the outlooks and the results that confirm them.

**What a change means.** Consider the published sequence through 2026, which is a lesson in reading rates rather than levels. TrendForce reported first-quarter 2026 DRAM contract prices climbing by a record ninety to ninety-five per cent quarter on quarter, with DRAM industry revenue up eighty-one per cent quarter on quarter (TrendForce, June 2026). For the second quarter it put conventional DRAM contract prices up fifty-eight to sixty-three per cent quarter on quarter, PC DDR5 up forty-three to forty-eight per cent, and mobile DRAM up ninety-three to ninety-eight per cent (TrendForce, 2026). For the third quarter it expected server DRAM contract prices to rise thirteen to eighteen per cent quarter on quarter, and named the reason for the slowdown explicitly: long-term agreements capping the increases (TrendForce, July 2026).

Read that sequence carelessly and it says the memory boom is ending. Read it properly and it says something else. Prices were still rising in every quarter; what decelerated was the rate, and the stated cause was contractual, buyers having locked in terms in advance so that the shortage stopped passing straight through into price. A deceleration caused by contracts is not the same event as one caused by demand, and the two have opposite implications. The published commentary usually says which it is. Most coverage does not repeat it.

**What would fool you.** Four confusions, all common. Spot quoted as though it were contract. Commodity DRAM quoted as though it were high-bandwidth memory, which is qualified per customer per accelerator design and behaves like an aerospace component. A price rise reported as unambiguously good news, when for every buyer above the memory layer it is margin compression arriving with a lag. And the assumption that a contracted order book removes the cycle. It does not; it moves where the cycle shows up, from price to the volumes and terms renegotiated at the next product generation.

One balance-sheet line pairs with this indicator. Where customers pay in advance to reserve capacity, the cash sits as a contract liability until the revenue may be recognised — so a growing pile of customer prepayments in a memory maker's accounts is, counter-intuitively, among the most encouraging lines in the statements, and a shrinking one is worth noticing early.

## Six: the power interconnection queue

The last indicator belongs to no company in this book. Under the silicon sits the grid, and a connection to it cannot be bought. It is a study. When a large generator — or, increasingly, a large load — asks to connect to the high-voltage network, the operator must work out what that does to everything else. The request joins a queue, the studies proceed in batches, and that queue has become one of the defining constraints on where compute can physically exist.

**Where to find it.** Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory publishes an annual survey of United States interconnection queues, Queued Up. The 2025 edition, with data to the end of 2024, counted nearly 11,600 active projects — about 1,570 gigawatts of generating capacity and a further 1,030 gigawatts of storage — and found the median time from request to commercial operation had doubled, from under two years for projects built between 2000 and 2007 to over four years for those built between 2018 and 2024. The 2026 edition, published in June 2026 with data to the end of 2025, counted about 8,200 active projects and roughly 2,061 gigawatts of combined generation and storage, down about ten per cent on the prior year's active total, the decline attributed principally to accelerating withdrawals rather than to completions (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Queued Up 2026 edition, per 2026 reporting of its findings).

The two editions do not use identical bases for the same date, so the prior-year comparison inside the 2026 edition is not the figure the 2025 edition published. That is data cleaning, not disagreement about the world, and the correct response is to compare editions of one publication rather than headlines from two.

The second source is the capacity auctions run by grid operators, where the price of a promise to be available is set. In the largest United States electricity market the annual auction cleared at three hundred and twenty-nine dollars seventeen cents per megawatt-day in July 2025 — the maximum the regulator allowed — for the delivery year from June 2026 to May 2027, against twenty-eight dollars ninety-two cents two auctions earlier; the following auction, in December 2025, hit a new cap of three hundred and thirty-three dollars forty-four cents, a record for the third auction running, as reported by Utility Dive and Canary Media. The market monitor, Monitoring Analytics, attributed sixty-three per cent of the increase in the 2025/26 auction to data centre demand, per Utility Dive's reporting of its analysis in 2025 and 2026.

**How often it updates.** The queue survey annually, published mid-year with data to the previous year end; capacity auctions annually; grid operators' own queue statistics continuously, at varying quality.

**What a change means.** A shrinking queue is not the same as a shortening one. A queue emptying through withdrawals is a queue so slow that speculative applications die in it; a queue emptying through connections is a bottleneck clearing. The two look alike in the headline total and mean opposite things, and the reports distinguish them. Lengthening median times against rising capital expenditure guidance is the clearest sign available that the binding constraint has moved from silicon to substations, and a capacity price at its regulatory cap for three auctions running is a market saying it cannot supply what has been asked of it at any permitted price.

**What would fool you.** The instinct that capital solves this. A hyperscaler with two hundred billion dollars of annual capital expenditure waits behind a solar developer with a filing fee, because the study of one affects the study of the other. There are lawful ways around the queue — co-locating with existing generation, building your own — and the industry is pursuing all of them. The default path still runs through a process no balance sheet accelerates.

## How the six fit together

Arranged by how far ahead they look, the list becomes a rough sequence rather than a collection.

| Indicator | Where it is published | How often | Which layer it reads | How far ahead it looks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equipment billings and orders | SEMI with SEAJ; suppliers' results and calls | Monthly, with quarterly releases | Silicon | Two to three years, via capacity |
| Power interconnection queues and capacity auctions | Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory; grid operators; market monitors | Annual, plus continuous operator data | Below silicon | Two to five years, via connection dates |
| Capital expenditure guidance | Results releases, earnings calls, cash flow statements | Quarterly, revised through the year | Compute | One to three years, via commitments |
| Memory contract prices | TrendForce surveys; company results | Monthly, quarterly outlooks | Silicon into compute | One to four quarters, via cost |
| Customer concentration | Segment and credit-risk notes; Item 1A | Annual, updated quarterly | Silicon and model | Backward-looking, but changes structure |
| Assumed useful lives | Accounting policy and estimate-change notes | Annual, changes announced when made | Compute | Immediate effect on reported profit |

The spread across physical, financial, contractual and regulatory sources is deliberate: a set drawn entirely from filings will miss a grid, and one drawn entirely from industry statistics will miss an accounting change worth three billion dollars of reported profit. In time the sequence runs roughly equipment orders, then capital expenditure commitments, then memory contracts, then wafer output, then compute capacity — with the interconnection queue setting a floor under the chain that none of the others can lift. Concentration and useful lives sit apart. They lead nothing; they tell you how to read everything else, which is who holds the bargaining power and how much of the reported profit is an estimate.

## An hour a quarter

The routine is short enough to be real.

Each quarter, when results season closes, open the cash flow statement of every large buyer you follow and write down capital expenditure and operating cash flow — two numbers each. Note whether guidance moved and in which direction, and note the language: confidence about multi-year demand, or talk of digestion and normalisation. Then read the equipment billings release and any revision to the forecasts, and write down where the informed parties disagree.

Each month, if memory matters to what you are reading, note the contract price direction by product category and — this is the part that gets skipped — the stated reason for any change in the rate.

Each year, when the annual reports land, there are three jobs. Compare the risk factors against last year's edition, line by line. Read the property and equipment policy note and check whether any assumed life moved. Read the segment note and the credit-risk note for concentration, side by side. And when the queue survey is published, note the median time from request to operation and whether the queue shrank through connections or through withdrawals.

That is the whole discipline: perhaps four hours a year for the annual items and an hour a quarter for the rest. It will put you ahead of most of what is written about this industry, for the unglamorous reason that most of it is written from the press release.

## What six numbers cannot do

They cannot tell you what anything is worth. Every indicator here describes commitments already made; a valuation describes expectations about commitments not yet made, and expectations are published nowhere. A reader who watches all six perfectly and concludes that a share is cheap has made a leap the data does not support.

They cannot close the gap between what is disclosed and what would be interesting. What a hyperscaler pays per chip is not disclosed. What it costs to serve one request from a frontier model is not disclosed by anyone. How much of a private laboratory's reported run rate is contracted, and on what terms, is not disclosed. Where that is so, the correct move is to stop rather than to fill the space with a plausible figure.

They cannot protect you from yourself. The commonest failure in indicator-watching is not being wrong about the data; it is deciding the conclusion first and then choosing which of the six to emphasise. The defence is to write down, before looking, what a rise and a fall in each would mean.

And they will not stay six. If the argument of this book is right — that value moves towards whichever input cannot be quickly reproduced — the list should change as the scarce thing changes. It has already changed once inside these pages: power was on nobody's watch list in 2020, and an interconnection study is now a harder constraint than a wafer allocation. The next constraint will announce itself the same way: a lengthening queue somewhere, a rising price for something previously assumed to be free, and a paragraph in risk factors that was not there the year before.

## The point of the exercise

Every figure in this book carries a date and a source, and by the time you read this most have moved. The capital expenditure guidance has been revised, probably upward. The memory prices are on a different quarter. Somebody has changed a useful life.

None of that matters much, because the numbers were never the point. The method was: knowing which document holds the answer, when it appears, and what a movement in it implies. That does not expire. It will work on whatever the scarce input turns out to be a decade from now, because the disclosure obligations, the accounting standards and the physics of building things will all still be there.

The difference between reading a headline about this industry and reading the industry itself is about six documents and the habit of opening them. That is a smaller gap than it looks from outside.
