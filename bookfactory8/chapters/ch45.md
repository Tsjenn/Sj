# Alphabet

In June 2017, eight researchers at Google published a paper called "Attention Is All You Need." It described a new neural-network architecture, the transformer, and it is the founding document of everything this book calls the model layer: the T in GPT stands for it. Every frontier chat assistant now competing with Google Search descends from work done inside Google, published freely by Google, and presented at an academic conference in December 2017 while Google's core business carried on printing money above it. All eight authors have since left the company, and the retrospectives that now attend the paper's anniversaries note where they went: to found and to lead a striking share of the companies that compete with Google today.

That is the situation this chapter examines, because Alphabet is the cleanest case in the book of a problem every incumbent dreads and few survive: the company owns the most profitable application in the history of software, it invented the technology most likely to disrupt that application, and it must now build the disruptor at full speed while defending the incumbent at full margin — under court supervision, with a hedge quietly parked on the balance sheet.

Alphabet's market capitalisation stood at roughly 4.20 trillion US dollars as at mid-August 2026, per companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings — behind Nvidia at 5.3 to 5.4 trillion and Apple at roughly 4.41 trillion, ahead of Microsoft at roughly 3.66 trillion, and, as always in this book, a photograph rather than a fact. What the photograph was pricing is the question the rest of the chapter works through.

## The machine being defended

Begin with what is at stake, in the only terms that matter here: the revenue line.

For the second quarter of 2026, reported on 22 July 2026, Alphabet's revenue was 119.8 billion US dollars, up twenty-four per cent year on year, per CNBC and Yahoo Finance coverage of the results. Within that, Google Search and other advertising produced 63.3 billion dollars, up seventeen per cent; Google's advertising in total, including YouTube's 11.1 billion, came to 81.6 billion, per the same results coverage. Search alone, in other words, is a business with a run-rate in the region of a quarter of a trillion dollars a year, still growing in the high teens, twenty-eight years after the company was founded.

It is worth being precise about why this one page of the internet became the most profitable application ever built, because the mechanics explain both the moat and the vulnerability.

A search engine sits at the exact moment of expressed intent. A person who types a query has just told the machine what they want, in their own words, at the instant they want it. An auction then runs — advertisers bidding for the right to appear beside that expressed intent — and the auction clears at whatever the intent is worth. Nothing else in advertising has this property. Television guesses at intent from demographics; social media infers it from behaviour; search is handed it in writing. The result is an advertising business with the highest-value inventory in the industry and a cost of goods that is mostly computing and traffic acquisition.

That second cost deserves a line of its own, because a court case dragged it into the light. Google pays to be the default search engine on other companies' devices and browsers, and the largest of those payments goes to Apple. The figure was one of the most closely guarded numbers in the industry until it surfaced in unsealed documents in the United States Department of Justice's antitrust case: Google paid Apple approximately twenty billion dollars in 2022 for default placement in Safari, as reported by Bloomberg in May 2024, an arrangement trial coverage described as a revenue share of thirty-six per cent of what Google earns from Safari search traffic. Read that as an accountant reads it. A company does not pay twenty billion dollars a year for a default unless the default is worth more than twenty billion dollars a year, and the willingness to pay it is itself a disclosure: distribution — being the box people type into without thinking — is the asset, and Google prices that asset annually, in cash, better than any analyst can.

The strategy that follows from these mechanics has been consistent for two decades: be the default everywhere, keep the results good enough that nobody resents the default, and let the auction do the rest. It produced the margin that funds everything else in this chapter — the research, the cloud, the chips, the bets. It also produced the court judgment that hangs over all of it, which a later section takes up.

## The paper that armed the challengers

Now the uncomfortable half of the story. Google did not merely fail to prevent the technology that threatens search; it created it, funded it, published it and, for several years, declined to ship it.

The record is easy to establish because so much of it is public. Google acquired DeepMind, the London research laboratory, in January 2014, for a price the company never confirmed; TechCrunch reported it at the time as more than five hundred million dollars, and contemporary reports ranged from roughly four hundred to six hundred and fifty million. Google Brain, the company's in-house deep-learning group, produced the transformer paper in 2017. The compute layer's chapter on Google Cloud and the TPU traced the hardware side: a chip programme started in 2013 and deployed in 2015, which meant Google possessed, before anyone else, every ingredient of the modern AI stack — the architecture, the research talent, the training infrastructure and the proprietary silicon to run it on.

What it did not do was put a conversational answer engine in front of its users. The reason is not mysterious, and it is not incompetence. It is arithmetic. A company earning the majority of its revenue from an auction that runs beside ten blue links has no financial incentive to replace the ten blue links with a single spoken answer, because nobody had demonstrated how to run a sixty-three-billion-dollar-a-quarter auction beside a single spoken answer. Every incumbent in business history has faced some version of this trap — the new thing cannibalises the old thing, the old thing pays for everything, and so the new thing waits — and the standard account of the trap says the incumbent hesitates until an outsider, with no revenue to protect, forces the issue.

That is precisely what happened. The outsiders built on the published architecture, hired a good number of the people who invented it, and shipped. The model layer's chapters told that story from the challengers' side. From Alphabet's side, the launch of consumer chat assistants by companies with nothing to lose converted a comfortable research lead into a public emergency: the company was widely portrayed through 2023 as having been caught flat-footed by a technology it had invented, and it reorganised accordingly, folding Brain and DeepMind together and putting the combined organisation behind a single model family, Gemini.

The score, three years on, is genuinely mixed, and both halves belong in the record. The half that flatters Alphabet: Gemini's consumer application reached 950 million monthly active users by the second quarter of 2026, up from a reported 750 million at the fourth-quarter 2025 results, with daily usage reported as having tripled year on year, per Forbes and CNBC coverage of the July 2026 earnings call. Those are the company's own operating metrics, unaudited and defined by the company, but the direction is steep. The half that does not: the standard twenty-dollar consumer subscription and the enterprise seat — the prices this part of the book opened with — were set by Alphabet's competitors, and the competitors' names, not Gemini's, became the category's shorthand. Being second into a market your own paper created is a strange kind of failure, and a recoverable one; but it is the reason the rest of this chapter exists.

## Defending and disrupting at once

The strategic problem, stated plainly: Alphabet must now run both sides of a disruption simultaneously. It must defend search, because search pays for everything. It must build the thing that could replace search, because if it does not, someone else will, and the history above shows the someone else is real. And it must do both inside one income statement, one capital budget and one share price.

Watch how this shows up in the product itself. Google has been folding generative answers into the search page — AI-generated summaries above the traditional results — which is the only move available to a company in its position: cannibalise yourself slowly, on your own page, where the auction still runs, rather than let the cannibalisation happen on someone else's page, where it does not. The bet is that the auction can be rebuilt around answers, the way it was once built around links.

Can it? The honest answer, as at mid-2026, is that the accounts do not yet say. What they do say is striking on its own: search advertising revenue grew seventeen per cent year on year in the second quarter of 2026, per the July 2026 results coverage — an acceleration, not a decline, three and a half years into the chat-assistant era. Whatever queries have leaked to chat assistants, the revenue line has not yet noticed. There are several available explanations — commercial queries, the ones worth money, may be the last to leave; search volume may be growing beneath the shift; AI summaries may be lifting engagement — and the disclosures do not let an outsider separate them. The reader should treat the seventeen per cent as the single most important number to keep watching in this company, and treat any confident story about *why* it is still growing, told from outside the company, as a story.

The second person this book is written for — the reader with the annual report open — should also note what a transition like this does to the cost side, because it is larger than the revenue question and it is visible now. Serving a generative answer costs more compute than serving ten links; that is the model layer's economics arriving inside the application layer. Alphabet raised its 2026 capital-expenditure guidance to 195 to 205 billion dollars at the July 2026 results, and its shares fell on the announcement, per CNBC's coverage — the market applauding twenty-four per cent revenue growth and wincing at what it now costs to defend it. A decade ago Alphabet was a company whose product cost almost nothing to serve at the margin. The strategic problem of this chapter has an accounting signature, and that capex line is it.

## The integration advantage

Set against the strategic problem is the asset no other company in this book holds: Alphabet operates on all four layers of the stack, at scale, simultaneously.

| Layer | Alphabet's position | Where it shows in the accounts |
|---|---|---|
| Application | Search, YouTube, Workspace, the Gemini app | Advertising revenue 81.6 billion dollars in Q2 2026; subscriptions within "Google services" |
| Model | Gemini, built by the merged DeepMind organisation | Not separately disclosed; folded into segment costs |
| Compute | Google Cloud, third of the hyperscalers | Segment revenue 24.8 billion dollars in Q2 2026, up eighty-two per cent, operating income 8.8 billion |
| Silicon | The TPU, ten generations deep, designed with Broadcom | Not separately disclosed; inside Cloud and capex |

The segment figures are from Alphabet's second-quarter 2026 results as reported by CNBC and Yahoo Finance in July 2026; the two undisclosed rows are genuinely undisclosed, and this book will not fill them in.

The previous part examined the bottom two rows in detail, and their conclusion carries up the stack. A company that designs its own accelerator adds capacity at manufacturing cost rather than at a vendor's price; the eighty-two per cent Cloud growth, and a segment operating income that went from 2.8 to 8.8 billion dollars in a year per the same results coverage, are consistent with that mechanism even though the TPU's own economics are nowhere disclosed. What matters for this chapter is what integration does to the strategic problem above. The transition to AI-served search is, among other things, a gigantic increase in compute cost per user interaction. Every other application company in this part of the book meets that cost at a market price — paying a model vendor, who pays a cloud, who pays a chip vendor, each taking a margin. Alphabet meets it at cost, on its own silicon, in its own buildings, tuned to its own models. If generative search is a war of serving costs, Alphabet has the lowest serving costs available to anyone, and it is difficult to overstate how unusual it is for the defending incumbent to hold that particular advantage.

Integration also compounds in quieter places. Gemini is trained and served on TPUs, per 2024 reporting cited earlier in this book, so the model layer's improvements and the silicon layer's roadmap are planned in the same building. Workspace gives the models a distribution channel into paying enterprises that a standalone lab must build seat by seat. And the Cloud segment sells the whole stack to outsiders — including, in a turn examined below, to the rival lab Alphabet part-owns.

The caution, before this reads like advocacy: integration is an advantage, not a verdict. Vertically integrated incumbents have lost disruptions before, with every structural advantage intact, because the disadvantage — the reluctance to damage one's own revenue — sits in the same integrated structure as the strengths. The stack lowers Alphabet's costs. It does not answer the auction question. Both things are true at once.

## The regulatory overhang

On 5 August 2024, Judge Amit Mehta of the United States District Court for the District of Columbia concluded a years-long case brought by the Department of Justice with a sentence that will follow the company for decades: "Google is a monopolist, and it has acted as one to maintain its monopoly." The court found that Google had monopoly power in general search and general search text advertising, maintained through the web of default agreements described earlier — the browsers, the Android device makers, the carriers, the twenty-billion-dollar Apple arrangement, per coverage of the ruling by TechPolicy.Press and others at the time.

Liability was the 2024 story; the remedy was the 2025 one, and it mattered more to the market. On 2 September 2025 the same court declined the government's structural demands — Google would not be forced to divest the Chrome browser, and a contingent divestiture of Android was likewise rejected — and imposed conduct remedies instead: default-placement contracts limited to one-year terms, a prohibition on tying Google's products together in those deals, a requirement to share certain search index and user-interaction data (though not advertising data) with qualified competitors, and a technical committee to oversee compliance for six years, per CNBC and NPR reporting of the decision. Alphabet's shares jumped on the ruling, per CNBC the same day — the clearest possible market statement that the outcome, monopoly finding and all, was lighter than feared. The final judgment embodying the remedies was entered in December 2025, per CNBC's report of 5 December 2025.

Three observations belong in a book about accounts rather than law.

First, the remedy reaches directly into the cost structure described earlier. The default payments — the twenty billion dollars to Apple and its smaller siblings — were the moat's carrying cost, contracted years at a time. One-year terms convert a settled arrangement into an annual renegotiation in which the counterparties now have alternatives to threaten with. Whether traffic-acquisition costs rise (Apple extracting more for a shorter commitment) or fall (Google paying less for a weaker exclusive) is exactly the kind of thing the next several annual reports will quietly disclose, and the reader now knows which line to watch.

Second, the data-sharing remedy is aimed at the future, not the past. Search index and interaction data are inputs a competing answer engine needs. A court has ordered the incumbent to provision its challengers — a remedy that would have been unthinkable in scope a decade ago and whose effects, six years out, nobody can honestly project. This book will not try.

Third, and most striking: the disruption threatening Google is part of what spared it. Coverage of the remedies decision was explicit that the rise of AI chat assistants as potential search competitors weighed against the harsher structural remedies — the court declining to break up a monopoly partly because technology was already contesting it. Alphabet thus finds itself in a genuinely odd position, which the reader should hold without resolving: the competitive threat examined in this chapter is simultaneously the strongest argument its lawyers have. A company defending in court on the theory that AI could erode search, while assuring investors of the opposite, is not lying to either audience. It is living on both branches of a probability tree, which is what the next section is about.

## The hedge

In 2025, filings in the search case disclosed a number Alphabet had never volunteered: Google owned approximately fourteen per cent of Anthropic — the laboratory behind the Claude models, and one of the two most important companies of this book's model layer — while holding no voting rights, no board seat and no observer rights, with its ownership capped, per the filings, at fifteen per cent, as reported by The New York Times and picked up widely at the time.

Assemble the full position from the 2026 reporting cited across this book, because no single disclosure assembles it for you. Google's committed investment in Anthropic has been reported at up to 43 billion dollars, including a 10 billion dollar tranche in April 2026. Anthropic closed a sixty-five-billion-dollar Series H at a post-money valuation of 965 billion dollars in May 2026, per CNBC — briefly the most valuable private company in the world. And in October 2025, Google and Anthropic announced an expansion of Anthropic's use of Google Cloud TPUs to as many as one million chips, bringing over a gigawatt of capacity online in 2026, in a deal CNBC reported as worth tens of billions of dollars.

So Alphabet is, at once: Anthropic's part-owner, one of its largest financiers, its silicon supplier and its landlord — while running Gemini in direct competition with Claude, and while Amazon, Alphabet's chief cloud rival, holds its own reported commitment of up to 33 billion dollars to the same company. Part Six of this book examines the circularity in these arrangements — the investor funding the customer who buys the investor's chips — as a structural feature of the whole industry. Here, the question is narrower: what is the stake *for*, from Alphabet's side of the table?

The honest answer is that it is a hedge, and a well-constructed one. Consider the branches. If Gemini wins the model race, the Anthropic stake is a profitable side bet on the second-place finisher. If Claude wins, Alphabet owns fourteen per cent of the winner — a larger economic interest in the disruptor than any other search incumbent could buy today at any price — and collects the winner's compute bill through Google Cloud regardless. The fifteen per cent cap and the absence of any governance rights, which read at first like weakness, are better read as design: a stake large enough to matter economically, small enough to survive regulatory scrutiny of exactly the kind the previous section described, and passive enough that Anthropic's independence — commercially essential to a lab selling trust — remains intact. Alphabet has arranged to profit from its own disruption without controlling it.

The accountant's job is to notice where this shows up, because it now moves the reported numbers materially. Alphabet's second-quarter 2026 net income of 112.1 billion dollars included roughly 98 billion of other income, largely gains on equity securities, per July 2026 results coverage — much of it the paper appreciation of exactly these kinds of stakes, marked up as private rounds reprice them. CNBC reported in August 2026 that Big Tech's holdings in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the earnings picture of the companies holding them, and the distortion is worth spelling out: these are unrealised gains, priced by the last private round rather than by any market a holder could actually sell into, flowing through an income statement most readers take as a record of operations. A reader who sees Alphabet's quarterly profit nearly triple should reach for the other-income note before reaching for any conclusion. The operating business had a good quarter; the balance sheet had a spectacular one; and only one of those is repeatable on purpose.

## What to watch in the accounts

Alphabet resolves, like every company in this book, into a short list of checkable things, and the list is this chapter in miniature.

Watch search advertising revenue, quarterly, against its own growth rate. Seventeen per cent in the second quarter of 2026 is the number that says disruption has not yet reached the revenue line; the first sustained deceleration will be the most consequential data point in the application layer, and it will appear in a segment table long before it appears in anyone's narrative.

Watch traffic-acquisition costs now that the default contracts run year to year under the December 2025 final judgment. That line is the annually repriced value of distribution, disclosed in the filings, and the remedies have turned it into a live negotiation.

Watch the Cloud segment's margin as TPU-heavy capacity comes online, and capital expenditure — 195 to 205 billion dollars guided for 2026, per the July 2026 results coverage — against the depreciation that will follow it into the income statement for years.

And watch the other-income line, where the hedge lives. The Anthropic stake will be repriced every time the private market reprices Anthropic, and none of it is cash until somebody sells something.

None of this is a recommendation to buy or sell anything; this book does not give those, and a market capitalisation of 4.20 trillion dollars as at August 2026 will have moved by the time you read this sentence — the current figure is a few keystrokes away, which is rather the point of the company. What the accounts show, and will keep showing on a quarterly schedule, is the strangest strategic position in the industry being managed in public: the inventor of the transformer defending the business the transformer threatens, with the deepest stack in the industry beneath it, a court looking over its shoulder, and fourteen per cent of its most credible rival tucked into other income. The company that wrote "Attention Is All You Need" is now obliged to pay attention to everything at once.
