# Inference Economics

On 27 January 2025 Nvidia lost five hundred and eighty-nine billion US dollars of market value in a single trading session, a fall of about seventeen per cent and the largest one-day loss in the history of American markets, as reported that day by CNBC, Bloomberg and Forbes. The cause was not a product failure or a lost customer. It was a research paper and a price list from a Chinese laboratory, and the inference the market drew from them: that answering a question with a machine had just become much cheaper, and that a world needing less compute per answer would need fewer chips.

Before the American market opened the following morning, Microsoft's chief executive posted a single sentence publicly. "Jevons paradox strikes again! As AI gets more efficient and accessible, we will see its use skyrocket, turning it into a commodity we just can't get enough of." Satya Nadella wrote that on 27 January 2025; it was covered within hours by Fortune, GeekWire and the rest of the trade press.

Two intelligent readings of the same fact, reached within a day of each other, pointing in opposite directions. Both cannot be right, and which one is right decides whether the model layer ever earns a return on the capital it has consumed. This chapter is about the arithmetic underneath the disagreement.

## Two costs, and only one of them comes back

Everything in this chapter follows from a distinction that the accounts make and the headlines do not.

Training a frontier model is a one-off cost. It is enormous, it is incurred before a single customer is served, and — as chapter thirty-three set out — it is almost entirely expensed as it is incurred rather than capitalised, so it lands in the income statement in a lump, below the gross margin line, in research and development. Once the run is finished, the cost is sunk. Serving the ten-millionth answer from that model costs nothing extra in training terms.

Inference is a per-use cost. Every answer consumes machine time: a slice of an accelerator, memory bandwidth, electricity, network. That cost is incurred each time, it scales directly with usage, and it sits in cost of revenue. It is what stands between the price a customer pays and the gross profit a lab reports.

This is why the model layer's financial behaviour is so unlike the software business it superficially resembles. In conventional software the marginal cost of one more user is close to zero, the cost base is nearly all fixed, and gross margins of eighty per cent and above are ordinary. In the model layer the cost base has a large variable component that arrives with every request. On the figures reported in 2026 — unaudited, and discussed at length in chapter thirty-three — Anthropic's compute cost ran at about seventy-one cents for every dollar of revenue in the first quarter of 2026, with a projected fifty-six cents in the second and gross margin tracking around forty-four per cent, per figures shared with investors and reported by PitchBook and Morningstar; OpenAI's first-quarter 2026 gross margin was reported by The Information at roughly thirty-nine per cent, against about thirty-three per cent a year earlier.

Those are manufacturing margins, not software margins. A business whose cost of sales moves with volume is a business with a unit economic problem to solve, and the unit is the answer.

Consider what that means for pricing. Take an illustrative lab, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It spends four billion dollars training a model. Serving one answer costs it two cents. If it expects to serve one hundred billion answers over the model's life, the training cost spread across those answers is four cents each, and a price of ten cents an answer covers both with margin to spare. If it serves only ten billion, the training cost is forty cents an answer and the same ten-cent price is catastrophic. The variable cost has not changed at all in those two cases. What changed is the volume over which the fixed cost is recovered.

That is the first structural point, and it is easy to miss because it runs against instinct: for a business with a large one-off cost and a modest per-use cost, volume is not merely nice to have. Volume is the entire recovery mechanism for the largest expense on the income statement. A model company that halves its price and quadruples its volume is not being reckless. It may be doing the only thing that makes the training bill recoverable.

## What "cost per token" actually contains

The industry's unit of account is the token — roughly three-quarters of a word — and the phrase "cost per token" is used as though it named a single number. It does not. Before any argument about falling costs can be assessed, the unit has to be unpacked, because almost every dispute about inference economics turns out to be a dispute about which cost is being quoted.

Prices are quoted separately for tokens going in and tokens coming out, and output is several times dearer than input. As at mid-2026, per the vendors' published price lists compiled by API pricing trackers, flagship models from the leading American labs were listed at about five dollars per million input tokens and twenty-five to thirty dollars per million output tokens, mid-tier models at around three dollars in and fifteen out, and small fast models at a dollar or less. Batch processing was offered at half price. Cached input — text the model has already processed and can reuse — was discounted by roughly ninety per cent.

That last one deserves attention, because it means a customer's effective price can fall by a large margin without any published price changing at all. An application that restructures its prompts to hit the cache pays a fraction of list. When analysts report that blended realised pricing has fallen, part of what they are measuring is not vendor generosity but customer engineering.

Underneath the price list sit at least three separate cost curves, and they do not move together.

The first is hardware. Nvidia stated when it announced the platform that its GB200 NVL72 system reduced cost and energy consumption for large-model inference by up to twenty-five times against the same number of H100 accelerators, and in 2026 the company published a claim that its Blackwell Ultra generation delivered up to thirty-five times lower cost per token than the Hopper generation for low-latency agentic workloads, citing SemiAnalysis InferenceX benchmarks from the first quarter of 2026. Read those as what they are: vendor-selected comparisons, on workloads the vendor chose, published by the party with the strongest possible interest in the answer. The direction is not in serious dispute; the multiple is marketing until an independent party reproduces it.

The second is serving software and operations. Batching requests together, keeping accelerators busy, caching what can be cached, routing short requests to small models and long ones to large — none of this changes the chip and all of it changes the cost per answer. This is unglamorous engineering that does not make announcements, and it is probably where the most reliable gains have come from.

The third is the model itself. Distillation, quantisation, sparsity and mixture-of-experts architectures reduce the arithmetic required per token. This is the curve that the January 2025 shock was about, and it is the one that moves in jumps rather than smoothly.

| What falls | Who controls it | How fast it moves | Whether it is proprietary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost of the accelerator per unit of work | Chip vendors, on their own schedule | In generations, roughly annually | No — every lab can buy the same part |
| Utilisation and serving efficiency | The lab, or its cloud | Continuously, in small increments | Partly, until competitors copy it |
| Arithmetic required per token | Model researchers everywhere | In jumps, when a method is published | Rarely, once published |
| Effective price paid, via caching and batching | The customer | Whenever the customer engineers for it | No — it is a discount, not a gain |

The last row is the one to keep hold of. Three of these four make the industry cheaper; only the middle one, and only temporarily, makes any single lab cheaper than its neighbours. That distinction decides who keeps the saving, and the chapter returns to it.

How far have costs actually fallen? The most defensible statement is directional. GPT-4's API launched in March 2023 at thirty dollars per million input tokens and sixty per million output. Three years later, capable models were being listed at a small fraction of that, and 2026 reporting cited in chapter one put inference cost per query down roughly ninety-five per cent since that launch. Epoch AI's published work on inference price trends makes the important qualification: the rate of decline depends entirely on what capability level you hold constant, and measured that way the fall ranges across orders of magnitude depending on the task chosen. There is no single number, and anyone quoting one has picked a benchmark without telling you.

## Falling price, rising revenue

Now to the central question. If the price of a token falls by ninety per cent, does the revenue of the companies selling tokens fall?

Only if the number of tokens sold rises by less than tenfold. Revenue is price multiplied by volume, and the entire argument is about the second term.

The evidence available in 2026 was one-sided. Google's chief executive told the company's developer conference in May 2026 that Google was processing over three point two quadrillion tokens a month, against four hundred and eighty trillion a year earlier and nine point seven trillion two years before that, per the coverage of that keynote. That is a sevenfold increase in a year and a rise of more than three hundred-fold in two, over a period when the published price of a token fell substantially. No plausible price decline outruns that.

The same pattern shows up in narrower data. Blended prices paid per million tokens were reported falling from eighteen dollars forty to six dollars seven between the first quarter of 2025 and the first quarter of 2026 — roughly sixty-seven per cent — in 2026 trade analysis. One platform's 2026 data showed, within a single month, volume up fifty-nine per cent, spend up thirty-seven per cent and average price paid per token down thirteen point six per cent. That is the whole mechanism in one line: the price fell, the volume rose faster, and the money going into the layer increased.

And the labs' own reported figures moved the same way. Reuters reporting across 2025 and 2026 traced Anthropic's annualised run rate from roughly one billion dollars at the start of 2025 to more than five billion by August 2025, thirty billion by April 2026 and past forty-seven billion in early May, with CNBC reporting sixty-five billion at the end of July 2026. Run rates are monthly figures multiplied by twelve and are not audited, as this book keeps saying. But no reading of them is consistent with falling prices having shrunk the model layer's revenue.

So Nadella's side of the January 2025 argument has, so far, been the better description of what happened. It is worth being precise about why, and about the conditions under which it would stop being true.

William Stanley Jevons observed in 1865 that improvements in the efficiency of the steam engine had increased, not decreased, Britain's consumption of coal. The mechanism was that cheaper steam power made a great many previously uneconomic uses economic. The observation generalises only where two conditions hold: there is a large reservoir of latent demand that price was suppressing, and consumption is not otherwise constrained. Where demand is already satisfied, efficiency reduces consumption in the ordinary way. Nobody buys a second refrigerator because the first became cheaper to run.

The relevant question, then, is not whether some general law applies. It is whether the reservoir of latent demand for machine-generated answers is still large. In 2026 it plainly was: enormous categories of work — reading every document, checking every record, drafting every routine reply — were uneconomic at thirty dollars a million tokens and became economic somewhere below that. Whether that remains true in 2030 is exactly the sort of thing a reader of this book should watch rather than assume.

An illustrative table makes the arithmetic explicit. Round numbers, chosen for clarity, belonging to no real company.

| Scenario | Price change | Volume change | Revenue effect | Gross margin effect if unit cost falls 60% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic demand | −50% | +200% | +50% | Improves |
| Unit elastic | −50% | +100% | Unchanged | Improves |
| Inelastic demand | −50% | +40% | −30% | Improves per unit, worse in total |
| Price war without cost gain | −50% | +100% | Unchanged | Collapses |

Notice the fourth row. Falling prices are only benign when they are funded by falling costs. A price cut made to hold market share, against a cost base that has not moved, transfers margin to the customer and nothing else. From outside the company, the two look identical: the price list is public and the cost base is not.

## The unit that matters is not the token

Here is the complication that most discussion of falling inference costs omits, and it is the most important operational fact in this chapter.

The customer does not buy tokens. The customer buys a completed task — a document reviewed, a bug fixed, a case summarised. Tokens are the input to that task, and the number of tokens a task requires has been rising sharply.

Two developments drive it. The first is reasoning: models that generate intermediate working before answering consume far more output tokens per question than models that answer immediately, and the amount varies enormously with the effort setting and even between runs of the same query. The second is agentic use: a system that plans, calls tools, reads results and tries again re-sends its accumulated context at every step, so token consumption compounds with the number of steps. A 2026 Gartner analysis cited across the trade press put agentic workflows at five to thirty times the tokens of a single chatbot exchange, and studies of coding agents have reported far higher multiples on long tasks, driven mostly by input tokens being re-read.

Put those together with falling token prices and the result is a genuine paradox for the buyer: the price of a token falls while the bill goes up. Both facts are true, and finance departments across 2026 discovered them in that order.

For the model layer, this is the best news in the chapter, and the reason to be careful with the phrase "commoditisation". A commodity market in tokens does not imply a commodity market in tasks. If a lab's model completes a task in a third of the tokens a rival needs, it can charge more per token and still be cheaper per task — and the customer, who is paying for tasks, will choose it. Efficiency at the task level is a real and defensible advantage, and it is invisible in a price-per-million-tokens comparison table.

It also reframes what falling costs are for. The declining cost curve is not primarily a margin story. It is what makes the next tier of applications possible at all: work that was uneconomic at one price becomes economic at a tenth of it, and that work arrives as new volume rather than as a saving on old volume. The model layer is, on this reading, buying its own future demand with its cost reductions. Whether it is also buying margin depends on what happens next.

## The four conditions for keeping the margin

Cheaper inference does not automatically make the model layer profitable, and it does not automatically make it poorer. Which happens depends on four conditions, each of which can be checked against disclosed numbers as they appear.

**The first is that cost falls faster than price.** Gross margin is the gap between them, and both are moving. On the reported 2026 figures at both leading labs, margin improved during a period when published prices were falling — which means cost was falling faster. That is the whole of the mechanism, and it is why margin can improve in a price war. It is also why the source of a cost gain matters more than its size. A gain that comes from a chip vendor arrives for every lab that buys the chip and is therefore competed away; a gain from a lab's own serving stack or model architecture is retained until it is copied. In 2026 the largest single cost improvement available to anyone — a new accelerator generation — was available to everyone.

**The second is that demand is elastic enough to convert a price cut into more revenue.** This has been comfortably satisfied so far, on the volume evidence above. The condition to watch is the shape of the elasticity as the market matures, and the honest position is that nobody knows where it turns.

**The third is that supply stays tight enough to put a floor under price.** This one is underappreciated. In a market where capacity is scarce, a seller does not need to cut price to fill it. Nvidia's chief executive said on the company's results call of 19 November 2025 that cloud GPUs were sold out, as reported by CNBC; through 2026 the trade press documented accelerator supply pre-committed to hyperscalers, high-bandwidth memory sold out and foundry capacity effectively spoken for, and chapter seventy-two examined the memory side of that in detail. Scarce compute means inference capacity cannot be added at will, which supports price.

But notice the trap in it. The same constraint that holds price up also caps volume growth, and volume is how the training bill gets recovered. A lab that cannot serve more customers cannot grow into its fixed costs, however good its price. Constrained supply protects the margin percentage and limits the absolute profit. Neither state is comfortable, and the industry has been oscillating between them.

**The fourth is that the customer cannot substitute freely.** Metered token pricing invites comparison, and comparison is what turns a product into a commodity. Chapter thirty-three noted the routing tools that exist specifically to send each request to whichever model is cheapest that week. Against that, the labs have built exactly the defences that make substitution harder: enterprise agreements with committed spend, seats wired into a company's working habits, and — increasingly — task-level performance that does not show up in a price comparison at all.

| Condition | What it looks like in the numbers | What breaks it |
|---|---|---|
| Cost falling faster than price | Gross margin rising while list prices fall | An industry-wide efficiency gain everyone receives at once |
| Demand elastic | Volume growth outrunning price decline | Saturation of the uses that price was suppressing |
| Supply tight | Capacity sold out; discounting rare | A capacity build arriving faster than demand |
| Substitution difficult | Committed contracts, low churn, task-level advantage | Routing tools and near-parity open models |

Two of those four conditions were plainly holding in 2026. One — cost falling faster than price — was holding on unaudited figures at two companies. The fourth was contested, and the contest was public.

## The pass-through problem

The August 2026 evidence on pass-through was direct. Reuters reported that OpenAI cut developer pricing for its frontier model by more than twenty per cent for a three-month period, taking it from five to four dollars per million input tokens and from thirty to twenty dollars per million output tokens, in the face of competition from Anthropic and from Chinese models. The same reporting noted a July cut of twenty per cent on another frontier tier and eighty per cent on a smaller model. Those are not the actions of a company that expects to keep an efficiency gain.

Set against that, analysis of enterprise cloud spending published by YipitData in 2026 found a striking split. In the developer market, blended token pricing on the open routing platforms fell by roughly fifteen per cent between the start of 2026 and the end of March, with the index down about twenty per cent from its peak between May and July, as developers switched providers and adopted cheaper open models the moment they became usable. In enterprise cloud data drawn from a panel representing around twenty billion dollars of annualised cloud spend, effective pricing across providers had not meaningfully changed over the same period, and customers were putting more of their volume onto premium models rather than less.

Two markets, one product, opposite behaviour. The developer buys tokens and optimises the bill. The enterprise buys an outcome inside a compliance perimeter, has already spent months integrating one vendor, and treats a fifteen per cent token saving as not worth the disruption. If the enterprise mix keeps growing — chapter thirty-three cited third-party profiles putting roughly eighty per cent of Anthropic's revenue on the API and business side rather than the consumer side — then the blended realised price the labs achieve will fall more slowly than the published price list suggests.

There is a further wrinkle in the flat-rate lines, and it points the other way. A consumer subscription priced at twenty dollars a month does not reprice when inference gets cheaper. Every cent taken off the cost of an answer drops straight into the gross margin of that subscription. Falling inference costs therefore repair the flat-rate lines automatically and repair the metered lines only if the lab declines to pass the saving on. Mix is not a detail here. It determines how much of an efficiency gain survives contact with the price list.

Which suggests a conclusion worth stating plainly, because it is the durable part of this chapter. Cheaper inference is unambiguously good for the model layer's costs and ambiguous for its prices. Whether it becomes profit depends on competitive structure, not on technology. The technology delivers the cost reduction to everybody at roughly the same time.

## What to watch after these numbers go stale

Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot of a market that moves weekly, and most of the important ones come from companies that publish no accounts. What follows is the durable part: the questions that will still be the right questions when the numbers above are years out of date.

| What to watch | Where it shows up | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin trend, not level | Reported figures, investor disclosures | Whether cost is falling faster than price |
| Blended realised price against list price | Third-party spend panels, routing platforms | How much of the list price is actually collected |
| Tokens served, over time | Vendor keynote disclosures, cloud commentary | Whether volume is still outrunning price |
| Cost per completed task | Almost nowhere; must be inferred | The only unit the customer actually buys |
| Where free-tier inference is charged | Undisclosed for private labs | Whether the stated margin is comparable to anything |
| Committed contract terms and duration | Disclosed only in fragments | How much revenue is insulated from spot pricing |
| Capacity commitments against revenue | Cloud and chip vendor disclosures | Whether the fixed cost base is growing faster than the volume that recovers it |

Three habits go with that list.

Ask what a quoted cost decline holds constant. A price that fell ninety-five per cent for a fixed capability level is a different statement from a price that fell ninety-five per cent for the best available model, and the second is usually not true. Epoch AI's work on inference pricing is explicit that the measured decline depends on the benchmark chosen.

Ask whether a margin improvement was earned or received. A lab whose costs fell because it engineered its serving stack has done something durable. A lab whose costs fell because its cloud provider passed on a new accelerator generation has received something its competitors also received. The reported margin looks identical in both cases.

And ask who is paying for the capacity. The model layer's cost of sales is the compute layer's revenue, and the compute layer's capital expenditure is the semiconductor layer's revenue. An efficiency gain at the top of that chain reduces the demand for machine-hours per answer, and an expansion of usage increases it. Which effect dominates has, so far, been decided emphatically in favour of expansion — which is the reason the market's judgement of 27 January 2025 was reversed in the months that followed, and the reason that reversal should not be mistaken for a permanent law.

None of this is investment advice, and nothing here is a forecast of any company's share price or valuation. It is a way of reading a set of numbers that will keep being published.

The durable formulation is the simplest one. A model company has a large one-off cost that only volume can recover, and a per-use cost that only efficiency can reduce. Efficiency is arriving quickly and arriving for everyone. Volume is arriving quickly too, and so far faster than price has fallen. The model layer keeps margin exactly to the extent that its own cost curve falls faster than the price its customers can negotiate — and since most of the cost curve is delivered by the layers below, on a schedule the layers below control, the model layer's profitability has never been entirely in the model layer's hands. That is the same conclusion this book has reached from four other directions, and it is the reason the next chapter asks who could still enter, and where.
