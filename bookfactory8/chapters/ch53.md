# How to Read a Cross-Holding

Amazon reported net income of sixty-two point six billion US dollars for the second quarter of 2026, against eighteen point two billion in the same quarter of 2025. Operating income for the quarter was twenty-seven point five billion. The gap between those two numbers is the subject of this chapter: the company's own results announcement of 30 July 2026 stated that net income included non-operating pre-tax other income of fifty-three point four billion dollars, primarily from investments in Anthropic, and the figure was widely reported at the time, including by CNBC.

Read that again slowly. More than half of the largest profit line Amazon has ever printed did not come from selling anything. It came from a change in the estimated value of shares in a private company that Amazon has not sold, cannot readily sell, and did not receive a penny of cash for during the quarter. Nothing about that is improper. It is what the accounting rules require, applied correctly, and Amazon disclosed it in the first paragraph of its own announcement.

But it means that anyone reading the income statement of a large technology company in this cycle is now, unavoidably, reading an accounting judgement about somebody else's business. This chapter is the toolkit for that. It sets out the three regimes a cross-holding can fall into, why the choice between them changes the investor's reported earnings without changing the investor's economics by a cent, and why a stake that arrives alongside a supply contract is a fundamentally different instrument from a stake that arrives alone.

## The question is influence, not size

The instinctive way to read a cross-holding is by percentage. Twenty-seven per cent sounds like a lot; four per cent sounds like a rounding error. The accounting frameworks do not think that way. They ask what the holder can *do*, and the percentage is only evidence.

Three answers are possible, and they produce three completely different sets of financial statements.

| | Passive investment | Equity method | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Test applied | No significant influence | Significant influence over financial and operating policy | Power over the investee, exposure to variable returns, ability to use that power |
| Usual evidence | Small stake, no board seat, non-voting shares | Board representation, participation in policy, material transactions, technological dependency | Majority of voting rights, or contractual power |
| Percentage rule of thumb | Under 20 per cent | 20 per cent or more, presumed but rebuttable | More than 50 per cent, or control by other means |
| Balance sheet | Carried at fair value, or at cost adjusted for observable price changes | Carrying amount moves with the investee's profits, losses and distributions | Every asset and liability of the investee, line by line |
| Income statement | Fair value movements, when they occur | The holder's share of the investee's profit or loss, one line | The investee's whole revenue and whole costs, with the outside share stripped out below |
| Investee's revenue appears in holder's revenue? | No | No | Yes, in full |
| Main US and IFRS references | ASC 321 and IFRS 9 | ASC 323 and IAS 28 | ASC 810 and IFRS 10 |

The twenty per cent line is a presumption in both frameworks, and presumptions can be rebutted in either direction. A holder with twenty-five per cent and no board seat, no information rights and a shareholders' agreement that excludes it from policy decisions may not have significant influence. A holder with twelve per cent, a board seat, veto rights over the budget and a supply relationship the investee cannot replace may well have it. The paperwork decides, not the arithmetic, and the paperwork is usually the part that is not public.

That is the first practical lesson. When you see a stake reported as a percentage in a news story, you have been told the least informative fact about it.

### Regime one: the passive stake, and why it moves

Most of the cross-holdings in this industry sit in the first column, and the first column contains a trap.

Under US rules, equity investments are generally carried at fair value with movements running through net income. Where an investment has no readily determinable fair value — which describes every stake in a private laboratory — a company may elect what the standard calls the measurement alternative: cost, less impairment, adjusted for observable price changes in orderly transactions for the identical or a similar instrument of the same issuer.

Read the last clause again, because it is the engine of the whole thing. The carrying amount does not move because the investee grew, or shipped a product, or won customers. It moves when somebody buys shares at a new price. A funding round is an observable transaction. When one closes, every holder of that class of shares must revalue, and the revaluation runs through profit.

This is why the gains arrive in lumps, and why they arrive on the calendar of the investee's fundraising rather than the investor's operations. Anthropic closed a sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, as reported by CNBC in May 2026. The consequences showed up two months later in the accounts of two listed companies that had nothing to do with each other except a shareholder register.

Amazon's fifty-three point four billion dollar non-operating gain, described above, landed in the quarter to 30 June 2026. Earlier the same year, in the first quarter of 2026, Amazon's net income of about thirty point three billion dollars included a pre-tax mark-to-market gain of roughly sixteen point eight billion dollars on the same investment, as reported by Fortune in April 2026. Alphabet, in its second quarter of 2026, reported net income of one hundred and twelve point one billion dollars, up around two hundred and ninety-eight per cent year on year, with diluted earnings per share of nine dollars eleven; the company's own release attributed a net gain of ninety-eight point zero billion dollars in other income primarily to net unrealised gains on its equity holdings, principally its stakes in Anthropic and SpaceX, as reported by Fortune on 22 July 2026.

Strip the gains out and the underlying quarter was ordinary. Coverage of the same results, including by TechTimes in July 2026, put Alphabet's earnings per share excluding the unrealised gains at about two dollars sixty-two, against roughly two dollars sixty-three that analysts had modelled — a penny short of expectations, inside the largest profit quarter in the company's history.

Two features of this regime deserve to be permanently in the reader's head.

The first is symmetry. A mechanism that writes an investment up on a rising round writes it down on a falling one. The gains that flattered these quarters were unrealised, and unrealised gains are reversible by the same rule that created them. A subsequent round at a lower price, or an impairment assessment that concludes the carrying amount is not recoverable, produces a charge of the same character and, potentially, of the same size. No cash entered on the way up. None leaves on the way down. Only the reported profit moves, in both directions.

The second is that the framework a company reports under can change the headline while changing nothing else. Under IFRS, equity investments are measured at fair value through profit or loss by default, but an entity may make an irrevocable election at initial recognition, for investments not held for trading, to present fair value changes in other comprehensive income instead — and those amounts are never recycled into profit, even on disposal. Two companies holding identical stakes in the same laboratory, one reporting under US rules and one under IFRS with that election made, would report the same equity and wildly different earnings per share. Before comparing two investors' profits, check which regime each is in. This book has made that point about inventory and about research costs; it applies with even more force here, because the amounts are larger.

There is also a valuation question sitting underneath. A private stake is measured at what the frameworks call a level three fair value: no quoted price, inputs that are unobservable, and a measurement that depends on judgement. The most persuasive input available is the price of the most recent round. Where the holder participated in that round, the input to its own valuation is a price it helped to set. That is not a scandal and it is not an accusation; it is the ordinary consequence of a market with few buyers. It is, however, exactly the sort of thing an auditor is required to think hard about, and exactly the sort of thing a reader should notice before treating a level three gain as equivalent to a realised one.

### Regime two: significant influence and the one-line window

The equity method is the strangest of the three, and the most useful to an outside analyst.

Where a holder has significant influence but not control, it does not consolidate and it does not mark to market. It records its share of the investee's profit or loss, period by period, as a single line in its own income statement, and adjusts the carrying amount of the investment by the same amount. Distributions received reduce the carrying amount rather than counting as income. The method is sometimes called one-line consolidation, which captures both what it does and what it hides.

The clearest example in this industry ran through Microsoft. In its quarterly filing published on 29 October 2025, Microsoft disclosed that its September-quarter net income was reduced by three point one billion dollars by its share of OpenAI's losses — a figure reported at the time by CNBC and The Register, the latter noting four point one billion before tax and inferring an OpenAI quarterly loss of something above eleven point five billion dollars from Microsoft's stake. Microsoft's holding was set at twenty-seven per cent when OpenAI completed its restructuring on 28 October 2025, a stake reported then as worth about one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars.

The inference in that paragraph is the press's, not the filing's, and this book flags the difference every time. But the mechanism is real, and it is remarkable: a listed company's income statement had become a monthly telemetry feed on a private competitor's cash burn.

Four properties of the equity method matter for reading it.

It is silent on revenue. However large the investee, not one dollar of its revenue appears in the holder's revenue line. An investor could hold a substantial share of the fastest-growing revenue in the industry and show nothing for it above the operating line.

It stops. Under both frameworks, equity-method losses are recognised only until the carrying amount of the investment — together with other long-term interests that in substance form part of the net investment — reaches zero. Beyond that, further losses go unrecognised unless the holder has a legal or constructive obligation to fund them, and any unrecognised losses must be made good out of later profits before income is recognised again. The practical consequence is important and almost never explained: a quarterly drag from an investee's losses can vanish without the investee's losses improving at all. If you see the charge disappear, check the carrying amount before you conclude anything cheerful.

It carries baggage from the price paid. Where a holder pays more than its share of the investee's net assets, the excess is not simply parked. Amounts attributable to depreciable or amortisable assets are unwound against the equity-method line over time, so the reported share of profit is not the same as the investee's profit multiplied by the stake. Anyone reversing the arithmetic to derive the investee's results is dividing by an assumption.

It gets tested as a whole. The investment is assessed for impairment as a single asset, rather than by looking through to the investee's individual assets. That test uses recoverable amount, which brings in projections, discount rates and judgement.

### Regime three: control, and the rule that surprises people

Control means consolidation. Every asset, every liability, every dollar of revenue and every dollar of cost enters the parent's financial statements as if the subsidiary were a division, and the share belonging to outside holders is presented separately: as non-controlling interests within equity, and as an allocation of profit below the net income line.

Consolidation has a consequence that catches readers out. Once control exists, transactions in the subsidiary's shares that do not change control are treated as transactions between owners — equity movements, not profits.

The clean illustration is Arm. SoftBank acquired Arm in 2016 and took it back to public markets in September 2023, retaining approximately eighty-nine point nine per cent of the shares after the underwriters' option was exercised, per SoftBank's own announcements of September 2023. Arm therefore remained a consolidated subsidiary, and SoftBank stated that the gain on the disposal would not be recorded in its consolidated statement of profit or loss. Billions of dollars were raised from public investors and the transaction produced no accounting profit at the group level, because in accounting terms the group had sold nothing to anyone outside itself; it had merely admitted new owners to a company it still controlled.

The reverse case is more dramatic, and worth knowing before it appears in a headline. When a parent loses control — by selling down, by diluting, or by a change in governance rights — it deconsolidates the subsidiary and remeasures any retained interest at fair value. That remeasurement frequently produces an enormous one-off accounting gain in the very period the parent gave something up. A company can book a profit for ceasing to control an asset it still substantially owns. If you ever see a technology group report a spectacular gain in a quarter when it appeared to be surrendering influence, the deconsolidation note is where the story is.

## The same investment, three sets of accounts

The cleanest way to see how much the treatment matters is to hold the economics fixed and change only the regime. Take an illustrative investor and an illustrative laboratory, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company.

The investor pays one thousand for a stake. In year one the laboratory loses two thousand. At the end of year one it raises new money at a price that values the investor's stake at four thousand. Assume the investor's own operating profit is five hundred in the year, and ignore tax.

| | Passive investment | Equity method (assume 20 per cent) | Control (assume 100 per cent) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating profit before the holding | 500 | 500 | 500 |
| Effect of the laboratory's loss | none | (400) | (2,000) on the face of the accounts |
| Effect of the new round | +3,000 gain | none | none |
| Reported profit for the year | 3,500 | 100 | (1,500) |
| Investment on the balance sheet | 4,000 | 600 | consolidated, no investment line |

Three profit figures — thirty-five hundred, one hundred, and a loss of fifteen hundred — from a single unchanged set of facts. The cash position is identical in all three columns. Not one of them is wrong. Each is the faithful application of a different rule to a different degree of influence.

That table is the reason this chapter exists. Reported earnings in this part of the industry are not a measurement of what happened. They are a measurement of what happened, filtered through a legal question about influence that most readers never think to ask.

It is also why the adjustment habit has spread so quickly. CNBC reported in August 2026 that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them. The market's response has been to look past the line: to strip out the investment gains, as the Alphabet coverage above did, and read the operating business underneath. That is the right instinct, provided it is applied in both directions. An analyst who excludes the gains in the good quarters and includes the charges in the bad ones is not adjusting. They are choosing.

## Rights, not percentages

Return to the point the frameworks keep making. What the holder can do matters more than how much it holds, and what a holder can do is set out in documents that outsiders mostly cannot see.

The instruments differ enormously. Ordinary shares carry votes. Preferred shares often carry a liquidation preference, meaning the holder is paid before ordinary holders in a sale, which makes the headline valuation an overstatement of what the ordinary shares are worth. Convertible notes are debt until an event converts them, and until then the holder is a creditor with a claim ahead of every shareholder. Amazon's exposure to Anthropic has been reported in 2026 coverage of its filings as taking the form of convertible notes and non-voting preferred stock — non-voting being the operative word for anyone trying to work out what the stake permits.

Around the instrument sit the rights that actually bind: a board seat or an observer seat, and the difference between them is the difference between voting and listening; information rights that determine whether the holder sees monthly management accounts or an annual summary; consent rights over budgets, new financings, or a sale; pre-emption rights on future rounds; rights of first refusal over the investee's commercial arrangements, which are commercial weapons rather than financial ones. When Microsoft's right of first refusal over OpenAI's compute lapsed in the October 2025 agreement, the effect was not on Microsoft's balance sheet at all. It was on who OpenAI could buy from.

Two stakes of identical size can therefore be entirely different assets. One is a passive financial exposure that moves when someone else prices it. The other is a seat at the table with a veto in its pocket. The financial statements distinguish them only through the treatment chosen — which is why the accounting policy note, the least-read page in any annual report, is where the answer is.

## The stake that comes with a contract

Now the part that separates a strategic holding from an investment.

An investment transfers risk. Capital leaves the investor, an asset appears on its balance sheet, and the investor's fortunes become partly dependent on the investee's. That is the whole of it.

A stake accompanied by a commercial agreement does something else. It transfers risk *and* creates a trading relationship — usually one in which the investor is also the supplier, and the money it invested comes back as its own revenue.

The examples are the largest arrangements in the industry. When OpenAI completed its restructuring on 28 October 2025, Microsoft took a twenty-seven per cent stake, and OpenAI contracted to purchase an incremental two hundred and fifty billion dollars of Azure cloud services, as reported by GeekWire, CNBC and Fortune on 28 October 2025. Amazon has committed up to thirty-three billion dollars to Anthropic and Google up to forty-three billion, including ten billion in April 2026, per 2026 reporting — and in both cases the commitment is entwined with compute supply, the investee running significant workloads on the investor's infrastructure.

For an accountant, four questions follow immediately, and they are questions about mechanism, not about anybody's conduct.

**Is the payment consideration for shares, or a discount on the sales?** Where a vendor pays cash or gives equity to a customer, the revenue standards require that consideration to be treated as a reduction of the transaction price, unless it is in exchange for a distinct good or service whose fair value can be reasonably estimated. Shares in the customer are a distinct good — the investor gets an asset. But the question of whether an investment and a supply contract negotiated together form one arrangement or two is a matter of substance, not of how many documents were signed. It is one of the harder judgements in the whole of revenue recognition, and it is exercised behind closed doors before the reader sees a single figure.

**Does the stake make the counterparty a related party?** Significant influence generally does. Once it exists, transactions between the two must be disclosed as related-party transactions: the amounts, the balances outstanding, and the terms. This is the disclosure that lets an outsider size the loop, and its absence in a filing is as informative as its presence. Note the awkward implication: the treatment that makes an investor's earnings look most conservative — the equity method, which recognises the investee's losses — is also the treatment that triggers the fullest disclosure of what the two of them trade.

**Where does the commitment live?** Mostly not on the balance sheet. A promise to buy two hundred and fifty billion dollars of cloud services over a period of years is a purchase commitment. Until the services are received, it sits in the commitments note, expressed as a total and sometimes as a maturity profile, alongside operating obligations that are far smaller and far better understood. The commitments note is where the true size of these relationships is disclosed, and it is at the back.

**What is the direction of the money?** Where the investor supplies the investee, cash leaves as investment and returns as revenue. The revenue is real: services are delivered, capacity is consumed, and both parties recognise the transaction properly. But growth funded by the supplier's own capital has different quality from growth funded by a customer's independent means, and the difference does not show up anywhere on the face of the income statement. The proportion of an investee's spending that traces back to its own investors is the number to look for, and it is almost never disclosed directly. Building it requires the commitments note, the related-party note, and the reported terms of the rounds. Chapters later in this part do that work for the specific relationships; this chapter is only establishing that the work is necessary.

None of these questions implies wrongdoing, and this book does not allege any. Every leg of every arrangement described here has been disclosed by the parties or reported by named outlets. The point is narrower and more durable: a supply agreement changes what a stake means, it changes what each side is obliged to do, and it changes what the accounts must say. Two investors holding the same percentage of the same laboratory, one with a compute contract and one without, own different things.

## What it obliges each side to do

The last element of a strategic stake is the one least visible in the financial statements: obligation.

Investment commitments are frequently staged. Money is announced as an upper limit — "up to" a figure — and released in tranches against milestones, further rounds, or events. That is why a headline commitment and a cash flow statement rarely agree, and why "committed" and "invested" must never be used interchangeably. Where the terms of a tranche are contingent, the announced figure is a ceiling, not a payment.

Supply commitments run the other way. The investee undertakes to buy, sometimes for years, sometimes exclusively, sometimes with pricing mechanisms nobody outside the parties has seen. Those obligations bind through downturns. A purchase commitment made in a period of scarcity is an asset to the supplier and a liability to the buyer in a period of abundance, and the accounting question of whether such a contract has become onerous is one of the quieter risks in this industry.

Governance obligations bind the investor. Rights the holder does not exercise still exist, and rights it does exercise can change the accounting: cross a line into participation in policy-making and the treatment changes from a passive holding to the equity method, converting an earnings tailwind into an earnings drag with no transaction taking place at all.

So when you encounter the next announcement of a multi-billion-dollar stake, the five things to establish, in order, are how large it is, what rights come with it, what commercial agreement sits alongside it, which of the three accounting regimes it therefore falls into, and what each side is now obliged to do. Only the first of those will be in the headline. The second is usually not public. The third is often in the same press release, further down. The fourth is in the accounting policy note. The fifth is in the commitments note and the related-party note, and it is the one that survives a change of sentiment.

Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot with a date attached, and the marks in particular will have moved: they move whenever a private company raises money, which in this industry has been several times a year. The current numbers are in the most recent quarterly filings of the companies named, in the other income line, the accounting policy note, the fair value note and the commitments note. The method does not move. That is why it is worth learning.

Nothing here is a view on whether any of these holdings is well priced, and nothing in this book is investment advice. The claim is only that a reader who knows which of the three regimes applies is reading a different, and truer, set of accounts from one who does not.
