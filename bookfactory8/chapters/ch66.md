# The Case That It Is Not

On 20 May 2026, Nvidia reported revenue of eighty-one point six billion US dollars for a single quarter — up eighty-five per cent on the same quarter a year earlier, with data centre revenue of seventy-five point two billion, up ninety-two per cent, and a GAAP gross margin of seventy-four point nine per cent, per the company's results announcement of that date and CNBC's coverage of it. In the same quarter the company returned about twenty billion dollars to shareholders in buybacks and dividends, and on 18 May 2026 its board authorised a further eighty billion dollars of repurchases.

Whatever else that is, it is not a company burning investor money to manufacture the appearance of demand. It is a company collecting cash faster than it can find uses for it.

The previous chapter set out the bear case at its strongest: capital expenditure running far ahead of end revenue, financing arrangements that loop back on themselves, depreciation lives that have never been tested against an asset like this, a customer base narrow enough to fit on one slide, and valuations that assume nothing goes wrong. That case is serious and it was stated seriously. This chapter owes the other side the same courtesy.

## The rule for this chapter

The rule is symmetry. Every argument here is made as its most competent proponents make it, not as its critics characterise it. Where a bull argument has a weak joint, the joint is named in the same section rather than saved for a rebuttal. And at the end this chapter does something that will annoy some readers: it declines to say which side is right.

That refusal is not evasion, and the last third of the chapter explains why. Nor is it a way of avoiding work. The work is the test — the specific, observable, publicly reported facts that would move a careful reader one way or the other, and where each of them can be found. A reader who finishes this chapter should be worse at holding a confident opinion and considerably better at knowing what to watch. Nothing here is investment advice; it contains no price target and no prediction.

## First: the revenue is collected, not promised

The bear case and the bull case agree on more than either likes to admit. Both accept that an enormous amount of capital is being committed. They disagree about what is arriving on the other side of it.

Start with the layer where the money lands. Nvidia's fiscal year 2026, which ended on 25 January 2026, produced revenue of two hundred and fifteen point nine billion US dollars, up sixty-five per cent, with data centre revenue of one hundred and ninety-three point seven billion against one hundred and fifteen point two billion the year before, GAAP operating income of one hundred and thirty point four billion and net income of one hundred and twenty point one billion, per the company's February 2026 results announcement and its annual report for that year. The first quarter of the following fiscal year, reported in May 2026, added the eighty-one point six billion dollars this chapter opened with. Guidance for the quarter after that was ninety-one billion dollars, plus or minus two per cent.

Those are audited annual figures and a reported quarter, not a run rate and not a projection. They have been collected. The cash conversion shows in the buyback authorisation.

This matters because of what the comparison cases looked like. The episodes in the previous chapters — the internet infrastructure build of 1999, the telecommunications equipment collapse that followed — were characterised not by an absence of reported revenue but by the *quality* of it. Equipment vendors lent customers the money to buy the equipment, then recognised the sale. Revenue existed on the income statement and the cash never came. The useful question to ask of any boom is not "is there revenue" but "did the cash arrive, and from whom".

The honest qualification: none of this tells you anything about *next* year. A collected dollar proves the buyer paid. It does not prove the buyer will pay again, and it does not prove the buyer earned a return on what it bought. That question belongs one floor up.

## Second: the buyers are incumbents with operating cash flow

The second bull argument is about the identity of the customer. In 1999 the buyers of infrastructure were, in large part, companies that had no revenue and were spending money raised from public markets and vendors. In 2026 the largest buyers of accelerators are four of the most profitable businesses in commercial history, spending money their existing operations generate.

That is a genuine structural difference and deserves full weight. A loss-making startup that stops receiving funding stops buying within a quarter. A company with an established, cash-generative advertising or software business can sustain a capital programme through a downturn in the new thing, because the new thing is not what pays the bills.

The contracted position supports the same reading. Microsoft's commercial remaining performance obligation was reported at six hundred and twenty-five billion US dollars, up eleven per cent, in its results for the quarter ended December 2025, per the earnings call transcript published by The Motley Fool in January 2026; commercial bookings were up twenty-three per cent in constant currency. Google Cloud's chief executive gave a remaining performance obligation figure of one hundred and six billion dollars, with about half expected to convert to revenue within two years, per Constellation Research's 2026 report of his remarks at an investor conference. Remaining performance obligation is contracted revenue not yet delivered. It is a weaker promise than cash but a much stronger one than a forecast, and it is disclosed under an accounting standard rather than asserted in a press release.

### What the bull concedes here

This is the point where the bull case has to be honest, and where a version of it that skips the concession should be distrusted.

Free cash flow across the largest buyers has been deteriorating sharply, and in places has gone negative. Alphabet's free cash flow was reported as turning negative in the second quarter of 2026 for the first time since its 2004 listing, at negative five point nine billion dollars, on quarterly capital expenditure of forty-four point nine billion, per 2026 reporting compilations from io-fund and Investing.com. Amazon's trailing twelve-month free cash flow was reported over the same period as having swung to negative seven point six billion dollars after three years of positive generation. Microsoft was described in 2026 coverage as the only US hyperscaler still generating positive free cash flow. Epoch AI's 2026 analysis put aggregate operating cash flow across the group growing at about twenty-three per cent a year against cash capital expenditure growing at about seventy per cent, with the two lines crossing around the third quarter of 2026 — the point at which aggregate free cash flow reaches zero.

Guidance has also been revised upward repeatedly rather than settling. Compilations early in 2026 put the four largest buyers on course for something approaching seven hundred billion dollars of capital expenditure, with other compilations of the same guidance arriving at about seven hundred and twenty-five billion; later in the year, reporting put Microsoft's guidance at one hundred and ninety-five to two hundred and five billion dollars and Amazon's raised to two hundred and twenty billion. Those are secondary compilations, not filings. The direction is the informative part.

The bull answer to this is not a denial. It is a distinction. Negative free cash flow caused by capital expenditure is a different condition from negative free cash flow caused by an operating loss. The first is a choice made by a board that could stop making it next quarter; the second is a business that does not work. A company with rising operating cash flow and negative free cash flow is spending its own money faster than it makes it, deliberately, on assets it owns. A company with falling operating cash flow is in trouble regardless of what it spends.

The bear answer to *that* is that a deliberate choice can still be a mistake, and that the assets in question depreciate. Both answers are correct. Which one dominates is exactly the thing that cannot be settled from the current record, and the tests section returns to it with a specific line item.

## Third: the capacity is spoken for years ahead

The classic pre-bust signature in semiconductors is not a shortage. It is capacity arriving into a demand curve that has already turned — fabs finished, inventory rising, prices falling, order books thinning. The cycle chapter set out why that pattern repeats: capacity decided today arrives in two or three years, by which point the conditions that justified it may have gone.

As at 2026, the observable position was the opposite of that pattern at every level of the supply chain.

Nvidia's management described visibility into five hundred billion dollars of Blackwell and Rubin revenue spanning the start of calendar 2025 through the end of calendar 2026, with roughly three hundred and fifty billion of that reported as yet to be fulfilled, per 2026 coverage of the company's results commentary. High-bandwidth memory capacity was reported as sold out through 2026 across the major suppliers, in a market estimated at about fifty-four point six billion dollars for the year, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, with SK Hynix holding the largest share, per 2026 industry analysis. SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, reported by CNBC in August 2026. TSMC guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion US dollars, above analyst expectations, in its January 2026 results.

Read those four facts as a set. The foundry is spending more than expected. The memory makers are sold out and building. The accelerator designer has an order book it cannot fill. That is a supply-constrained market, and a supply-constrained market cannot simultaneously be a glut. Whatever is going to go wrong here has not gone wrong yet in the place where these things usually go wrong first.

Two qualifications belong in the same paragraph as the argument, not after it.

The first is that "visibility" is not a defined accounting term. It is management language. It is not a signed obligation in the sense that remaining performance obligation is, and it is not disclosed under a standard that constrains how it is calculated. Chapter fifty-six set out at length what happens to announced numbers in this industry when they meet the filings — an announced hundred billion becoming a settled thirty is not an aberration.

The second is that the sold-out condition is precisely what causes the next glut. Every participant in the chain is now ordering capacity against demand signals that include everyone else's orders. That is how the cycle has always worked. The bull argument is not that the cycle has been repealed; it is that the turn is not visible yet, and that being early to call a turn is indistinguishable in real time from being wrong.

## Fourth: the thing being sold is still getting better and cheaper

Manias generally involve an asset whose usefulness stops improving while its price keeps rising. The bull case argues that the opposite is happening: capability is rising and unit cost is falling, both quickly, both measurably.

On capability, METR's task-completion time horizon measures the length of task, in human-expert time, at which a model succeeds at a given reliability. Its published work reports that metric increasing exponentially over roughly six years with a doubling time of about seven months, and similar rates across nine further benchmarks covering scientific reasoning, mathematics, robotics and computer use. That is a research organisation's measurement, not a vendor's claim, which is what makes it usable.

On cost, the direction is dramatic. Blended prices paid per million tokens were reported falling from eighteen dollars forty to six dollars seven between the first quarter of 2025 and the first quarter of 2026 — roughly sixty-seven per cent — in 2026 trade analysis. Google's chief executive has described the company processing over three point two quadrillion tokens a month, against nine point seven trillion two years earlier, per 2026 reporting of those disclosures. One platform's 2026 data showed, in a single month, volume up fifty-nine per cent, spend up thirty-seven per cent and average price paid per token down thirteen point six per cent.

Hold those three together. Price per unit falling. Volume rising faster than price falls. Total spend still rising. That is the standard signature of a technology becoming genuinely useful — the same shape as bandwidth, storage and transistors before it. It is not the signature of a speculative asset, where price rises and consumption does not.

For the accountant there is a second reading. Falling unit cost is the mechanism by which the model layer's margins repair themselves. Reported figures put compute at about seventy-one cents for every dollar of Anthropic's revenue in the first quarter of 2026, with a projected fifty-six cents in the second, and gross margin tracking around forty-four per cent; OpenAI's gross margin was reported at roughly thirty-nine per cent in the first quarter of 2026, up from about thirty-three per cent a year earlier. Both companies are private, neither figure is audited, and both reached the press through people with reasons to share them. Treat them as indicative. But the *direction* is the bull argument in miniature: a business whose largest cost line is falling as a share of revenue is a business that becomes structurally more profitable without selling a single additional unit.

The qualification the bull must accept: falling price per token is also what a competitive market looks like when nobody has a durable advantage. The same fact that repairs a margin can destroy a moat. Both readings are available from the identical number, which is a recurring feature of this whole debate and the reason the middle of this chapter goes where it goes.

## Fifth: the customer base has barely started

The last bull argument is the one most often asserted and least often evidenced. It is possible to evidence it, and the evidence is more interesting than the assertion.

The cleanest public series is the United States Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey, which asks firms directly whether they use artificial intelligence in producing goods or services. It is a government statistical product, its respondents are not selling anything, and it is published on a regular cadence — three properties that almost nothing else in this field has.

Read that table as a bull would. Four fifths of American businesses do not yet use this at all. Even in the sector that builds it, six in ten do not. Whatever demand exists today comes from a minority of firms, most of them large, most in two or three industries. If the technology is useful at all, the addressable base has hardly been touched.

Read the same table as a bear would. The national rate moved within a three-point band across roughly six months, and a flat series is not obviously one about to inflect. The share expecting to adopt within six months has consistently exceeded the share that subsequently did.

Both readings come from one table. That is not a failure of the data. It is what an early, uneven diffusion looks like while it is happening.

The vendor-sourced evidence points the same way as the bull reading but carries less weight, and should be labelled clearly. A 2026 Nvidia survey of more than three thousand two hundred respondents reported eighty-six per cent saying their AI budget would rise in 2026 and about two per cent expecting a cut. Gartner has projected worldwide AI spending of two point five two trillion dollars in 2026, a forty-four per cent increase; a separate 2026 compilation put it at two point five nine trillion, up forty-seven per cent from one point five trillion in 2025. Those two disagree by seventy billion dollars and three percentage points, which is a useful reminder of what a market-sizing forecast is: a model, built on assumptions, published by an organisation that sells research about the market it is sizing. A supplier's survey of its own prospective customers is evidence of sentiment, not of spend.

The stronger version of this argument needs none of the forecasts. It needs only the Census series and one observation: roughly one business in five uses this at all, and the ones that do are concentrated in the industries best equipped to try new software first.

## What the bull case is actually claiming

Stripped of numbers, the bull case makes four claims and one implicit assumption.

The claims: that the cash is real and collected; that the buyers can fund the programme from operations if they choose to; that supply is behind demand rather than ahead of it; and that the underlying product is improving in capability and falling in cost at rates that have historically preceded broad adoption rather than followed a peak.

The assumption, which is rarely stated and is the whole argument, is this: that end demand — real businesses paying real money for outcomes they value — will grow into the capital being committed *before* the assets bought with that capital have depreciated.

The bear case, stripped the same way, does not dispute the four claims. It disputes the assumption, and specifically the timing inside it.

That is worth sitting with. The two cases in these two chapters are not built on different facts. They are built on the same facts and a different view of one variable: the speed at which end demand arrives relative to the useful life of the asset. Everything else — the circular financing, the concentration, the valuations, the margins — is downstream of that single question.

## Why this book will not adjudicate

Here is the accountant's reason for refusing the verdict, and it is not modesty.

A judgement about whether this is a bubble requires a forecast of end demand over a period longer than any disclosure currently covers. No filing contains it. No standard requires it. Remaining performance obligation disclosures reach some years out, and only for the contracts that exist. Capital expenditure guidance covers a year. Depreciation schedules embed a management estimate of useful life, which is itself the thing in dispute. There is no line item anywhere in the public record from which the answer can be read.

Anyone who tells you they know is doing one of three things: forecasting and calling it analysis, repeating a position they already hold, or selling something.

There is a second reason, less philosophical. The question "is it a bubble" is badly formed, because it collapses two questions with different answers. Is the technology real and durably useful? Is the *price currently paid* for exposure to it justified? The internet was not a delusion in 1999; a great many companies built on it were correctly valued at zero within eighteen months. Both facts held at once. A reader who insists on one answer for both will get at least one of them wrong.

So: no verdict. Instead, the tests.

## The tests

What follows is a set of observable facts, each publicly reported, each with a stated location, each capable of moving a careful reader toward one side or the other. They are grouped by layer, and none requires access to anything private.

### Tests at the silicon layer

**The conversion of the order book.** The five hundred billion dollar visibility figure, with roughly three hundred and fifty billion reported as unfulfilled, is a claim about the future made by management. It converts into reported revenue quarter by quarter, or it does not. Compare quarterly data centre revenue against the trajectory that figure implies. A shortfall shows up as revenue, not as an announcement.

**Inventory and purchase commitments.** In every previous semiconductor turn, inventory turned before revenue did. Watch days of inventory and disclosed supply purchase commitments each quarter. Rising inventory alongside flat revenue is the earliest reliable signal that demand has softened while the supply chain still runs at the old rate.

**Gross margin direction.** A supply-constrained market produces the margins reported through 2026. When margin compresses with no product transition to explain it, pricing power is going — and pricing power is the entire thesis at this layer.

**Announcements versus completions.** Announced fab and memory investments — the thirty-eight billion dollars from SK Hynix in August 2026, the fifty-two to fifty-six billion of TSMC's 2026 guidance — arrive as concrete years later. A deferral is rarely announced; it appears as a changed guidance number.

### Tests at the compute layer

**The depreciation policy note.** This is the single most informative disclosure in the entire debate, and it sits in a note most readers skip. Every hyperscaler states the useful lives it assumes for server and network equipment. Extending those lives raised reported profit across the industry without a dollar of cash changing hands. If lives are now *shortened* — because AI hardware is found to age faster than general-purpose servers — reported profit falls immediately and the bear case gains its most concrete evidence. Read the accounting policy note and the changes-in-estimate disclosure, every year, in every one of them.

**The financing mix.** Capital expenditure funded from operating cash flow is a choice. Capital expenditure funded by debt, leases, or off-balance-sheet vehicles that hold the assets is a different risk. Watch the cash flow statement's financing section and the disclosures on leases and unconsolidated arrangements. A shift from the first to the second is the clearest evidence that the buyers' cash generation is no longer sufficient, which would remove the second bull argument entirely.

**Operating cash flow, separately from free cash flow.** Free cash flow going negative because capital expenditure is rising is survivable. Operating cash flow *falling* is not the same event. Distinguish them every quarter. On the reported 2026 position, the first has happened at more than one of these companies and the second has not.

**The composition of the backlog.** Microsoft's reported six hundred and twenty-five billion dollar commercial remaining performance obligation included forty-five per cent — about two hundred and eighty-one billion — attributed to OpenAI contracts, per the January 2026 earnings call transcript published by The Motley Fool. A backlog is only as good as the counterparties in it. Watch the concentration disclosures, and watch whether the non-concentrated remainder keeps growing at the rate reported.

### Tests at the model layer

**Compute as a share of revenue.** Reported figures moved from about seventy-one cents in the dollar to a projected fifty-six cents across two quarters of 2026 at one of the private labs. If that ratio keeps falling, the layer is becoming a business. If it stalls or reverses, the layer is a pass-through and the value sits below it. This stays unaudited until one of these companies lists — itself an event worth watching.

**Run rate versus recognised revenue.** Annualised run rates are monthly figures multiplied by twelve. When any of these companies publishes audited annual revenue — through a listing, a bond issue, or a regulatory requirement — compare it to the run rates previously reported. The gap, in either direction, will be one of the most informative numbers this industry ever produces.

**Price and volume together.** Falling price per token with faster-rising volume is healthy. Falling price per token with volume that does not compensate is a price war. The 2026 reported position was the first. Watch whether it stays the first.

### Tests at the application layer

**The Census series.** The Business Trends and Outlook Survey adoption rate is the least conflicted public measure of whether businesses actually use this. If it breaks decisively above its 2026 band, the bull argument about early adoption is being confirmed in the one place where nobody is selling anything. If it stays flat for another two years while capital expenditure doubles again, that is the bear case arriving in data rather than in argument.

**The gap between intention and adoption.** The same survey reports the share expecting to adopt within six months. Does the intending share become the using share? A persistent gap is a measure of disappointment, published quarterly and free.

**Pricing.** The consumer subscription price has been stubbornly conventional. If prices rise and customers stay, the layer has pricing power it has not yet demonstrated. If prices fall or bundling accelerates, the value is moving elsewhere.

### One test that sits outside the accounts

**Power.** Capacity that cannot be energised is not capacity. Grid interconnection queues, regional power prices and utility filings show whether the announced build is physically deliverable on the announced schedule. This constraint binds regardless of who is right about demand, and regulators report it rather than participants.

## How to keep score

Three habits separate watching these tests from merely reading about them.

Date every figure and name its source, including in your own notes. A number without a date is not evidence, and this industry produces numbers that are stale within a quarter. Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot and several will have moved by the time it is read.

Distinguish the three states of a commitment: announced, contracted, delivered. Announcements are marketing documents. Contracted obligations appear in remaining performance obligation disclosures and are constrained by an accounting standard. Delivered revenue has been collected or is owed. Most confusion in this subject is a category error between those three.

And watch the direction of revisions rather than the level of the number. A guidance figure revised upward three times running tells you more than the figure itself. So does one quietly revised down. The level is a forecast; the revision is behaviour.

## The verdict this chapter does not give

Two chapters, the same facts, opposite conclusions, both argued by serious people who have read the filings.

That should not be comfortable, and it is not meant to be. It is an accurate description of where the public record stands as at 2026. The bull case is not credulity and the bear case is not cynicism. They differ on one variable — how fast real end demand arrives relative to how fast the assets wear out — and that variable is disclosed nowhere, by anyone, because it has not happened yet.

What can be done is to know precisely where the answer will first become visible: in a depreciation policy note, in a days-of-inventory figure, in the financing section of a cash flow statement, in a government survey of whether businesses actually use the thing, and in the gap between what was announced and what was eventually contracted. Those are the places. They are all public, and most of them are free.

The reader who watches them will know before the people who are certain.
