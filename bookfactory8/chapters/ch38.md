# Anthropic II: Two Clouds, Two Investors

In the space of four days in April 2026, two of the largest companies on earth — direct competitors in the cloud business — each committed tens of billions of dollars to the same private company. On 20 April, CNBC reported that Amazon would invest up to a further twenty-five billion dollars in Anthropic as part of an infrastructure agreement, taking its total commitment to as much as thirty-three billion. On 24 April, CNBC reported that Google would invest up to forty billion dollars in the same company — ten billion immediately, with up to thirty billion more contingent on performance milestones — taking Google's total commitment, including its earlier rounds, to as much as forty-three billion per 2026 reporting.

Stop and consider what that sentence contains. Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud sell against each other every day of the week. Each has its own frontier model business to feed: Amazon through its stakes and partnerships, Google through Gemini, which competes with Anthropic's Claude more directly than almost any product on the market. Yet both are major investors in the same model company, both supply it with chips of their own design, and neither controls it.

There is no precedent for this in the industries an accountant is trained on. Two rival oil majors do not jointly fund a refinery that undercuts them both. Two rival banks do not co-own a fintech that competes with each of their retail arms. To understand why this structure exists, you have to take it apart the way it was assembled: deal by deal, clause by clause, with a date and a source on every number.

## Two ledgers, one company

The relationship built up in layers, and the order matters, because each round was negotiated against the backdrop of the one before.

Google moved first. Its initial investment — roughly three hundred million dollars for a stake of about ten per cent — dates to early 2023, per CNBC's later accounts of the relationship. Amazon arrived in September 2023 with an announcement that it would invest up to four billion dollars, beginning with an initial one point two five billion, as reported by CNBC and TechCrunch at the time. Google answered within weeks: in October 2023 it committed up to a further two billion dollars, five hundred million up front and one point five billion over time, per CNBC in October 2023.

Amazon completed its first four billion in March 2024 with a second tranche of two point seven five billion — described by CNBC at the time as the largest outside venture investment in Amazon's history. In November 2024 it added another four billion, taking its total to eight billion, and Amazon Web Services was named Anthropic's primary cloud and training partner, with Anthropic committing to train and deploy on Amazon's own Trainium and Inferentia chips, per CNBC in November 2024. The same reporting noted two things worth underlining: Amazon would remain a minority investor, and the money bought no seat on Anthropic's board.

Then the deals stopped being mainly about money and started being about machines. In October 2025, Google and Anthropic announced a cloud agreement reported by CNBC as worth tens of billions of dollars, under which Anthropic would gain access to up to one million of Google's tensor processing units and more than a gigawatt of capacity coming online in 2026. In the same month, Amazon brought Project Rainier online: a data centre campus in Indiana reported by CNBC at eleven billion dollars of investment, running roughly half a million of Amazon's Trainium2 chips — reported by Data Center Dynamics as nearly five hundred thousand — dedicated to training and serving Claude.

And then came April 2026, and the two commitments this chapter opened with.

## What "up to" is doing in every sentence

Read the table again and notice the phrase that recurs: *up to*. Up to four billion. Up to two billion. Up to twenty-five billion. Up to forty billion. An accountant treats those two words as load-bearing, because they mark the difference between a cheque and a promise.

A commitment of "up to" a sum is typically structured in tranches, with the later tranches conditional. Sometimes the condition is simply time and the investor's option, as in Google's October 2023 commitment of five hundred million up front and one and a half billion over time, per CNBC. Sometimes the condition is explicit performance: CNBC's April 2026 report on Google's forty billion described ten billion invested now and up to thirty billion contingent on milestones. The headline number is the ceiling of the escalator, not the floor.

The instruments matter as much as the amounts. Court filings in the United States government's search case against Google, reported by The New York Times in 2025, revealed that Google's position in Anthropic had been built partly through convertible debt — with a further seven hundred and fifty million dollars of convertible notes then scheduled — and that Google's total funding exceeded three billion dollars at that point, more than had previously been public. Convertible notes are a lawyer's answer to an awkward question: how does a strategic investor put money in without immediately taking the ownership percentage, voting weight and regulatory attention that straight equity brings? The note sits on the books as debt until it converts; the influence it could represent stays latent.

So when this book says Amazon has committed up to thirty-three billion dollars and Google up to forty-three billion, per the 2026 reporting cited above, understand those figures for what they are: the maximum extent of two negotiated programmes of tranches, notes, and conditional milestones, stretched over years, and revisable. They are not two wire transfers. No audited account yet exists in public that shows how much has actually landed, because Anthropic is private — the subject of this book's fifth chapter — and its investors disclose only what they choose or must.

### An illustrative anatomy of a tranche

To see why the drafting matters, take an illustrative deal, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. A cloud provider announces it will invest "up to ten billion dollars" in a model lab. The papers behind the headline say: two billion now, as convertible notes; three billion in eighteen months at the investor's option; five billion more if the lab reaches agreed revenue and deployment milestones, some of which involve using the investor's own chips. Alongside the investment agreement sits a separate commercial contract under which the lab commits to spend, say, six billion dollars on the investor's cloud over four years.

Now watch how differently the two parties' books tell the story. On day one the investor's balance sheet shows a two billion dollar note — an asset — and its press release says ten. The lab's balance sheet shows two billion of cash and a matching liability, because a convertible note is debt until it converts. Nothing about the remaining eight billion appears on either balance sheet, because none of it has happened; at most it surfaces in the notes to the accounts as a commitment or a contingency. Meanwhile, as the lab spends its committed six billion on the investor's cloud, that spending books as the investor's *revenue*, quarter by quarter, growing the very cloud segment that analysts scrutinise most closely. If the notes later convert at a higher valuation, the investor records a gain — potentially a large one — that arrives through the income statement without a single new customer being signed.

Every element of that illustration is a normal, disclosed, legitimate instrument. The point of assembling them in one place is to show what the assembly does: a headline of ten billion can coexist with two billion of actual cash moved, revenue for the investor that originated as the investor's own capital, and a paper gain contingent on a private valuation nobody can independently verify. When you read that a hyperscaler has committed "up to" some enormous sum to a model company, this machinery — tranches, options, notes, spending commitments — is what the phrase compresses. The real deals cited in this chapter each disclose some of these elements and not others; no public account yet joins them all up, which is itself the most important thing to know.

## How a deal like this gets negotiated

No transcript of these negotiations is public, and this chapter will not pretend to one. But the structure that emerged tells you a great deal about the bargaining positions, because deal terms are fossils of leverage. Consider what each side held.

Anthropic held two scarce things. The first was a frontier model business — one of only a handful in existence, and by May 2026 the basis of a sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, as reported by CNBC in May 2026. The second, less obvious, was its usefulness as a customer. A hyperscaler that has spent years and billions designing its own AI accelerator has a problem money alone cannot solve: nobody serious is betting their model on it. Amazon's Trainium and Google's TPU both needed a frontier lab willing to train flagship models on their silicon at scale, publicly, because that is the only demonstration that persuades every other customer. Anthropic could confer that. Roughly half a million Trainium2 chips at Project Rainier, per the October 2025 reporting above, and a commitment of up to a million TPUs, per CNBC in October 2025, are exactly that demonstration, purchased at exactly that scale.

The hyperscalers held the other scarce things: capital in quantities venture funds cannot match, and — more binding still — compute itself. In 2026 the constraint on a model company was not ideas but megawatts and accelerators, and the two companies at the table controlled both. A cheque from a financial investor buys the right to queue for capacity; a cheque from a hyperscaler *is* the capacity.

Now put those positions opposite each other and the shape of the deal falls out almost mechanically. Each hyperscaler wanted the anchor tenancy for its chips, priority in Anthropic's compute spending, and equity exposure to the upside. Anthropic wanted maximum compute, minimum control ceded, and — critically — no exclusivity, because a supplier who is also your sole source is a supplier who owns you at renewal. The presence of two bidders was not an accident of history; it was Anthropic's single most valuable negotiating asset. A company negotiating with one hyperscaler is negotiating its own acquisition in slow motion. A company negotiating with two can sell each of them the thing they want — validation, tenancy, upside — while playing the terms of each against the other. The multi-cloud outcome, with training and serving spread across Trainium, TPUs and Nvidia hardware as described in the October 2025 coverage of the Google deal, is what that looks like when the papers are signed.

The sequence in the table above shows the auction running in real time. Amazon's November 2024 round bought it the title of "primary cloud and training partner", per CNBC in November 2024 — language that sounds like exclusivity and is not. Eleven months later, Anthropic signed the tens-of-billions TPU agreement with Google, per CNBC in October 2025, and the reporting around that deal described a deliberate three-way split of workloads across Google's TPUs, Amazon's Trainium and Nvidia's hardware. Then, in April 2026, both hyperscalers escalated within the same week, per CNBC's reports of 20 and 24 April 2026. Each announcement reset the other side's arithmetic. A negotiator on either cloud's side of the table knew that any capacity or capital it declined to offer would be offered by the rival within months, and priced accordingly. Whether the timing of the April announcements reflected direct competitive response is not publicly documented, and this book will not guess; the pattern, though, is on the record and speaks for itself.

There is one more party at every such table: the other investors and employees, whose stakes are diluted by each round. For them, the hyperscaler money carried a price worth watching — capital that arrives entangled with commercial obligations is worth less per dollar than clean capital, because some of it is, in substance, a prepayment for cloud services flowing back to the investor. Which brings us to governance, because that entanglement is precisely what the company's protections were built to contain.

## The protections the company kept

The clearest public window into those protections opened in 2025, in litigation neither company sought. As part of the United States government's antitrust case over Google's search business, a filing revealed — per reporting by The New York Times in 2025 — that Google held about fourteen per cent of Anthropic, was capped at fifteen per cent, and held no voting rights, no board seat, and no board observer rights. Reporting of Amazon's November 2024 investment, by CNBC and GeekWire, had already recorded the same essentials on the other side: a minority position and no seat on Anthropic's board. Between them, the two largest investors in one of the world's most valuable private companies had bought, in governance terms, almost nothing.

Each element of that list is a specific, negotiated protection, and each is worth naming, because these are the clauses any company in this position seeks.

An **ownership cap** — Google's fifteen per cent, per the 2025 reporting — prevents an investor from creeping toward control through follow-on rounds and note conversions. It also does quiet regulatory work: many merger-control regimes are triggered by thresholds of ownership or influence, and a hard cap is designed to stay below them.

**Non-voting stock** separates economic exposure from decision rights. The investor eats with the shareholders but does not vote with them. For a company whose two biggest backers compete with it and with each other, this is not a nicety; it is the mechanism that makes the whole triangle possible.

**No board seat and no observer** protects something subtler than votes: information. A director — even a silent observer — sees the board pack. The board pack of a model company contains its training roadmap, its compute negotiations, and its customer pipeline, which is to say, a précis of everything a rival cloud would like to know. Excluding both investors from the room is what allows Anthropic to negotiate with Google while Amazon money sits on its balance sheet, and vice versa.

Above all of these sits a structure that predates both investments. Anthropic is a Delaware public benefit corporation — the Federal Trade Commission's January 2024 orders were addressed to "Anthropic PBC" — and its founding documents create a Long-Term Benefit Trust, an independent body whose members hold no equity in the company and whose function is to appoint directors. By mid-2026, following the Trust's appointment of Novartis chief executive Vas Narasimhan to the board, directors chosen by the Trust held a majority of Anthropic's seven-member board, with the remaining seats elected by stockholders, per the company's governance announcements and 2026 reporting. Whatever one thinks of the arrangement — and it will be tested properly only in a crisis — its accounting consequence is plain: the majority of the board answers to a body that owns nothing and that neither Amazon nor Google appointed. Control of this company was never on the shelf. An investor could buy exposure to Anthropic; no investor could buy Anthropic's steering wheel.

## The regulators read the same documents

Structures like this do not go unexamined, and the examinations are themselves a source — some of the best public information about these deals exists only because officials demanded it.

In January 2024 the Federal Trade Commission issued compulsory orders under section 6(b) of the FTC Act to Alphabet, Amazon, Anthropic, Microsoft and OpenAI, seeking information on the partnerships between the largest cloud providers and the leading model developers. Its staff report, published in January 2025, described the equity and revenue-sharing rights the cloud providers had taken, and the consultation, control and exclusivity rights that came with some of the agreements. In the United Kingdom, the Competition and Markets Authority examined both partnerships as potential mergers. In September 2024 it decided that the Amazon–Anthropic partnership did not qualify for investigation under the merger provisions of the Enterprise Act 2002; its review of the Google–Anthropic partnership, concluded in 2024, found that Google had not acquired the ability materially to influence Anthropic's commercial policy, and so the deal fell outside UK merger control altogether. And in the remedies phase of the US search case, the Department of Justice at one stage proposed that Google divest its stakes in generative AI companies, including Anthropic, as reported by outlets covering the case in 2025 — the very proceedings that put Google's fourteen per cent on the public record.

Notice the symmetry. The clauses that protected Anthropic from its investors — the cap, the non-voting stock, the empty board seats — are the same clauses that carried the deals past the merger regulators. "No material influence" is simultaneously a governance promise to the company and a jurisdictional answer to the CMA. It would be naive to think the lawyers drafting these terms were unaware of that double duty. It would be equally wrong to present the clearances as vindication for all time: the FTC's report catalogued the ways such partnerships can concentrate access to compute and talent, and both regimes reserved the right to look again. This book takes no view on where that ends. It notes only that, as at the decisions cited, the structure held.

## Why a minority position was worth it

Which leaves the question the whole chapter has been circling. Amazon and Google are not charities, and neither is in the habit of writing eleven-figure cheques for stakes it cannot vote. Why did two companies that habitually control what they buy accept so little here?

Because control was the wrong prize, and both of them knew it. Follow what each investor actually receives, and the minority position stops looking like a concession and starts looking like the point.

First, the money comes home. Anthropic's dominant cost is compute — figures shared with investors and reported by PitchBook and Morningstar in 2026 put its compute costs at about seventy-one cents of every revenue dollar in the first quarter of 2026 — and its compute is bought, under the agreements above, substantially from its own investors. A dollar invested by Amazon that returns as AWS revenue, or by Google that returns as Google Cloud revenue, is capital expenditure financing wearing equity's clothes. This is the mechanism to understand rather than a misconduct to allege: nothing in the public record suggests these flows are improperly accounted for, and each was disclosed. But a reader of either hyperscaler's accounts should hold the two ends of the loop in mind at once — the investment on one page, the customer's spending on another — and ask how much of the cloud growth is, economically, the investors' own capital making a round trip. The FTC's January 2025 report flagged revenue-sharing and spending commitments as a defining feature of these partnerships for exactly this reason.

Second, the chips get their proof. Trainium without Anthropic is an in-house science project; Trainium with Project Rainier's roughly half a million chips training Claude, per the October 2025 reporting, is a merchant product with the most demanding reference customer on earth. The same holds for Google's TPUs and the million-unit commitment reported in October 2025. Neither validation was purchasable at any price from a lab the buyer controlled — the demonstration only works because Anthropic was free to choose otherwise, and conspicuously keeps both suppliers, plus Nvidia, in the building.

Third, the equity itself has been extraordinary on paper. A stake acquired when Anthropic was valued in the tens of billions — CNBC reported a sixty-one point five billion dollar valuation as recently as March 2025 — was marked against a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation fourteen months later, per CNBC in May 2026. Those gains are unrealised, private-market, and preference-laden, as the next chapter takes apart; but they flow through the investors' own reported results, and by August 2026 CNBC was reporting that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them. An investor whose profit and loss account now moves with a private company's funding rounds has acquired exposure enough, votes or no votes.

And fourth — least measurable, most strategic — each investor bought a hedge against its own model bet and against the other's cloud. Google wins if Gemini prevails; it also wins, through fourteen per cent and a TPU tenancy, if Claude does. Amazon, with no frontier model of comparable standing of its own at the relevant dates, bought its position in the model layer outright. Both accepted minority terms because the alternative was not a controlling stake — that was never for sale, and would have brought the merger regulators in through the front door — but no position at all, while the rival took everything.

That is the resolution of the strangeness. Two competitors can co-invest in the same company precisely because neither is buying the company. Each is buying a customer for its silicon, a tenant for its data centres, an option on the model layer, and a line in its accounts that appreciates when the tide rises. The corporation in the middle, holding the models and the steering wheel, negotiated the one structure under which being fought over is safer than being owned.

All of it, as ever, is a photograph. The commitments cited here — thirty-three billion, forty-three billion, a million TPUs — are the announced ceilings of April 2026 and will have been drawn down, renegotiated or extended by the time you read this; the ownership figures date to filings reported in 2025. Check the current numbers against the investors' own disclosures and the financial press before repeating any of them. And none of this, here or anywhere in this book, is a recommendation to buy or sell anything. The next chapter turns to the number that hangs over the whole arrangement: nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollars, and what a private valuation of that size does and does not mean.
