# The Incumbent Software Companies

On 27 May 2026 Salesforce reported that annual recurring revenue from Agentforce, its agent product, had reached one point two billion US dollars, up two hundred and five per cent on the year, on quarterly revenue of eleven point one billion dollars, up thirteen per cent, with a non-GAAP operating margin of thirty-four point eight per cent and free cash flow of six point six billion dollars — all per the company's first-quarter fiscal 2027 results announcement. The shares were trading near a fifty-two-week low. Coverage in mid-2026 by the Motley Fool and Yahoo Finance put the stock down roughly thirty-seven per cent for the calendar year.

That gap is the subject of this chapter. A company is growing its newest product faster than almost anything in the history of enterprise software, and the market is valuing the business it sits inside as though it were winding down.

Both positions can be held honestly. The accounts describe what has already happened. The share price is a claim about how long it will keep happening. Nowhere else in this book do those two things disagree so publicly, and nowhere else does an accountant's habit of separating them earn its keep so quickly.

## The charge sheet against enterprise software

The bear case on the incumbent software companies is not vague unease. It has three distinct parts, and each attacks a different line of the income statement. It is worth stating them at their strongest before answering any of them.

The first is the seat argument. Most of these companies bill per user per month. Revenue is therefore seats multiplied by price multiplied by renewal rate. If artificial intelligence removes work, it removes workers, and the seat count that a customer renews next year is smaller than the one it renewed this year. This is not speculative. Salesforce's own chief executive said in September 2025 that the company had reduced its customer support headcount from about nine thousand people to about five thousand, that roughly half of customer interactions were then being handled by agents rather than humans, and that support costs had fallen about seventeen per cent, in comments reported by Fortune and Fox Business. He described it as a rebalance rather than a redundancy programme, with staff moved into sales. The distinction matters for the people involved. It does not change the arithmetic for a vendor that charges per seat: the world's most prominent seller of seat-based software had publicly demonstrated the mechanism by which seat-based software shrinks.

The second is the build argument. These businesses sell software. Software is now, in part, generated. A capable engineering team with a model can produce in a fortnight an internal tool that would once have required a licence, an implementation partner and a two-year contract. If the cost of building falls far enough, the buy side of buy-versus-build loses cases it used to win automatically.

The third is the feature argument. A great deal of what enterprise software charged for was, stripped of marketing, the arrangement of information on a screen and the routing of a task between two people. A general model does some of that natively. The specific feature — summarise this ticket, draft this email, extract these fields — is no longer a differentiator, because everybody has it, including the model providers who sell it directly.

Put together, the charge is that the incumbents face falling volume, a cheaper substitute, and the commoditisation of their differentiators, simultaneously. That is a serious case. It is also, so far, not the case the financial statements describe.

Nothing in that table looks like an industry being dismantled. Growth is double digit at every one of them. The AI-specific lines, where disclosed, are growing at rates between forty per cent a quarter and two hundred per cent a year. ServiceNow raised its full-year subscription revenue guidance to between fifteen point seven six and fifteen point seven eight billion dollars in its July 2026 results, and reported one hundred and twenty-three transactions of more than a million dollars in net new annual contract value in the quarter, up nearly forty per cent on the year, with six hundred and fifty-eight customers above five million dollars of annual contract value.

Now put the market's view beside it. As at August 2026, companiesmarketcap put SAP at roughly two hundred and forty-five billion US dollars, ServiceNow at roughly one hundred and twenty-one billion, and Adobe at roughly one hundred and one billion. Salesforce was reported at roughly one hundred and sixty-nine billion dollars as at 20 August 2026 by stockanalysis, with other trackers reading nearer one hundred and eighty-four billion in the same month; the divergence between trackers on a single company in a single month is itself a useful reminder of how loosely this number should be held. All four figures move daily and can be looked up in seconds.

Adobe is the extreme case. In 2026 coverage its shares were reported down roughly thirty-seven per cent for the year, on a business that had just posted record quarterly revenue and raised full-year guidance to between twenty-six point five and twenty-six point six billion dollars. Analyst commentary compiled by Investing.com in 2026 put the shares at a low double-digit multiple of forward earnings against a software sector median more than twice that. The company announced a twenty-five billion dollar share repurchase authorisation running to April 2030, and its chief executive of eighteen years said on 12 March 2026 that he would step down once a successor was named, as reported by CNBC; the chief financial officer resigned effective 15 June 2026.

The disagreement is not about this year's numbers. Everybody agrees on those; they are filed. The disagreement is about duration — how many more years the revenue lasts, and at what margin. Valuation is arithmetic performed on a guess about duration. When a multiple halves while revenue grows, no one has decided the company is worth less today. They have decided the tail is shorter.

That distinction has a practical use. It tells you which disclosures matter. If the argument is about duration, then this quarter's revenue is nearly irrelevant and the contracted figures are nearly everything.

## Read the backlog, not the headline

The most useful numbers these companies publish are the ones almost nobody quotes: remaining performance obligation, current remaining performance obligation, and cloud backlog. They are the contracted revenue a customer has already committed to and the vendor has not yet recognised. They come straight out of the revenue recognition standards, they are audited, and they are the closest thing enterprise software has to a forward order book.

Salesforce reported total remaining performance obligation of seventy-two billion dollars at its fiscal year end in January 2026, up fourteen per cent, and current remaining performance obligation — the portion due within twelve months — of thirty-three point six billion dollars in its May 2026 results. SAP reported total cloud backlog of seventy-seven point two nine billion euros at 31 December 2025, up twenty-two per cent as reported and thirty per cent at constant currencies, and current cloud backlog of twenty-two point nine billion euros in the second quarter of 2026, up twenty-seven per cent.

A backlog growing faster than revenue means customers are committing further out, not less far out. A backlog growing more slowly than revenue is the first hard evidence that duration is compressing, and it will appear there before it appears anywhere else — before revenue, well before earnings, and years before anyone writes an article about it. When SAP's shares fell on its January 2026 results despite record cloud growth, the reporting attributed it to backlog concerns rather than to the revenue line. The market was reading the right number.

This is the single most transferable habit in the chapter. When you want to know whether the software incumbents are being disrupted, do not read the growth rate. Read the order book, and read it net of currency and net of acquisitions.

## Embedding the feature is necessary and insufficient

The first response every incumbent made was to put the model inside the existing product. It is the cheapest response and the least defensible, because everyone can do it and everyone did.

The economics are worth setting out properly, because this is where the accountant's objection lands. Enterprise software has been an unusually good business for one structural reason: the marginal cost of serving one more customer was close to zero. Gross margins in the seventies and eighties per cent were not a sign of gouging; they were the natural consequence of a product that cost almost nothing to reproduce. Every incremental dollar of revenue dropped nearly intact to gross profit, which is why these companies could spend forty per cent of revenue on sales and marketing and still compound.

Inference is not free. Every summarised ticket, every generated image, every agent that reasons across a customer record consumes compute that somebody bills for. For the first time in the industry's history, the marginal cost of an enterprise software feature is a real, variable, metered number that scales with usage.

Take an illustrative vendor, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It sells a seat for one hundred dollars a month at an eighty per cent gross margin, so twenty dollars of cost and eighty dollars of gross profit. It now embeds an assistant. Suppose the average user triggers four hundred model interactions a month at two cents each: eight dollars of new cost of revenue per seat, per month. The seat price has not changed. Gross margin falls from eighty per cent to seventy-two per cent, and the whole of that decline flows through to operating profit unless something else gives. If usage doubles because the assistant turns out to be popular, margin falls to sixty-four per cent, and the vendor is in the peculiar position of being punished by its own product's success. That is the structural problem in one paragraph, and it is why the pricing question below is not a marketing question.

There is a disclosure point attached, and it deserves stating plainly rather than darkly. None of the incumbents discussed here separately discloses the gross margin earned on its AI products. They disclose AI revenue, or AI-influenced revenue, or AI annual contract value. They do not disclose the cost of goods sold that sits underneath it. That is entirely permissible — segment disclosure follows how management runs the business, and there is no requirement to carve out a product line that is not a reportable segment. But a reader should notice the asymmetry. The revenue from artificial intelligence is a headline. The cost of artificial intelligence is inside a consolidated cost of revenue line, mixed with hosting, support and amortised software. Until it is separated, nobody outside these companies can say what the new products earn.

The related caution concerns the metrics themselves. Adobe's disclosure of AI-influenced annual recurring revenue above five billion dollars in fiscal 2025 sits beside AI-first annual recurring revenue that exceeded five hundred million dollars in the second quarter of fiscal 2026, having more than tripled. Those are two different things. The second is revenue from customers paying specifically for AI features. The first is revenue from customers whose subscription includes AI features, whether or not the AI is why they subscribed. Adobe defines both terms and reports them consistently, which is more than many do. They are still company-defined, non-GAAP measures with no auditor's opinion attached to the definition, and "influenced" is doing a great deal of work. Read them as the company's argument about its own progress, presented in good faith, rather than as a figure comparable to revenue.

## Repricing: the hardest decision on the list

If the seat is the exposed unit, the obvious answer is to stop selling seats. That is easier to say than to do, and Salesforce's experience shows why.

Agentforce launched on a conversation-based model at two dollars per conversation — simple, predictable, and immediately contentious, because in an agentic system nobody could agree what constituted one conversation. Salesforce announced Flex Credits in May 2025, a consumption model in which actions draw down a credit balance, with trade guides in 2026 putting a typical action at twenty credits, or about ten cents. Customers gained granularity and lost predictability. The vendor gained revenue that scales with value delivered and lost the thing that made software a wonderful business to model: a subscription that renews whether or not anyone uses it.

The industry is moving this way in aggregate, though more slowly than the commentary suggests. Benchmarkit's 2026 data, as reported in trade coverage that year, put median net revenue retention at ninety-five per cent for seat-based pricing models against one hundred and eight per cent for usage-based ones — a gap that already exists before a single seat is displaced by an agent. Gartner forecasts cited in 2026 coverage have at least forty per cent of enterprise software spending shifting to usage, agent or outcome-based models by 2030, with seat-based revenue share falling from twenty-one per cent to fifteen per cent. Those are forecasts by a research firm, not measurements, and should be weighted accordingly; the direction is better evidenced than the magnitude.

Meanwhile the largest seat-based AI product in the market stayed a seat. Microsoft 365 Copilot has been sold as a thirty dollar per user per month add-on to a qualifying base licence, per vendor pricing compiled in 2026 licensing guides, with a bundled Business Premium plan at thirty-two dollars per user per month introduced from 1 July 2026 and a small-business tier at a promotional eighteen dollars per user per month, standard price twenty-one, running to the end of 2026. The company with the most to lose from seat erosion priced its flagship AI product per seat, and priced it at a level that assumes the seat survives.

Net revenue retention deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the metric where all of this shows up first. It measures what last year's cohort of customers spends this year, including expansion, contraction and churn. Above one hundred per cent, the existing base grows on its own. Below one hundred, every new customer signed is partly replacing revenue lost from customers already won. It is the number an incumbent cannot dress up, because it is arithmetic on a fixed cohort. If the seat argument is right, net revenue retention falls first, backlog growth slows second, revenue growth slows third, and margin goes last. Anyone watching that sequence will know years before the headlines.

The three repricing models are worth laying out side by side, because each fails differently.

| Model | What the vendor earns | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Per seat | Predictable, contracted, renewable revenue independent of usage | Falls with headcount; hard to justify when the software does the work |
| Per unit of consumption | Revenue that scales with value delivered and with inference cost | Unpredictable; harder to contract; customers optimise usage down |
| Per outcome | Best alignment with what the buyer actually wants | Requires agreeing what an outcome is and who caused it; disputes are expensive |

Outcome pricing is the one everybody praises and few implement, because attribution is genuinely hard. If a support agent resolves a ticket, was the outcome the vendor's, or the knowledge base the customer spent four years writing? Both parties have a commercial view, and no accounting standard settles it.

## Buying capability, and what it does to the P&L

The third response is acquisition, and this is where the strategy becomes visible in the accounts rather than in the marketing.

Salesforce completed its eight billion dollar purchase of Informatica in November 2025, having announced it in May 2025, per CNBC's reporting at announcement and trade coverage at completion. By the first quarter of fiscal 2027 the company was reporting Agentforce and Data 360 annual recurring revenue of nearly three point four billion dollars, of which one point one billion was Informatica Cloud ARR — that is, roughly a third of the combined AI and data figure arrived by purchase rather than by growth. Salesforce disclosed the split, which is to its credit; a reader who ignores the split will overstate the organic momentum by a wide margin.

ServiceNow acquired Moveworks in a transaction valued at two point eight five billion dollars in cash and stock, announced in March 2025 and its largest deal to that point, per Constellation Research. SAP acquired Dremio and Prior Labs, and in July 2026 cut its full-year non-IFRS operating profit outlook to between eleven point eight and twelve point two billion euros, citing dilution of more than one hundred million euros from those completed acquisitions.

That last item is the most instructive sentence in this section. It is a rare, dated, company-confirmed instance of an AI capability purchase visibly reducing a headline profit number. Acquisitions of loss-making capability businesses always do this: the acquirer consolidates the target's costs immediately, the target's revenue is small, and the purchase price allocation creates intangible assets that are amortised against profit for years afterwards. Goodwill, the residue that cannot be allocated to identifiable assets, sits on the balance sheet and is tested annually for impairment rather than amortised, which means it does nothing at all to reported profit until the day it does a great deal.

So when an incumbent buys capability, three things happen to the accounts and only one of them is discussed. Revenue rises, and everyone mentions it. Operating profit falls, and the guidance gets revised. And a large intangible balance is created whose eventual write-down, if the capability disappoints, will arrive years later as a non-cash charge that management will invite you to look past. The reader's job is to note the goodwill added at the time of the deal, and to remember it.

Adobe took the opposite decision with its capital, authorising twenty-five billion dollars of share repurchases through April 2030 rather than acquiring its way into new capability at scale. Buying back stock the board considers cheap is a defensible use of cash and a clear statement of management's view. It is also, unavoidably, capital not spent on the product. This book takes no position on which choice is better and does not offer a view on any of these shares. The observation is only that the two strategies are visible in the cash flow statement, and that a reader can see which one each company chose without listening to a single earnings call.

## The moat, examined honestly

Which brings us to the defence the incumbents actually make: that the moat was never the features.

The argument goes like this. A model can generate software. It cannot generate the twelve years of transactions in your general ledger, the four hundred custom fields your business added to the standard object model, the integrations to seven other systems, the permission structure that determines who may approve what, the audit trail your regulator expects to see, or the accumulated institutional agreement about what a "qualified opportunity" means in your company. The software was never the asset. The system of record was.

There is real substance to this. In accounting terms the moat is a switching cost, and switching costs are the classic asset that never appears on a balance sheet: they were incurred by the customer, not the vendor, and they benefit the vendor entirely. A finance director contemplating a migration off an ERP system is not evaluating features. They are pricing a two-year programme, a data migration with regulatory exposure, a retraining exercise, and a period during which the monthly close might not work. That calculation has defeated better products for thirty years and it does not become easier because a model can write code.

The evidence that the incumbents are converting this into revenue is reasonably good. SAP reported that AI and its Business Data Cloud featured in more than ninety per cent of its fifty largest deals in the second quarter of 2026. ServiceNow's AI annual contract value crossed one billion dollars in the same quarter, up more than forty per cent quarter on quarter, with agentic production deployments reported as growing ninefold over nine months. Workday was reported in 2026 trade coverage of its results as generating close to five hundred million dollars from agentic products across around four thousand customers, with new annual contract value from them up more than two hundred per cent; that figure comes from analyst and trade write-ups rather than a line in the accounts, and should be held more loosely than the audited totals of nine point five five two billion dollars of fiscal 2026 revenue and eight point eight three three billion of subscription revenue that the company itself reported.

Salesforce's purchase of Informatica is the moat argument expressed as a cheque. Informatica sells data cataloguing, metadata management and master data management — the machinery for knowing what your data is, where it came from and whether it can be trusted. Eight billion dollars is a considerable sum to pay for plumbing. It is a rational sum to pay if you believe the agent era will be won by whoever can ground a model in a customer's own governed data, and that features are a commodity.

Now the honest counterweights, because a moat argument that survives no cross-examination is worthless.

The first is that switching costs decay in exactly the conditions AI creates. Much of the cost of migration was the labour of understanding an old system and rewriting the integrations. That labour is precisely what code generation is best at reducing. The moat is not made of stone; it is made of person-hours, and the price of a person-hour is falling.

The second is that the moat is uneven. It is strongest where the record is regulated and shared — the ledger, the payroll, the patient record, the audit trail — because there the customer's obligation to keep an authoritative history is a legal one, and the vendor's product is the history. It is weakest where the product is a tool that produces a file. Nobody's regulator requires them to keep their images in a particular application.

Which is why Adobe is the sharpest test in this chapter and not merely the worst-performing share. Adobe's customers own their files. The switching cost of a creative professional is skill and habit, both real, neither contractual. Competitors including Figma and Canva have grown on collaboration and simplicity, per 2026 coverage of Adobe's competitive position, and generative image and video tools have reduced the amount of skilled manipulation a given output requires. Set against that, Adobe reported record revenue of six point six two billion dollars in the quarter ended June 2026, up thirteen per cent, said that Firefly users had generated more than twenty-two billion assets since the model's launch in March 2023, and reported that eighty-five per cent of Firefly use occurred inside Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere Pro and Express rather than in a standalone tool. That last statistic is the entire incumbent strategy in a single measure: the generative capability is being consumed inside the products people already have open. Whether that constitutes a moat or a delaying action is the question the share price and the income statement are currently answering differently, and this book will not pretend to settle it.

## What to watch, quarter by quarter

For a reader who wants to follow this argument in the filings rather than in the commentary, the sequence is short and mostly free.

| What to read | Where it is | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Net revenue retention or dollar-based expansion | Supplemental metrics, investor deck | Whether the existing customer base is still growing on its own |
| cRPO or current cloud backlog growth vs revenue growth | Results release; revenue recognition note | Whether customers are committing further out or pulling in |
| Gross margin trend, quarter on quarter | Income statement | Whether inference cost is arriving faster than price |
| Organic vs acquired growth in any AI metric | Results release, if split is given | How much of the headline was bought |
| Goodwill and intangibles added | Balance sheet; business combinations note | The size of the future impairment risk |
| Sales and marketing as a share of revenue | Income statement | Whether growth is getting more expensive to buy |

Two of those six are about acquisitions, which is deliberate. In a period when every incumbent is buying capability, the difference between a company growing and a company assembling is the single easiest thing for a careless reader to miss and the single easiest thing for a careful one to check.

## Where this leaves the top of the stack

The four-layer frame puts these companies at the application layer, where the user finally pays. That is where their revenue comes from. It is not, any more, where all of their costs sit.

Every AI feature these companies ship generates a bill from the layer below. Some of them pay a model provider by the token. Some pay a cloud by the hour. Some do both, and some are building their own inference capacity to stop doing either. Whichever route, a business that for twenty-five years converted revenue into gross profit at close to eighty cents in the dollar has acquired a variable cost that grows with product usage and is priced by someone else. That is what "value moving down the stack" looks like from inside an application company's income statement: not a dramatic loss of relevance, but a new line in cost of revenue, growing.

The incumbents' answer is that they hold the two things the layers below cannot manufacture — the customer's own data, and the workflow the customer's organisation has agreed to run on. If that is right, the application layer keeps a durable margin and pays its inference bill out of it, much as it once paid its hosting bill. If it is wrong, these companies become a distribution channel with a support desk, and the margin migrates to whoever owns the model and the machines.

The accounts as at August 2026 do not yet decide it. They show growth, record revenue, expanding AI metrics and compressed valuations, all at once. That is not a contradiction. It is a market pricing duration while an income statement reports a period. The reader's advantage is knowing which of the two is a measurement and which is an opinion, and where to look each quarter to find out which one was right.
