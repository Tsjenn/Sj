# Meta

On 29 July 2026, Meta reported the largest quarter of advertising revenue in its history — fifty-nine point three six billion US dollars, up twenty-seven per cent on the year — and the shares fell hard anyway, on light revenue guidance and a free cash flow figure that had nearly vanished, as reported by CNBC that day. A company posting record sales and getting punished for it is not unusual in this industry. What makes Meta worth a chapter of its own is the reason the cash had gone, and the fact that, alone among the giants, Meta cannot point to a single external customer who will ever pay it back directly.

Every other large company in this book that spends heavily on AI infrastructure can name the invoice that justifies the spending. Microsoft rents Azure capacity to enterprises and charges for Copilot seats. Amazon rents AWS capacity to nearly everyone, including the model labs it has invested in. Alphabet rents Google Cloud capacity and sells Gemini access. Nvidia, one layer down, sells the machines themselves. In each case the capital expenditure is a factory, and the factory has customers, and the customers generate revenue lines you can find in the accounts and margins you can argue about.

Meta's AI capital expenditure has no such invoice. Nobody rents Meta's data centres. Nobody pays Meta by the token for its models — the models are given away. The company guided its 2026 capital expenditure to between one hundred and thirty and one hundred and forty-five billion US dollars, per its second-quarter 2026 results reported by CNBC in July 2026, and essentially none of that money will ever be billed to an outside party. The return, if it comes, arrives through a different door entirely: better-targeted advertisements, shown to people who linger a little longer, sold to advertisers at slightly better prices. The payback is measured in engagement and auction dynamics, not in contracts.

That changes almost everything about how the spending should be analysed, and this chapter works through what it changes: why the closed loop is a genuine strength, why it makes giving models away not merely tolerable but rational, and why it removes the one discipline that keeps every other capex programme honest — a customer who can decline to pay.

## A business with one product and one payer

Start with what Meta actually sells, because it is routinely misdescribed. Meta does not sell social networking; users have never paid for it. Meta sells attention, in the form of advertising placements, to businesses.

The accounts make the point without ambiguity. For the full year 2025, Meta reported total revenue of two hundred point nine seven billion US dollars, up twenty-two per cent on 2024, per the company's fourth-quarter and full-year 2025 results reported at the end of January 2026. In the fourth quarter alone, advertising revenue was fifty-eight point one four billion US dollars of total revenue of fifty-nine point eight nine billion — which is to say that advertising was roughly ninety-seven per cent of everything the company took in that quarter, per the same results. Full-year net income was reported at sixty point four six billion US dollars.

The audience that attention is harvested from is enormous and still growing. The company reported three point five eight billion "family daily active people" on average for December 2025, up seven per cent year on year, per the same results announcement — meaning that on an average day, a number of people approaching half the population of the planet opened Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp or Messenger. There is no second act hiding in the segment note, either. The company's other reportable segment, Reality Labs, produced revenue of two point two one billion US dollars in 2025 and an operating loss of nineteen point one nine billion, wider than the seventeen point seven three billion lost the year before, per the full-year 2025 results as covered by specialist XR reporting in early 2026. Reality Labs is a bet; advertising is the business.

Hold that structure in mind: one revenue engine, extraordinarily profitable, funded by millions of advertisers rather than a handful of large customers, with no meaningful customer concentration and no single contract whose loss would matter. Then ask where AI fits into it.

## The different equation

For the other giants, the AI investment case runs through the market. Capital becomes data centres; data centres become rentable capacity or metered tokens; capacity and tokens become revenue from customers; the revenue either covers the depreciation or it does not, and the segment disclosures let outsiders keep score. It is the ordinary industrial loop, and most of this book has been spent inside it.

Meta's loop never leaves the building. Capital becomes data centres; data centres train and run models; the models rank feeds, target advertisements, generate ad creative, power assistants inside the apps; better ranking and targeting produce more engagement and better auction prices; more engagement and better prices produce more advertising revenue. The customer at the end of the chain — the advertiser — is often not aware that the machinery has changed at all. They simply observe that a campaign converted better this quarter, and they bid accordingly.

The two loops differ in ways that matter to anyone reading the accounts.

| | The hyperscaler equation | Meta's equation |
|---|---|---|
| What the capex builds | Capacity sold to outside customers | Capacity consumed internally |
| Where the return appears | A named revenue line (cloud, AI services) | Inside the existing advertising line |
| Who verifies the return | Customers, by renewing or leaving | Nobody outside the company |
| Pricing pressure on the return | Direct — rivals quote against you | Indirect — via ad auction dynamics |
| Failure mode | Empty capacity, visible in segment margins | Invisible — absorbed into a growing ad business |

The strengths of Meta's side of the table are real. There is no price war to fight at the model layer, because Meta charges nothing there. There is no customer concentration, because the paying customers are millions of advertisers whose individual decisions barely register. And the channel through which the AI return arrives — the advertising auction — is one of the highest-margin revenue mechanisms ever built. If a model improvement genuinely lifts conversion rates, the auction converts that lift into revenue automatically, without a sales force, without a renegotiation, without a procurement department on the other side. It is the only place in this industry where better intelligence monetises itself silently.

The weaknesses sit in the last two rows of the table, and the rest of this chapter is mostly about them.

## Why giving the models away is rational

Meta's Llama family of models has been distributed since 2023 on open-weight terms: the trained parameters can be downloaded, fine-tuned and run by almost anyone, free of charge, with a licence carve-out requiring separate terms for products exceeding seven hundred million monthly active users. The downloads have been on a scale that no paid model could match — one point two billion by April 2025, as reported by TechCrunch that month, and past two billion by mid-2026 per industry trackers. Set against the business model of the labs one layer up the stack, which live or die by revenue per token, this looks like an act of charity. It is nothing of the kind.

The logic is an old one in this industry: commoditise the thing adjacent to your profit engine. Meta earns no revenue from selling model access, so giving models away costs it no revenue. But every rival at the application and model layers must charge for intelligence, because intelligence is their product. A free model of respectable quality, available to any developer, sets a floor under the whole market's pricing. It makes "good enough and free" the default alternative in every procurement conversation a paid lab has. Meta spends its capex either way — the models exist to serve the advertising machine — and releasing the weights converts a by-product into a competitive weapon at approximately zero marginal cost.

There are quieter benefits stacked on top. An open model attracts researchers who want their work used widely, in a hiring market where the scarce resource is people. It recruits an unpaid ecosystem that finds bugs, builds tooling and demonstrates use cases. And it positions Meta with regulators and developers as the open alternative to closed labs — a reputational asset that cost, again, nothing it was otherwise going to sell.

The strategy is contingent, though, not a philosophy, and 2026 demonstrated that. In April 2026 the company shipped the first model in a new proprietary series developed by Meta Superintelligence Labs — its first significant step away from strict open-weight release, as reported by CNBC in April 2026 — while continuing to publish Llama alongside it. The reading offered here is the simple one: Meta gives away what it is rational to give away, and withholds what it is rational to withhold, and the boundary between the two will move as the competitive arithmetic moves. A reader who treats the openness as ideology will be surprised by its limits; a reader who treats it as pricing strategy will not.

Superintelligence Labs itself was assembled the expensive way. In June 2025 Meta invested fourteen point three billion US dollars in the data-labelling firm Scale AI for a forty-nine per cent stake, bringing Scale's founder Alexandr Wang across to lead a new superintelligence unit, as reported by CNBC in June 2025. Fourteen point three billion is a figure worth pausing on: it approached the total that some national governments were then proposing to spend on AI altogether, and Meta paid it substantially to acquire a team and its leader. The accounting treatment of a forty-nine per cent stake — an investment, not a consolidated subsidiary — also means most of Scale's own economics stay off Meta's income statement. That is not improper; it is simply another way in which the true cost of this programme resists being read off any single line.

## What the accounts show, and what they cannot

Here is the difficulty for anyone auditing Meta's AI thesis from outside: the return has no line of its own.

Reality Labs demonstrates what visibility looks like. Because it is a reportable segment, its nineteen point one nine billion dollar operating loss for 2025 sat in the results for anyone to see, and has been argued over publicly every quarter for years. The AI programme, which now consumes several times Reality Labs' spending, enjoys no such exposure. Its costs are dispersed — depreciation of data centres through cost of revenue, researcher compensation through R&D, the Scale AI stake on the balance sheet as an investment — and its benefits are dispersed further still, folded invisibly into a two-hundred-billion-dollar advertising line that was growing anyway. When advertising revenue rises twenty-two per cent, as it did in 2025 per the company's results, how much of that was AI-driven ranking improvements, how much was price, how much was user growth, and how much would have happened regardless? The accounts do not say, because they cannot. No accounting standard requires a company to isolate the counterfactual.

What the accounts do show, with perfect clarity, is the cost side arriving. The second quarter of 2026 was the demonstration. Alongside the record advertising revenue, the company reported total costs and expenses of forty-two point zero three billion US dollars, up fifty-five per cent year on year — a figure inflated by two point four billion in legal charges and one point one eight billion in severance tied to a reduction of about eight thousand roles, but driven underneath by infrastructure and compensation. Operating margin compressed to thirty-one per cent from forty-three per cent a year earlier. Capital expenditure in the single quarter was thirty-one point one billion, consuming roughly ninety-eight per cent of the quarter's thirty-one point eight six billion of operating cash flow and leaving free cash flow of seven hundred and eighty-four million US dollars — down about ninety-one per cent year on year. All of these figures are from the company's second-quarter 2026 results as reported by CNBC and earnings coverage in July 2026. Full-year expense guidance went to one hundred and sixty-five to one hundred and sixty-nine billion; capex guidance, as noted, to one hundred and thirty to one hundred and forty-five billion.

A company generating sixty billion dollars of revenue in a quarter and less than a billion of free cash flow is not in difficulty. It is, however, making a very large claim about the future, and the claim compounds. Capital expenditure does not hit profit when it is spent; it hits profit later, as depreciation, spread over the assumed life of the assets. Chapter four of this book dealt with that machinery at length, and Meta is now its largest single exhibit outside the cloud vendors. Roughly one hundred and forty billion dollars of 2026 spending becomes a rolling charge against the profits of 2027, 2028, 2029 and beyond — joined each year by the depreciation from the next year's spending, for as long as the programme runs. The advertising business does not merely need to keep growing. It needs to keep growing fast enough to absorb a depreciation charge that is itself growing, or reported margins fall even while the business performs.

And the commitments reach years out. The company is building a data-centre campus in Richland Parish, Louisiana, planned to reach around five gigawatts of capacity, with the investment there reported at more than fifty billion US dollars, per CNBC in July 2026; a one-gigawatt cluster in Ohio was reported as coming online in 2026, per data-centre trade reporting. Five gigawatts is the scale of a large city's power draw, committed to before anyone can know what the models of 2030 will need or earn. That is the nature of infrastructure, as earlier chapters said of TSMC's fabs: capex is a forecast expressed in concrete. The difference is that TSMC's forecast is disciplined by customers who prepay and commit. Meta's is disciplined by Meta.

## An illustrative payback test

Because no disclosure isolates the return, the only honest way to show the arithmetic is with invented numbers. Take an illustrative company, with round figures chosen for clarity and belonging to no real business.

Suppose it spends one hundred and forty billion dollars in a year on AI infrastructure, and suppose the assets are depreciated straight-line over seven years — a generous assumption if the hardware ages the way accelerators have been ageing. That is twenty billion a year of new depreciation, every year the spending is repeated at that level, stacking until the charge from year one rolls off.

| Illustrative item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Annual AI capex | 140 billion |
| Assumed asset life | 7 years |
| New annual depreciation created | 20 billion |
| Steady-state annual charge if spending repeats | 140 billion |
| Advertising operating margin assumed | 40 per cent |
| Extra ad revenue needed to cover 20 billion of charge | 50 billion |

On these invented numbers, each single year of spending obliges the advertising business to find fifty billion dollars of additional annual revenue — at a healthy forty per cent margin — just for that year's capex to break even at the operating line. Repeat the spending and the requirement stacks. The real company's figures will differ in every particular: actual asset lives are a management judgement, some capacity replaces cost that would have been incurred anyway, and some spending buys defence rather than growth, which is a return of a different kind. But the shape of the test is right, and it is the test the reader should keep applying: put the incremental depreciation on one side, the incremental advertising profit on the other, and ask whether the second line is plausibly larger. Nobody outside the company can compute it precisely. That is exactly the point.

## Engagement is not an invoice

Now name the risk directly, because it is the heart of the chapter.

When Microsoft builds a data centre and misjudges demand, the error surfaces. Capacity sits idle, cloud growth disappoints against guidance, the margin in a disclosed segment sags, analysts ask pointed questions on the call, and the next year's capex gets trimmed. The customer relationship acts as an audit. Payment is the most honest feedback mechanism commerce has ever produced, and everyone selling compute or tokens is subject to it continuously.

Meta has removed itself from that mechanism. Its AI spending is validated internally, by metrics the company alone can see: watch time, session length, conversion rates, revenue per person. Those metrics are real and the people analysing them are capable. But they are leading indicators standing in for a return, not the return itself, and the company both makes the investment and marks its own homework. When engagement rises, was it the new model or the new format? When ad prices firm, was it targeting quality or simply advertiser demand? Inside the company these questions get careful answers. Outside, there is no way to check them, and no customer whose refusal to renew would force the issue.

The nearest thing to an external check is the one that operated on 29 July 2026: the equity market's response to guidance and free cash flow. It is a blunt instrument. It disciplines the share price, not the programme, and a founder-controlled company — Meta's dual-class structure leaves control with its founder, per the company's own governance disclosures — can elect to ignore it for a very long time.

There is a precedent for what that latitude permits, and it sits in Meta's own segment note. Reality Labs has recorded cumulative operating losses across years that exceed the annual research budgets of most industrial companies — nineteen point one nine billion in 2025 alone, on revenue of two point two one billion, per the full-year results — sustained through every external objection because internal conviction, not external payment, was the test being applied. This book does not allege the spending is wasted; the glasses category grew in 2025, per the same results, and long bets are sometimes right. The observation is narrower and matters more: Meta has demonstrated, at the scale of tens of billions, that it will fund an internally-justified programme for a decade without an invoice in sight. The AI programme is running on the same fuel, at several times the burn rate.

Set against that, the honest counterweight: Meta's internal-returns model has already worked once, spectacularly. The company's original machine-learning buildout — the ranking and recommendation systems of the 2010s — was also justified by engagement rather than invoices, was also invisible in the accounts, and produced one of the most profitable businesses ever operated. The 2025 results, with revenue up twenty-two per cent on an already vast base and three point five eight billion daily users, are partly the compounded return on exactly this kind of unsegmented internal spending. The question is not whether the mechanism can pay. It has paid. The question is whether it pays at one-hundred-and-forty-billion-dollar annual scale, against physical assets that depreciate on a schedule, in competition with rivals who face the same costs but also collect revenue from them.

## Challenge and opportunity

The challenge, then, in the form this book has used throughout: Meta is running the industry's largest capex programme without external revenue attached, funded from a single advertising engine, governed by internal metrics, controlled by one person, with a depreciation wave building years into the future and commitments — Louisiana alone reported above fifty billion dollars, per CNBC in July 2026 — that cannot be quickly unwound. If advertising growth slows while the charge arrives, margins compress with no offsetting revenue line to point to, and the market's July 2026 reaction suggests how that gets received. The free cash flow number to watch was seven hundred and eighty-four million dollars for the second quarter of 2026. Whether that figure recovers or stays pinned near zero is the single clearest external signal of how the equation is balancing.

The opportunity is the mirror image, and it is not small. If the models genuinely improve targeting and creative — and generative tools that build advertisements for small businesses are the kind of product that shows up directly in auction revenue — then Meta owns the shortest, highest-margin path from model quality to money that exists anywhere in this industry, applied across three and a half billion daily users, with no sales cycle and no per-token price war. Its assistant reached a reported one and a half billion monthly users by mid-2026, per industry trackers — a figure the company promotes and nobody audits, and the reader should weight it accordingly — but distribution at that scale is the asset every model lab covets and none possesses. Meta did not have to buy its way to the application layer. It has been standing on it all along.

Where does that leave Meta in the four-layer frame? Oddly placed, and deliberately so. It occupies the application layer's most profitable corner while refusing to sell anything at the model or compute layers; it spends at compute-layer scale — its market capitalisation stood at roughly one point four trillion US dollars in mid-August 2026, per companiesmarketcap and stockanalysis rankings, around eleventh in the world and a fraction of Nvidia's roughly five point three to five point four trillion over the same period per the same class of sources — yet its spending flows down the stack to Nvidia and the foundries like everyone else's. In a book about value moving down the stack, Meta is the giant betting it can hold value at the top by consuming the lower layers rather than selling them.

None of this is a view on the shares — this book does not give those, and both market figures above will have moved by the time this page is read. The verdict worth having is not a price target but a habit: each quarter, open the results, put the capital expenditure and depreciation lines against the advertising revenue line, and ask the accountant's question one more time. The equation is different here. It is not exempt.
