# The Margin Question

In the quarter ended 26 April 2026, Nvidia reported revenue of eighty-one point six billion US dollars, up eighty-five per cent on the year, at a GAAP gross margin of seventy-four point nine per cent, per the company's results announcement of 20 May 2026. In its fiscal 2026 fourth quarter, Microsoft reported a Microsoft Cloud gross margin of sixty-five per cent, down year on year, which the company attributed to sales mix shifting towards Azure and to continued investment in AI infrastructure. One floor above that, the two large private model companies were reported during 2026 to be running gross margins in the low forties and high thirties respectively, unaudited. And at the top, where the end user actually pays, no one publishes a gross margin on artificial intelligence at all, because the revenue is bundled into products that were sold before any of this began.

Read those four numbers in order and the shape of the present moment is plain. The profit is being taken at the bottom, first, in cash, at the highest margin, and it thins as it travels up. That is the observation this book has been assembling for fifty-one chapters.

It is not, however, an answer to the question this chapter asks. The question is not who is taking the profit now. It is who keeps it.

## What the question actually asks

Be precise about the terms, because the loose version of this argument is what fills conference stages.

Every dollar of profit anywhere in this stack originates at the top. There is no other source. Someone buys a subscription, a seat, a contract, or an advertisement, and that money enters the system. Everything that happens afterwards — the model company paying the cloud, the cloud paying the chip vendor, the chip vendor paying the foundry, the foundry paying the equipment maker — is an internal transfer. The stack does not create cash. It distributes cash that arrived at the top, plus, for the moment, a very large volume of investor capital that arrived through the side door.

So "which layer keeps the profit" is a question about distribution, not creation. Three things decide it, and they are the same three that decide it in any industry.

The first is bargaining power: who can refuse. A supplier with no substitute sets its price. A supplier with three competent rivals discovers what its cost base looks like.

The second is capital intensity: what it costs to stay in position. A layer can post a magnificent gross margin and keep none of it, because everything it earns is consumed by the next fab, the next training run, the next generation of hardware. Gross margin measures the price you can charge. Return on capital measures whether charging it was worth the trouble.

The third is durability: whether the position survives the buyer adapting to it. High margins are not stable states. They are invitations. Every layer in this book with a seventy per cent gross margin has, at this moment, several very well-funded customers working on paying less.

Hold those three, because the framework at the end of this chapter is built from them.

## The case for the bottom of the stack

Start with the argument that has been winning.

The semiconductor layer is the only floor where the constraint is not money. A leading-edge accelerator requires a design, a process, a fab and lithography tools that have exactly one supplier. Capacity is decided years in advance — TSMC guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion US dollars, above analyst expectations, in its January 2026 results, and that money buys output for 2028 and beyond. A buyer who wants more chips in eighteen months cannot obtain them by paying more. There is nothing to buy.

That is what real scarcity looks like in an income statement. Nvidia's data centre revenue in the quarter ended 26 April 2026 was seventy-five point two billion US dollars, up ninety-two per cent year on year, including fourteen point eight billion of networking, per the company's May 2026 announcement, and it carried a gross margin near seventy-five per cent. TSMC's gross margin was sixty-seven point seven per cent in the second quarter of 2026, up more than nine points on the year, per company results. ASML crossed five hundred billion US dollars in market value for the first time in January 2026, per Reuters and Yahoo Finance, on the strength of being the only company in the world that makes a particular machine.

The bottom of the stack also gets paid first, and gets paid in cash, before anyone above it knows whether the investment worked. This is not a small point. The four largest buyers were on course to spend approaching seven hundred billion US dollars of capital expenditure in 2026, per CNBC in February 2026, with other compilations of the same guidance reaching about seven hundred and twenty-five billion. Whatever the return on that spending turns out to be, the vendors have already banked the revenue. The risk sits upstairs.

Now the case against, stated as honestly.

Seventy-five per cent gross margin is the strongest possible signal to a customer that they should build it themselves, and the customers here are four of the most capable engineering organisations on earth, all of whom now design their own accelerators. Customer concentration in this layer is extreme and disclosed as such in the filings; a handful of buyers account for a large share of the revenue, which means a handful of purchasing decisions can change the picture in a quarter.

And the memory business, one aisle over, is the standing warning about what scarcity does when it ends. The 2026 HBM market was estimated at about fifty-four point six billion US dollars, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, with capacity reported sold out across major suppliers through 2026, per industry analysis in 2026; SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, per CNBC in August 2026. Memory has been sold out before. It has also, repeatedly, been the most brutal commodity in electronics, because the response to shortage is capacity, and capacity arrives in a lump three years later, whether or not demand is still there. A thirty-eight billion dollar plant is a bet that it will be.

## The case for the compute layer

The cloud's argument is that it owns the thing that is becoming genuinely scarce next: not chips, but the place to put them. Power, land, grid connections, water, planning consent and a workforce that can build a data centre. Chips can be manufactured faster than substations can be energised. On that view the cloud is not a reseller of Nvidia's product; it is the owner of the site, and sites are finite.

The numbers currently support a strong version of this. AWS reported second-quarter 2026 revenue of forty-two point two billion US dollars, growing thirty-seven per cent, with operating income of sixteen point six billion — an operating margin near thirty-nine per cent, expanded roughly six hundred and fifty basis points year on year — per Amazon's results as reported by CNBC and other coverage on 30 July 2026. That is a remarkable margin for a business whose principal input is bought from a supplier charging seventy-five per cent.

The cloud also owns the enterprise relationship, and enterprise relationships are sticky in a way consumer subscriptions are not. Data has weight. Moving a petabyte and the eleven systems that depend on it is a project, not a decision.

Against that, the accountant has an objection, and it is the sharpest objection in this book.

Cloud operating margin is not a measurement. It is substantially a policy choice, because the largest single cost of running a fleet of machines is the depreciation charge, and the depreciation charge depends on an estimate of useful life made by management. Amazon has now demonstrated the mechanism in both directions inside two years. Effective 1 January 2024 it extended the useful life of its servers from five years to six, which reduced depreciation and amortisation by about three point two billion US dollars and increased net income by roughly two point five billion. Effective 1 January 2025 it reversed course for a subset of servers and networking equipment, cutting the life from six years back to five and citing the increased pace of technology development in artificial intelligence and machine learning; that change increased depreciation and amortisation by about one point four billion dollars and reduced 2025 net income by about one billion, or ten cents a share, falling mainly on the AWS segment. Those figures are disclosed in Amazon's own annual reporting for 2025 and were set out in filings analysis published by Hudson Labs and Calcbench.

Nothing about the machines changed on either date. Several billion dollars of reported profit did.

Microsoft's disclosure makes the second half of the point. Microsoft Cloud gross margin was sixty-five per cent in the fiscal 2026 fourth quarter, down year on year, attributed to the mix shifting towards Azure and to AI infrastructure investment and increased usage, partly offset by efficiency gains. The AI part of cloud is, so far, the lower-margin part of cloud. Growth in it dilutes the average.

So the compute layer's claim on the profit rests on two things that are not yet settled: whether the assets last as long as the accounts assume, and whether power and land stay scarce once the current construction programme lands. If AI hardware ages faster than a five-year schedule — and the whole industry's replacement behaviour will reveal that before any auditor does — then a meaningful portion of the last three years of cloud profit was borrowed from the next three.

## The case for the model layer

The model layer has the most interesting argument and the weakest accounts.

The argument runs like this. Inference cost per unit of capability falls with each hardware generation, each software optimisation and each architectural improvement, and it has been falling quickly. Prices to customers fall too, but historically prices fall more slowly than costs, because a price is a negotiated thing and a cost is an engineering thing. That gap is exactly the mechanism that makes a mature process node profitable for a foundry, and it is the model layer's route from forty per cent gross margin to something that looks like software.

There is evidence for the direction, even in unaudited numbers. Figures shared with investors and reported in 2026 put Anthropic's compute cost at about seventy-one cents for every dollar of revenue in the first quarter of 2026, with about fifty-six cents projected for the second, and gross margin tracking around forty-four per cent. OpenAI's gross margin was reported at roughly thirty-nine per cent in the first quarter of 2026, against about thirty-three per cent a year earlier. Both companies are private, neither figure is audited, and both reached the public through people with reasons to share them. Read as direction rather than as measurement, they point the same way: cost per unit of output falling, margin improving.

The second argument is brand. If one model is meaningfully better than the others at something a customer cares about, the customer pays for that model specifically, and the layer below becomes a supplier rather than a rival. Both large private labs have raised capital on that proposition at scale — Anthropic closing a sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, per CNBC; OpenAI closing a one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round in March 2026 at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes. Those are prices agreed between a seller and a buyer for a slice of a private company. They are not audited valuations and they are not the same thing as evidence that the margin arrives.

The case against is threefold and each part is serious.

Training cost is not a one-off. In an ordinary software company, the product is built once and sold many times, and the gross margin is the whole point. In a frontier model company, the product decays. A model that is two generations old is not a paid-off asset; it is a liability with running costs. That means research and training spending behaves less like capital expenditure and more like the cost of remaining in business — closer to a pharmaceutical company's pipeline than to a software licence.

Competition among near-equals. When several models are close enough that a customer routes work between them by price and latency, the buyer stops paying for a brand and starts paying for a unit. Unit pricing is the beginning of commoditisation everywhere it has ever appeared.

And the price floor is zero for anything merely good. Meta gives capable models away, rationally, because its returns come from advertising rather than from token sales, as chapter forty-seven set out. A layer with a well-capitalised participant that has no need to charge has a difficult time defending the middle of its price list.

## The case for the top of the stack

The application layer's argument is the oldest one in commerce: own the customer.

It is also the argument with the best historical record, which is why so many people assume it will win again. The application layer is the only floor whose price the buyer cannot easily decompose. A customer paying per token knows exactly what a token costs and can compare it. A customer paying for a seat in a system that holds their records, their approvals, their audit trail and eleven years of history is buying something with no published unit rate at all. That is the definition of pricing power.

The mature version of this business is visible in the accounts of the incumbent software companies of chapter forty-nine. Salesforce reported fiscal 2026 revenue of forty-one point five billion US dollars, up ten per cent, with GAAP operating margin of twenty point one per cent and non-GAAP operating margin of thirty-four point one per cent, per the company's fourth-quarter fiscal 2026 results announced in February 2026. Note what that is and is not. It is not a seventy-five per cent gross margin. It is a durable, cash-generative, twenty-per-cent-operating-margin business built on customers who have been there for a decade and are not going anywhere this year. Over twenty years, that has compounded into more shareholder value than most of the hardware companies that supplied it.

Against that, chapter forty-three set out the squeeze plainly and it need not be repeated at length: the consumer price at the top was set by convention rather than derived from cost, the costs below it are metered and controlled by layers with more scarcity, the doorway is taxed at up to thirty per cent under Apple's published App Store terms, and the model providers sell the same intelligence to the application's own customers through their own consumer tiers.

The honest summary is that the application layer has the strongest structural claim and the weakest current evidence. Switching cost is real, but it is built over years, and most of the companies trying to build it started in the last three.

## The answer has changed before

Anyone who tells you confidently where the profit will sit in 2035 should be asked what they would have said in 1985, and in 1999.

In the mainframe era the question did not arise, because one company sold the machine, the operating system, the applications, the maintenance contract and the training. The stack existed but it had no seams, so there was nothing for the profit to move between.

The personal computer put in the seams, and the profit moved to the bottom — decisively, and against the expectation of nearly everyone who had grown up in the previous arrangement. Intel's own annual report for 1997 shows net revenues of twenty-five point zero seven billion US dollars against cost of sales of nine point nine four five billion — about sixty cents of gross profit in every dollar of sales — and net income of six point nine four five billion. In the same period the companies whose brands were on the actual computers were being ground down. Compaq had grown accustomed to a comfortable thirty-five per cent gross margin and found a way to live on twenty-seven, while Dell was making an estimated twenty-two per cent selling cheap boxes, per Forbes in January 1998. The company that made a component inside the machine earned nearly three times the gross margin of the company that made the machine, and the buyer, in the end, chose the machine by which component was inside it.

That was a bottom-of-stack outcome, and it lasted about fifteen years.

Then it reversed. In the internet and mobile era the profit moved back up. The component makers who had won the previous round found that their products had become interchangeable inputs into devices, and the value collected instead at the point where the customer was — in the device and its operating system, in the doorway to the applications, in the applications themselves, and in the advertising businesses attached to them. The clearest survivor of that shift is still near the top of the market capitalisation table, with Apple at roughly four point four one trillion US dollars as at mid-August 2026, per the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings, against Nvidia at roughly five point three to five point four trillion over the same period. The telecommunications carriers, meanwhile, spent enormous sums building the networks the whole thing ran on and captured very little of what ran across them. They were, in the language of this book, a compute layer whose capital expenditure created a profit pool that someone else drank from.

| Era | Where the profit concentrated | What the previous era's consensus had expected |
|---|---|---|
| Mainframe, to the early 1980s | The integrated vendor — no seams for it to move through | That integration was permanent |
| Personal computer, roughly 1985 to 2005 | The components: processor and operating system | That the brand on the box would keep it |
| Internet and mobile, roughly 2007 to 2020 | The device, the doorway and the applications | That the component makers would keep it |
| AI, 2023 to date | So far, the bottom: silicon and the tools to make it | That the software layer would keep it, as before |

Three reversals in forty years. Each time, the consensus at the start of the era was that the previous era's answer would hold, and each time the consensus was wrong. That is not a reason to bet on reversal. It is a reason to distrust the confidence of anyone in the fourth row.

Note also that these things move slowly enough to be invisible while they happen and fast enough to be obvious afterwards. Nobody circulated a memorandum announcing that the profit had left the personal computer manufacturers. Their gross margins simply drifted down a point or two a year for a decade.

## The framework instead of the forecast

This book will not tell you which layer wins. It will not give a price target, it will not recommend buying or selling anything, and it takes the view that a confident forecast here would be worth less than nothing, because it would replace the reader's own attention with a borrowed conclusion.

What is worth having is a set of tests you can run yourself, every reporting season, against public numbers. Six of them follow. They are ordinary questions, and they are the ones that decide the outcome.

### One: who can say no?

Pricing power is the ability to decline a sale. Look for it in the customer concentration disclosure, which every listed company has to give and almost no headline mentions. If a supplier's five largest customers are a large share of its revenue, the supplier has less power than its gross margin suggests, because five conversations can change everything. If the customers have no alternative source, the supplier has more power than its size suggests.

Watch, specifically, for the moment a large customer starts qualifying a second source. That is usually disclosed by the customer, in a product announcement, long before it appears in the supplier's revenue.

### Two: is the margin cash, or is it an estimate?

Take the reported operating margin and ask which parts of it a person chose. Depreciation policy is the big one, and the useful-life note in the accounting policies section is where it lives. Read it every year and note when it changes. Amazon's two changes in two years, described earlier, moved billions of dollars of reported profit in opposite directions without touching a single machine.

Then compare operating cash flow with reported profit over three or four years. If reported profit is consistently and increasingly above cash generation, the difference is being carried by accounting estimates, and estimates revert.

### Three: what does it cost to stay in position?

This is the test that separates a good margin from a good business. Add capital expenditure and research spending together and express them as a share of revenue. Then ask what happens if the company stops. A foundry that stops building fabs falls off the leading edge in four years. A model company that stops training falls behind in about one. An enterprise software company that stops developing loses customers slowly, over many years, and can harvest cash for a long time while it does.

A layer that must spend its entire gross profit to remain where it is does not keep the profit. It rents its position, annually, from the layer below.

### Four: can the buyer compare units?

Commoditisation is not primarily a technical event. It is the moment the buyer acquires a unit of comparison. Petrol became a commodity when it was sold by the litre at a posted price.

So watch the pricing page. Per-token pricing invites comparison. Per-seat pricing resists it. Per-outcome pricing resists it more, and is harder to sell. Any layer moving from bundled pricing to metered pricing is moving towards commoditisation, whatever its reasons for doing so.

### Five: what happens if demand pauses for four quarters?

Not stops — pauses. Every layer in this stack is now carrying commitments made in the expectation of continuous growth: purchase obligations, take-or-pay capacity contracts, construction in progress, and in the semiconductor layer, inventory.

Inventory days are the earliest published warning of a turn in this industry, and they are in every quarterly report. Purchase obligations and other contractual commitments are in the notes, are rarely reported on, and tell you what a company has promised to spend whether or not the revenue arrives. A layer that can shrink its costs faster than its revenue falls survives a pause. A layer that has signed for five years of capacity does not.

### Six: where does the switching cost actually live?

Ask a specific question: if this customer wanted to leave, what exactly would they have to rebuild? For a chip, the answer is a software ecosystem and a compiler toolchain, which is why compatibility is worth more than performance. For a cloud, it is data and the systems attached to it. For a model, in many applications, it is an afternoon of work and a changed configuration line. For an application holding a company's records and approvals, it is a project with a budget and a steering committee.

Remaining performance obligations and backlog disclosures are the nearest thing to a published measure of this. They tell you how much revenue is already contracted and over what period. They do not tell you whether the customer is happy, but they tell you whether the customer can leave this year.

Run those six tests across the layers each quarter and you will not have a prediction. You will have something more useful: a view that updates when the facts do.

## What can be said without predicting

Three things can be said with reasonable confidence, and they are worth separating from the guesswork.

The first is that the current distribution is not an equilibrium. A stack in which the bottom floor earns seventy-five cents of gross profit on the dollar and the top floor cannot publish an AI margin at all is a stack with a great deal of pressure in it. Pressure resolves. It resolves through second sourcing, through in-house design, through renegotiated contracts and through customers doing less than they said they would. None of that requires anything dramatic to happen.

The second is that the whole structure rests on the application layer's revenue proving durable, which is the subject chapter fifty-one dealt with. The capital expenditure at the bottom is justified by expected demand at the top. If that demand is largely the purchase of a saved hour and a genuinely new capability, the spending is a factory. If a material share of it is the purchase of relief from competitive anxiety, the spending is a fashion, and fashions unwind faster than depreciation schedules. The honest position is that both are present in current revenue and nobody outside the buying organisations knows the mix.

The third is that "which layer wins" may be the wrong shape of question, because no important company is confined to one layer. Alphabet operates on all four floors; Amazon on three and is the largest investor in a company on the fourth; Microsoft rents compute, sells models, sells applications and now designs silicon; Nvidia sells chips and has become a significant investor in the companies that buy them. When the same company sits on several floors, the profit does not have to move between companies in order to move between layers. It moves between segments in a single set of accounts, and the segment note is where you find out. The web of cross-ownership that makes this so tangled is the subject of the next part of this book, and it complicates the margin question considerably — because a shareholder who owns a piece of every floor is indifferent to which floor the margin lands on, and a company that owns a piece of its own customer has an incentive that no single-layer analysis will capture.

## The one thing that will not change

Whatever happens to the distribution, the arithmetic at the top does not move. Somebody has to pay, at the end, for something they wanted. Every trillion dollars of market capitalisation in this industry, every fab, every substation, every training run and every valuation agreed in a private round is a claim on future payments by people buying an outcome they judge worth the money.

That is why this part of the book has been about the application layer, even though the profit is currently being taken four floors below it. The bottom of the stack is where the margin is. The top of the stack is where the money comes from. Neither is more important than the other, and the industry only works when both are true at once.

Figures in this chapter are snapshots. Nvidia's gross margin, AWS's operating margin, Microsoft Cloud's gross margin and Salesforce's operating margin are all published quarterly and will have moved by the time you read this; the depreciation policy notes are republished annually and are the first thing to check. The framework is the durable part. Apply it to the current numbers rather than to these.
