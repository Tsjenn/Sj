# What Would Actually Break It

On 1 January 2025, Amazon shortened the estimated useful life of a subset of its servers and network equipment from six years back to five, naming the increased pace of technology development, particularly in artificial intelligence and machine learning, as the reason. The company disclosed that the change was expected to reduce 2025 operating income by approximately seven hundred million US dollars, and separately took a charge of about nine hundred and twenty million dollars for equipment retired early, per Amazon's disclosures in 2025.

There was no press conference. The most important admission any large operator had yet made about the economics of AI-era hardware arrived as two sentences in a note, quantified, dated and easy to miss.

That is how this ends, if it ends. Not with an announcement. With a line item.

## What this chapter is for

The previous chapters put the bear case and the bull case side by side and declined to choose between them. This one refuses to forecast at all. It names six specific ways the current arrangement could fail, describes what each would look like in its early stage rather than its dramatic one, and says where in the public record each would appear first.

Three rules govern what follows.

The first is that "break" needs a definition. A bad quarter is not a break. A share price falling forty per cent is not a break either — that is a repricing, and chapter two explained why a market capitalisation is a photograph rather than a fact. A break, for the purposes of this chapter, means something narrower: an event that permanently impairs the economics of the chain, so that capital already committed does not earn what it was committed to earn. That is an accounting question, not a market one, and it has accounting answers.

The second rule is that early signatures are boring by construction. By the time a failure is legible in a headline it is legible in the share price, which means it has stopped being information. Everything below is chosen because it is published, dated, dull, and available before the interesting part.

The third rule is that none of this is a prediction, a probability, or investment advice. It contains no price target and no view on whether any of the six will happen. Several have partly happened already, which is why they are worth watching rather than debating. Nor are the six independent: at least three share a single underlying variable, and a section near the end deals with what that means.

| Failure mode | The mechanism in one line | Where it surfaces first |
|---|---|---|
| End demand disappoints | Revenue at the top arrives slower than the schedule the capital assumed | Supply purchase commitments; remaining performance obligation; independent adoption surveys |
| Power becomes the hard cap | Capacity is built but cannot be energised on schedule | Construction in progress; grid interconnection queues; capacity auction results |
| A depreciation reset | The useful-life estimate is revised down across the industry | Accounting policies note; changes-in-estimate paragraph; disposal charges |
| Credit tightens for the financed buyers | The marginal buyer's funding becomes expensive or unavailable | Debt note; financing section of the cash flow statement; credit spreads |
| A capability plateau | Models stop improving fast enough to justify the next cluster | Independent capability measurements; model-layer gross margin; R&D disclosure |
| A supply shock | A single point in the chain fails or reprices violently | Commitments note; supplier prepayments; contract price series |

## One: end demand disappoints

Start with the mode everyone names and almost nobody defines.

"Demand disappoints" does not mean demand falls. It means demand grows more slowly than the schedule embedded in decisions already taken. A fab commissioned in 2026 arrives in 2028 or 2029. A depreciation charge assumes revenue across five or six years. A power contract runs to 2042. The industry has committed to a rate of arrival, and the failure mode is arrival at a different rate — which can happen while every year sets a record.

The early signature is a vocabulary change, not a number. It sounds like digestion, normalisation, optimisation, a pause to absorb what has been deployed. Chapter sixty-three noted that this language has appeared at every previous turn, and that executive confidence has been expressed with equal sincerity at every peak and every trough. Treat the words as a prompt to check the figures, never as the finding itself.

Three published series carry the finding.

The first is the supplier's own forward commitment. Nvidia's total supply-related commitments stood at one hundred and nineteen billion US dollars at the end of the quarter ended 26 April 2026, against ninety-five point two billion at the end of the fiscal year in January 2026 and forty-five point eight billion two quarters before that; inventory over the same period rose to twenty-five point eight billion from twenty-one point four, per the company's quarterly chief financial officer commentary as compiled in 2026 trade coverage. Those are binding arrangements with the foundry and the wider supply chain for capacity not yet delivered. They cost money to be wrong about, which is exactly what makes them informative. A supplier's commitment line that flattens while revenue guidance stays confident is the earliest quantified doubt available anywhere in this chain.

The second is contracted revenue. Microsoft's commercial remaining performance obligation was reported at six hundred and twenty-five billion dollars, up eleven per cent, for the quarter ended December 2025, per the earnings call transcript published by The Motley Fool in January 2026. Oracle reported remaining performance obligations of six hundred and thirty-eight billion dollars in its fourth quarter of fiscal 2026. Both are contracted rather than forecast, and both are disclosed under an accounting standard that constrains how they are calculated. Watch two things about them: the growth rate, and the portion expected to convert within twelve months. A backlog that grows while its near-term slice shrinks is lengthening, and a lengthening backlog is a forecast being pushed further out one quarter at a time.

The third sits outside the industry entirely. The United States Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey asks firms directly whether they used artificial intelligence in producing goods or services. Its published results put use between about seventeen and twenty per cent of businesses from December 2025 through May 2026, at nineteen point eight per cent as at May 2026, with between twenty and twenty-three per cent expecting to use it within six months. The respondents are not selling anything, the survey is free, and it is published on a regular cadence. The gap between the expecting share and the using share is a measure of disappointment that nobody in the industry controls.

What would count is the commitment line and the near-term backlog turning together, in the same period, while capital expenditure guidance stays where it is. That combination has a specific meaning: the suppliers have stopped believing the schedule before the buyers have stopped announcing it.

## Two: power becomes the hard cap

This mode is not about demand at all, and that is what makes it easy to misread. It is a schedule failure.

The evidence that it is already binding is public and quantified. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's Queued Up report, 2025 edition, counted nearly eleven thousand six hundred active projects seeking transmission interconnection at the end of 2024, and found that the median time from interconnection request to commercial operation had doubled — from under two years for projects built between 2000 and 2007 to over four years for those built between 2018 and 2024. In July 2025 the capacity auction for the largest electricity market in the United States cleared at the regulatory maximum of three hundred and twenty-nine dollars and seventeen cents per megawatt-day for the delivery year from June 2026, and the December 2025 auction hit a new cap of three hundred and thirty-three dollars and forty-four cents, per Utility Dive and Canary Media. The market monitor, Monitoring Analytics, attributed sixty-three per cent of the price increase in the 2025/26 auction to data centre demand, per Utility Dive's reporting of its analysis. On the equipment side, GE Vernova's gas equipment backlog reached one hundred and sixteen gigawatts by the second quarter of 2026, against annualised production of roughly twenty gigawatts, with delivery slots described as effectively sold out through 2030, per company statements reported by Turbomachinery Magazine, Utility Dive and Power Engineering.

So the constraint is real. The question is what it would look like if it moved from expensive to binding — from a cost problem to a delivery problem.

It would look like assets that exist and do not earn. The place that shows is construction in progress, the category of property and equipment that has been paid for and not yet placed in service. Assets in construction in progress are not depreciated, which produces a peculiar and temporarily flattering effect: cash has left, the balance sheet has grown, and the profit and loss account has not yet been charged. Watch construction in progress as a share of gross property and equipment, quarter by quarter, and watch how long it takes to convert. A rising balance that converts on schedule is a company building. A rising balance that stops converting is a company waiting for a substation.

The corroborating signature is the decision to self-generate. The first Stargate campus at Abilene, Texas, with demand at full build-out reported at around one point two gigawatts, filed permits for on-site gas generation — an initial three hundred and sixty megawatts of turbines, per public filings reported in 2025 — and in 2026 Parker Hannifin announced a supply agreement covering twenty-nine GE Vernova aeroderivative turbines for the campus, a combined one point zero one five gigawatts, per Data Center Dynamics. On-site gas is rarely the cheapest electricity. It is the electricity available on the date the chips arrive. When a developer accepts a permanently higher running cost to protect a schedule, the schedule is the binding constraint, and that decision is disclosed in filings and trade coverage long before it reaches a margin.

The political version of this mode has a precedent with dates attached. Ireland's grid operator stopped accepting new data centre connection applications in the Dublin region in 2022, a freeze only lifted in early 2026 under conditions reported to include majority-renewable supply and full on-site backup, per Energy Connects and KPMG's 2026 policy analysis. Data centres consumed about twenty-two per cent of the country's metered electricity in 2024, up from five per cent in 2015.

Note who this mode hurts, and in what order. It hurts the compute layer's return first, because the compute layer owns the idle asset. The chip vendor has already been paid.

## Three: a depreciation reset

Chapter thirty-two set out the mechanics in full, so the recap is short: the useful life is an estimate made by management, reviewed annually, and revised prospectively. Shorten it and the remaining book value spreads over fewer remaining years. There is no restatement, no catch-up charge and no transaction to announce. The past stays as printed and the future charge rises.

A reset would therefore be the quietest of the six. It would also be the most synchronised, because it would not require a decision by any single company. It requires only that the evidence about how long these machines earn becomes clear enough that the range of defensible estimates narrows, and auditors across the sector reach the same conclusion in the same season.

Four signatures precede it, in roughly this order.

Language comes first. A filing that says management "assessed the useful lives" of equipment during the year is telling you the estimate was actively revisited, whatever conclusion it reached. The sentence appears before the change does.

Disposal and retirement charges come second. Equipment retired before its schedule expires produces a loss on disposal, and that loss is the estimate being contradicted by events. Amazon's roughly nine hundred and twenty million dollar charge in 2025 is the worked example.

Divergence between peers comes third. When companies in the same trade hold visibly different assumptions for near-identical fleets — six years against four, as reported of CoreWeave and Nebius in 2025 — at most one of those reported margins is on the comfortable side of the range. You do not need to know which. You need only restate both to a common life before comparing them, which takes minutes with the property and equipment note.

An observable residual value comes fourth, and would be the most decisive of all. The residual is currently assumed to be small or nil across the industry because there is no deep second-hand market for used accelerators. If a liquid market in used hardware develops and publishes prices, the residual stops being an assumption and becomes a quotation. That would be genuinely new information, and it would arrive from outside the accounts.

One consequence of a reset is worth stating precisely, because it is usually got wrong. Depreciation is a non-cash charge. A reset would cut reported operating profit and shareholders' funds across the compute layer without changing a single dollar of cash flow, and it would leave earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation — the measure most borrowing covenants are written against — untouched. So a depreciation reset is an earnings event and an equity event, not automatically a solvency event. It matters enormously to reported returns on capital and hardly at all to whether the interest gets paid. Anyone who conflates the two will misread the quarter it happens in.

## Four: credit tightens for the financed buyers

This is the fastest-transmitting mode on the list, because credit markets can close in a week for reasons that have nothing to do with anything in this book.

The build is now substantially debt-funded, and the scale is documented. The five largest hyperscalers issued approximately one hundred and twenty-one billion US dollars of United States corporate bonds in 2025, more than four times their 2020 to 2024 annual average of about twenty-eight billion, per Bank of America compilations reported by Fortune in 2026. In March 2026 Amazon completed what IFR described as the largest corporate bond sale in capital markets history, raising fifty-three point eight billion dollars equivalent across two legs against about one hundred and fifty-eight billion of demand, including a euro debut of fourteen and a half billion euros that Bloomberg reported as the biggest corporate deal ever in that currency. In July 2026 the company raised at least a further twenty-five billion dollars and said it would not issue more debt that year, per CNBC on 7 July 2026. Forbes reported in July 2026 that global AI-related debt issuance was heading toward about five hundred and seventy billion dollars in 2026, with roughly two hundred and thirty-six billion priced by 31 May, four times the year-earlier pace.

Some of it does not appear on the borrower's balance sheet at all. Meta placed its Hyperion campus in Louisiana into a joint venture with Blue Owl Capital funds owning eighty per cent and Meta twenty, with roughly twenty-seven point three billion dollars of debt issued through the vehicle, as reported by Bisnow in 2025; because Meta is not the primary beneficiary of the entity, the debt does not consolidate. Among the specialist operators, CoreWeave's total debt reached about thirty-five billion dollars as at 30 June 2026, per Capacity in 2026.

None of that is improper or unusual for a capital-intensive build. The failure mode is not misconduct; it is dependency. The question is what happens to the marginal buyer — the one whose expansion is funded by the next issue rather than by operating cash flow — when the next issue prices materially worse.

The early signature is in the price and structure of new debt rather than in any default. Order-book coverage on hyperscaler bonds had slipped below two times by July 2026, per Fortune's reporting, which is a demand measure the borrower cannot manage. Five-year credit default swaps on Oracle reached about two hundred and twelve basis points in July 2026, described by Bloomberg on 20 July 2026 as near an eighteen-year high on concern about the company's AI-related debt — and, read fairly in both directions, CNBC reported on 2 February 2026 that the same spreads fell sharply when the company set out its financing plan. That is a market forming a view and revising it, which is what markets do; it is also the only place in this chain where an independent party prices the risk rather than describing the opportunity.

Four places in the disclosures move before the newspapers do. The debt note, for maturity profile and the concentration of refinancing in any one year. The financing section of the cash flow statement, where the mix of issuance, repayment and equity is visible without commentary. The commitments and guarantees note, where support given to unconsolidated vehicles is described. And the interest note, for interest capitalised into assets under construction rather than charged against profit — a wholly ordinary treatment that nevertheless flatters reported earnings during exactly the period when construction in progress is at its largest.

The signature that would matter most is structural: a shift from public bonds to private credit and special-purpose vehicles, shorter tenors, and the appearance of the hardware itself as collateral. Lenders do not ask for security when they are relaxed.

## Five: a capability plateau

The fifth mode differs from the other five in that it would not reduce anyone's revenue in the quarter it happened. It would remove the reason to buy the next cluster.

Be precise about what a plateau is. It is not models ceasing to improve. It is the rate of improvement slowing enough that the marginal training run stops earning its cost, while deployment of existing capability continues for years. On that scenario, demand for inference could keep growing while demand for frontier training capacity flattens — and those two demands are served by overlapping but not identical hardware, bought by different budgets, on different schedules.

This is the mode that is hardest to see in accounts, and the only one whose primary evidence sits outside them. The most useful public measurement is METR's time horizon work, which estimates the length of software task that frontier systems can complete at a given success rate. Its published finding is that this horizon has been doubling roughly every seven months over 2019 to 2025, with a faster doubling of roughly four months over 2024 to 2025, and it released an updated estimate, version 1.1, in January 2026 using an expanded task suite — thirty-four per cent more tasks, and roughly double the number running eight hours or longer. What makes it useful is not that it is definitive; it is that it is independent, versioned, and published on a cadence by an organisation with nothing to sell.

Read it with two cautions, both of which METR itself notes. A single year's data supports a weaker inference than the full series. And a change in the measurement instrument moves the line without anything in the world having changed — which is why the version number matters, and why a plateau claimed on one data point is not a plateau.

Inside the accounts, three things would corroborate. Research and development as a share of revenue at the model builders, which is the training budget in its most public form. The mix of guidance between training and serving, which vendors describe qualitatively on earnings calls even where they do not disclose it. And the model layer's gross margin, which moves the other way: a plateau would be good for near-term margins, because serving a static model gets cheaper every year while the cost of chasing the next one disappears. Figures reported to the press in 2026 by PitchBook and Morningstar put Anthropic's compute cost at about seventy-one cents per dollar of revenue in the first quarter of 2026 with a projected fifty-six cents in the second, and gross margin around forty-four per cent; OpenAI's gross margin was reported at roughly thirty-nine per cent in the first quarter of 2026, against about thirty-three per cent a year earlier. Both companies are private, neither figure is audited, and chapter five's warnings apply in full.

The distinctive shape of this mode, then, is a model layer whose margins improve while the equipment order book stops growing. That combination is difficult to explain any other way.

## Six: a supply shock

The sixth mode is the one the industry has actually experienced most often, and the one most consistently misdescribed, because a supply shock does not hurt the companies most people watch. It helps them. It hurts everyone downstream of them.

The chain has several single points. Leading-edge logic is concentrated in one foundry. The lithography tools have one supplier. Advanced packaging is its own separate queue: TSMC's chief executive described advanced packaging capacity as extremely tight and sold out through 2026, per 2026 trade reporting, and Epoch AI's 2025 analysis concluded that packaging and high-bandwidth memory, rather than logic wafers, were the binding constraints on accelerator production in that period. Memory has three meaningful producers. High-bandwidth memory capacity was reported sold out through 2026 across major suppliers, and SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, per CNBC in August 2026.

What a shock looks like in numbers is on the record right now. TrendForce estimated that global DRAM contract prices rose ninety to ninety-five per cent quarter on quarter in the first quarter of 2026, followed by a projected fifty-eight to sixty-three per cent in the second and ten to fifteen per cent in the third, with the market deficit the widest since 2011. That is not a disaster for the three memory makers; it is the best trading environment they have had in years. It is a direct cost increase for everyone who buys memory, and the buyers include every server maker, every cloud operator and every consumer device manufacturer in the world.

Where it shows first, in order: the commitments note, for non-cancellable purchase obligations and their maturity profile; prepayments and deposits within other assets, where buyers pay in advance to secure allocation; and then, one or two quarters later, cost of revenue rising faster than volume in the accounts of the buyers. On the way up, that sequence is a scramble for supply. On the way down, the same disclosures become the exposure: a non-cancellable obligation to buy at a price that has since collapsed is how previous cycles turned a shortage into a write-down, and it is disclosed in the same note both times.

The book handles the geography of this risk elsewhere and factually. What belongs here is only the structural point: in a chain with this many single points, the shock does not need to be dramatic to be expensive, and it will appear as an obligation before it appears as an event.

## The six are not independent

Anyone treating this as six separate risks has already made the main mistake.

Modes one, four and five share a single underlying variable, which is expectation about future end revenue. If that expectation falls, demand for the next cluster falls, the credit that funds the marginal buyer reprices, and the case for the training budget weakens — not sequentially, but in the same season, because they are all functions of the same belief. Modes two and six share a different one: both delay revenue while the depreciation clock runs, which means both convert into mode three. And mode three, once it happens, changes the reported returns that justified modes one and four.

| Mode | Moves with | Because |
|---|---|---|
| End demand disappoints | Credit; capability plateau | All three price the same expectation about future revenue |
| Power as the hard cap | Supply shock; depreciation | Both delay revenue while the asset ages on schedule |
| Depreciation reset | Everything upstream of it | It is the accounting consequence of the other five |
| Credit tightens | End demand; equity valuations | Spreads follow the same forecast the multiple does |
| Capability plateau | End demand | The plateau is one reason demand would disappoint |
| Supply shock | Cost lines at every buyer | Concentration transmits the price directly |

The practical consequence is that correlated risks arrive together and feel, at the time, like a single catastrophic event rather than five ordinary ones. That is what a downturn is: not one thing going wrong, but several linked things going wrong at a speed that makes them look like one.

## What does not count as a warning

An equal and opposite discipline is required about false signals, because there will be many.

A share price fall is not a warning about the business. It is a change in what other people will pay, and it says more about sentiment than about cash flows. A single quarterly miss is not one either; quarters move on shipment timing, currency and one contract slipping a fortnight. Executive confidence about multi-year demand is the least informative statement in the sector, expressed identically at every peak and every trough. A famous investor's estimate of overstated profits is a forecast, not a fact, however well argued. Announced capacity that never becomes contracted capacity is normal rather than sinister — chapter fifty-six showed how routinely announced numbers shrink on contact with filings. And a record of any kind tells you about the past.

Two habits protect against most of this. Distinguish announced, contracted and delivered, and refuse to let a sentence slide between them. And watch revisions rather than levels: a guidance figure revised upward three times running says more than the figure, and so does one quietly revised down.

## Running the watch list

The whole list can be maintained in about an hour a quarter, using nothing that costs money.

| What to read | Where it lives | Cadence | The signal, not the level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supply purchase commitments and inventory at the largest accelerator vendor | Quarterly financial commentary and the commitments note | Quarterly | Commitments flattening while guidance stays confident |
| Remaining performance obligation, total and near-term | Revenue note and earnings call | Quarterly | Backlog lengthening as the near-term slice shrinks |
| Construction in progress as a share of gross property and equipment | Property and equipment note | Quarterly | Balance rising without converting into service |
| Useful lives, and any "assessed the useful lives" language | Accounting policies and changes-in-estimate note | Annual, sometimes quarterly | Any movement at all, in either direction |
| Debt maturity profile and financing cash flows | Debt note and cash flow statement | Quarterly | Shorter tenors, collateral, off-balance-sheet vehicles |
| Independent capability measurement and the government adoption survey | Published outside the industry | Regular | Doubling time lengthening; the intention-to-use gap widening |

Two supplements sit outside company reporting and are free: grid interconnection queue statistics and capacity auction results for the relevant markets, which tell you about dates rather than ambitions, and the memory contract price series, which tells you about everyone's cost base at once.

Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot carrying the date given and the source named, and a good many will have moved by the time it is read. That is not a defect in the chapter; it is the condition of the subject, and the remedy is always the same — go to the note, not to the commentary about the note. None of this is investment advice, and nothing here recommends any action in any security.

## The honest limit of a watch list

A list of six observable failure modes is better than a forecast, and it is still not enough, for a reason worth stating plainly.

The list is bounded by what is currently measurable. Every item was chosen because it has a public early signature, which quietly excludes anything whose signature is private or absent. A regulatory change, a security failure at scale, a legal finding about training data, a competitor commoditising something everyone assumed was scarce — none appears above, not because they are impossible but because this chapter can only point at what is already disclosed.

So the discipline matters more than the list. For any risk you can name, ask where it would first become visible in a document somebody is obliged to publish. If you cannot answer that, you do not have a risk you can monitor — you have an opinion, and opinions in this industry have been held with great conviction and poor timing at every previous turn.

The six above all have answers. A commitment line. A backlog conversion rate. A balance in construction. A sentence in an accounting policy. A maturity profile. A published measurement made by someone with nothing to sell. None requires access, expertise beyond what earlier chapters have supplied, or a view about the future.

The reader who checks them each quarter will not know what happens next. They will know it earlier than the people who are certain, which is the only advantage this subject reliably offers.
