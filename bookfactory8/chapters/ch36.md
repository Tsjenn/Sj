# OpenAI III: Restructuring and the Road to a Trillion

On 31 March 2026, OpenAI closed a financing of one hundred and twenty-two billion US dollars at a post-money valuation of eight hundred and fifty-two billion, the largest private funding round on record, as reported by Bloomberg and CNBC on the day it closed. The single biggest participant, reported at fifty billion dollars, was Amazon — a company whose cloud business is the direct competitor of OpenAI's oldest and largest backer, and whose principal model-layer investment until then had been OpenAI's closest rival.

Both halves of that sentence repay close reading, and this chapter reads them the way an accountant would: as a sequence of transactions, each with a price, a counterparty and a set of conditions, climbing a ladder that began with charitable donations and now ends one rung short of a trillion dollars. The previous two chapters covered the founding structure and the Microsoft relationship. This one brings the capital history current: the restructuring that cleared the way for the money, the round that followed it, what a valuation of that size does and does not tell you, why the identity of the cheque-writers matters more than the total, and what a public listing — much discussed, still unannounced — would finally force into daylight.

A caution before the numbers start. Almost every figure in this chapter describes a private company. None of it comes from audited accounts filed with a regulator; all of it comes from company announcements and press reporting, and it is cited that way deliberately. Where the sourcing is a journalist's account of a private document, that is what you are getting, and you should weigh it accordingly.

## The ladder so far

The structure OpenAI spent 2025 dismantling was described in detail two chapters ago, but the shape of it matters here, so briefly: the organisation was founded as a non-profit in December 2015; in 2019 it created a "capped-profit" subsidiary, OpenAI LP, in which investors' returns were limited — for the earliest investors, at one hundred times their money — with anything above the cap flowing back to the non-profit. Microsoft invested one billion dollars in July 2019 and became the preferred cloud partner, and its cumulative investment was reported by CNBC in 2023 at around thirteen billion dollars.

That structure did its job for a while. It raised real capital while keeping a charity in control. But each successive round strained it further, because conventional investors do not much like caps on their returns, mission clauses that outrank their interests, or a board that answers to neither of them. By late 2024 the strain was visible in the terms. OpenAI raised six point six billion dollars in October 2024 at a valuation of one hundred and fifty-seven billion, as recapped in CNBC's reporting of the round that followed it — and that next round is where the story of this chapter properly begins.

In March 2025, OpenAI closed forty billion dollars at a post-money valuation of three hundred billion, led by SoftBank, which was reported as contributing thirty billion of the total, with a further ten billion from a syndicate including Microsoft, Coatue, Altimeter and Thrive (CNBC and Bloomberg, March 2025). At the time it was the largest private technology financing on record, per PitchBook. And buried in it was the clause that drove everything that followed: SoftBank's commitment could be cut to as little as twenty billion dollars if OpenAI failed to restructure into a conventional for-profit entity by the end of the year, as CNBC reported when the round closed.

Read that clause the way a lender would read a covenant. It converted a governance question — should a charity control a frontier lab? — into a financing question with a date on it. Twenty billion dollars of committed capital now depended on corporate paperwork. Whatever the philosophical arguments for and against restructuring, and there were serious people on both sides, the accounting reality was that the old structure had acquired a carrying cost, and the cost was denominated in billions.

## October 2025: the restructuring

The paperwork arrived on 28 October 2025. OpenAI completed a recapitalisation in which the non-profit was renamed the OpenAI Foundation and the operating business became OpenAI Group PBC, a Delaware public benefit corporation, with the Foundation retaining control, as reported by CNBC and Fortune that day. The restructuring had the blessing of the attorneys general of Delaware and California, whose offices had spent months examining whether charitable assets were being fairly treated.

The ownership that emerged, per CNBC's report of the announcement, was this: the Foundation held roughly twenty-six per cent of the new company, an equity stake valued at about one hundred and thirty billion dollars; Microsoft held roughly twenty-seven per cent on an as-converted diluted basis, valued at about one hundred and thirty-five billion; and current and former employees and investors held the remaining forty-seven per cent.

Pause on the mechanics, because they are the interesting part. A public benefit corporation is an ordinary for-profit company with one modification: its directors are required to weigh a stated public mission alongside shareholder interests. It can issue ordinary equity without profit caps. That single change is what the restructuring was for. The capped-profit structure had made OpenAI's equity a peculiar instrument — a claim on profits up to a ceiling, junior to a mission — and peculiar instruments price badly. Conventional equity in a PBC, with a non-profit as the largest single shareholder rather than as a ceiling on everyone's returns, is a thing the world's institutional investors know how to buy.

The Microsoft terms changed in the same transaction, and the changes ran in both directions. Microsoft's stake was fixed and its access to OpenAI's technology extended through 2032, including any model deemed to be artificial general intelligence — with the notable provision, reported by CNBC, that such a declaration would be verified by an independent expert panel rather than simply asserted. Against that, Microsoft gave up its right of first refusal to be OpenAI's compute provider. And OpenAI contracted to purchase an incremental two hundred and fifty billion dollars of Azure services, as reported by GeekWire and Data Center Dynamics in October 2025 — a purchase commitment larger than the annual revenue of almost every company in this book. The relationship was revised again the following spring: CNBC reported in April 2026 that the companies had agreed to cap OpenAI's revenue-share payments to Microsoft.

For the accountant, the striking feature of the restructuring is that it created, in one stroke, one of the largest charitable endowments in existence — a foundation holding equity valued at about one hundred and thirty billion dollars — whose wealth is entirely a function of the private marks discussed later in this chapter. The Foundation's stake is worth what the last round says the company is worth. It has sold nothing, received no cash for the bulk of its holding, and its balance sheet rises and falls with a number that no market trades. Keep that thought; it returns shortly.

## March 2026: the largest round on record

Five months after the recapitalisation, the money it was designed to admit duly arrived. The March 2026 round — one hundred and twenty-two billion dollars at eight hundred and fifty-two billion post-money — was co-led by SoftBank, which was reported as investing thirty billion dollars alongside Andreessen Horowitz, MGX, TPG and T. Rowe Price, with Nvidia also reported at thirty billion, Microsoft participating again, and Amazon reported as investing fifty billion (Bloomberg and Forbes, March 2026). TechCrunch reported that roughly three billion dollars of the round came from retail investors through brokerage platforms — ordinary savers buying into a private company, an arrangement with its own chapter's worth of implications for who bears risk in this industry.

One reported term deserves particular attention. Of Amazon's fifty billion, thirty-five billion was reported by Bloomberg to be contingent on OpenAI either going public or reaching a defined milestone of artificial general intelligence. A conditional commitment of that size is not really an investment yet; it is an option, and the condition tells you what the option-writer wants. Amazon's money, on the reported terms, arrives in full only if OpenAI lists or declares the technology finished. Both triggers push in the same direction: toward a liquidity event.

The natural next question — the one this book keeps insisting you ask — is what a number like eight hundred and fifty-two billion actually measures.

## What eight hundred and fifty-two billion tells you, and what it does not

A post-money valuation is arithmetic, not appraisal. Investors paid a price per share for newly issued shares; multiply that price by every share in existence after the round, and you have the post-money figure. It is a real number derived from a real transaction, which is more than can be said for some numbers in this industry. But be precise about how thin the underlying transaction is relative to the headline. In March 2026, sophisticated parties committed one hundred and twenty-two billion dollars — roughly fourteen per cent of the implied total — at that price. The other eighty-six per cent of the valuation was not transacted. It is an extrapolation from the marginal price to every share nobody sold.

Chapter two made this argument about market capitalisation, and a private post-money is the same construction with three additional weaknesses. First, there is no continuous market. A listed company's price is tested every trading day by anyone with an opinion and a brokerage account, including pessimists; a private valuation is tested only when the company chooses to raise, from investors it chooses to admit, at a moment it chooses. The number is always set at a moment of strength. Second, the shares are not all the same. Late-stage investors typically buy preferred stock carrying liquidation preferences, anti-dilution provisions or other protections; the reported terms of Amazon's tranche, contingent on an IPO or a technological milestone, illustrate how far from plain ordinary equity a headline number can sit. Multiplying a protected share's price across unprotected shares overstates what the unprotected shares would fetch.

The mechanism is worth a worked example, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Take an illustrative private business with one hundred shares outstanding. An investor buys ten newly issued shares at ten dollars each, but negotiates a liquidation preference: if the company is ever sold cheaply, that investor is repaid in full before anyone else receives a cent. The headline says the company is now worth one thousand one hundred dollars — one hundred and ten shares at ten dollars. But the investor did not pay ten dollars for an ordinary share; it paid ten dollars for an ordinary share plus downside insurance. Strip the insurance out and the ordinary shares are worth something less than ten dollars each — how much less depends on terms nobody outside the deal can see. The headline valuation silently prices every share as though it carried the best protections in the room. Now scale the illustration up four orders of magnitude and remember that the terms of the March 2026 round are known only in the fragments that reached the press.

Third, there is no audited denominator. A public company's market capitalisation at least sits on top of filed, audited accounts; the revenue and margin figures beneath OpenAI's valuation are press reports of private documents.

What are those reports? OpenAI's chief financial officer, Sarah Friar, said the company's annualised revenue crossed twenty billion dollars in 2025, up from roughly six billion the year before, with CNBC first to report the figure in late 2025. Figures shared with investors and reported in 2026 by PitchBook and Morningstar put gross margin at roughly thirty-nine per cent in the first quarter of 2026, up from about thirty-three per cent a year earlier. Fortune reported in February 2026 that the company had forecast revenue exceeding two hundred and eighty billion dollars in 2030. The company has also been widely reported to be loss-making at the operating line, which it has not publicly disputed; the precise scale of the losses is not disclosed in any audited public document, and this book will not repeat the various leaked figures as though they were facts.

Divide the round's valuation by the reported annualised revenue and you get a little over forty — an arithmetic observation on two unaudited numbers, offered as arithmetic and nothing more. What the multiple tells you is not whether the price is right, which nobody knows, but what kind of claim the buyers were buying: not the business as it stood, but a forecast — and a forecast, moreover, that the buyers themselves are helping to finance into existence, which is where the identity of the buyers stops being a footnote.

|---|---|
| ...does tell you | Sophisticated investors paid a specific price for a minority of newly issued shares on that date, with 122 billion dollars of real commitments behind it |
| ...does not tell you | What all the shares would fetch if sold; what a continuous public market would pay; anything audited about revenue, margin or losses |

Every figure above is a snapshot. By the time you read this, the ladder will have another rung or a missing one; the current numbers will be wherever the latest reported round or listing has put them, and the method for reading them will not have changed.

## The rival cloud's fifty billion

Now the second half of this chapter's opening sentence. Amazon's reported fifty billion dollars is strategically remarkable for a reason that a diagram of the industry makes obvious: Amazon Web Services and Microsoft Azure are the two largest clouds, OpenAI was built on Azure, and Amazon had already committed up to thirty-three billion dollars to Anthropic, OpenAI's nearest rival, per 2026 reporting. In March 2026 the same company became the largest reported investor in the other side of the model duopoly.

The sequence that made it possible runs through the restructuring. When Microsoft's right of first refusal over OpenAI's compute lapsed in the October 2025 agreement, OpenAI was free to buy capacity anywhere — and within a week, on 3 November 2025, it signed a seven-year, thirty-eight billion dollar compute agreement with AWS covering large clusters of Nvidia's newest chips, as reported by CNBC, whose report noted Amazon's shares closing at a record on the news. The fifty billion dollar investment followed four months later.

Look at the money's circuit, because this is the pattern the whole industry now runs on. Amazon invests in OpenAI. OpenAI has contracted to spend thirty-eight billion dollars with Amazon's cloud over seven years. Amazon's cloud spends its revenue on chips from Nvidia — which was itself reported as investing thirty billion dollars in the same OpenAI round. A dollar can, in principle, travel from an investor's balance sheet into a model company, back to the investor as compute revenue, and down the stack to the chipmaker, which recycles some of it into the next model-company round. Nothing in that circuit is improper, and nothing here alleges that it is: every leg is a real transaction at a disclosed or reported price. But an accountant reading it should reach for the concept the profession calls vendor financing — a supplier funding its own customer's purchases — and apply the standard tests. How much of the customer's spending is funded by the supplier's own capital? Would the revenue exist without the investment? At what point does reported growth become, in part, the supplier's money describing a circle?

The vigilant reader has one main place to look: the investing companies' own accounts. A minority stake in a private company generally sits on the holder's balance sheet either at cost, at fair value with changes flowing through earnings, or under the equity method, where the holder books its share of the investee's profit or loss. Which treatment applies depends on the size of the stake and the influence it confers, and the choice matters enormously: under fair-value treatment, a write-up of a private mark becomes reported profit without a dollar of cash moving; under the equity method, an investee's losses flow straight into the holder's earnings line. The accounting policy note and the earnings-release reconciliations are where the treatment is disclosed, and they have become required reading for anyone analysing Big Tech's results.

The distortion is now large enough to reach the earnings of the investors themselves. CNBC reported in August 2026 that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them — paper gains and losses on private marks flowing through the income statements of some of the world's largest listed companies. The private valuations of two unlisted labs, set in negotiated rounds a few times a year, now move the reported profits of public companies whose own shares trade every day. A reader of Microsoft's or Amazon's accounts must now form a view on OpenAI's valuation to understand the earnings line — and the only public evidence for that valuation is the round described above, in which Microsoft and Amazon were themselves participants.

Why do it? Because at the model layer, capital is the product. A cloud that wants frontier workloads must be where the frontier models run, and the surest way to be there is to arrive holding a cheque. Amazon's position after March 2026 — up to thirty-three billion committed to Anthropic, fifty billion reported into OpenAI, a thirty-eight billion dollar compute contract in hand — is not a bet on either lab. It is a bet that the model layer will spend most of what it raises one floor down, on compute, and that whoever finances the layer gets first claim on the spending. Google's reported commitments to Anthropic — up to forty-three billion dollars, including ten billion in April 2026, per 2026 reporting — follow the identical logic. Even SoftBank's part of the story obeys it: the firm sold its entire remaining Nvidia stake in November 2025 for five point eight three billion dollars, as reported by CNBC, explicitly to fund its OpenAI and Stargate commitments — capital rotating out of the silicon layer's equity and into the model layer's rounds, on its way, in large part, back down to silicon.

## What an IPO would force into daylight

Within a day of the October 2025 restructuring, Reuters reported — and Bloomberg relayed on 29 October 2025 — that OpenAI had begun laying groundwork for a public listing that advisers had discussed at valuations of up to one trillion dollars, raising at least sixty billion, possibly filing as soon as late 2026, more likely 2027. The company's response was carefully worded: an IPO was not its focus and no date had been set. When the March 2026 round closed, CNBC framed its report around the anticipation of a listing, and the reported structure of Amazon's tranche — thirty-five billion contingent on an IPO or a technological milestone — shows that at least one investor negotiated with a listing squarely in mind. As this book went to press, no listing had been announced; whether one has happened since is a fact the reader can check faster than any book can.

This book takes no view on whether anyone should want, buy or avoid such a listing; it is not investment advice, here or anywhere. What it can do is set out, from the accountant's side of the desk, exactly what a listing would force into the open — because the gap between what is known about OpenAI today and what a prospectus must contain is the best available measure of how much of this chapter rests on reporting rather than accounts.

A company listing in the United States must publish audited financial statements, and everything in this chapter changes character the moment that happens. Reported revenue becomes recognised revenue, with the recognition policy stated: how the twenty-dollar subscriptions, the metered API and the large enterprise contracts are each booked, and over what period. Reported gross margin becomes a stated cost of revenue, and the reader finally learns where the compute sits — how much of it is bought, how much prepaid, how the two hundred and fifty billion dollar Azure commitment and the thirty-eight billion dollar AWS commitment are carried, and what happens to margin as they are drawn down. Purchase commitments of that scale must be disclosed and scheduled by year. The losses stop being a leak and become a stated number with a trend.

Then come the disclosures this industry needs most. Related-party transactions: Microsoft is simultaneously a shareholder of roughly twenty-seven per cent, a supplier under a two hundred and fifty billion dollar contract, a revenue-share counterparty and a competitor; Amazon and Nvidia are reported investors and, respectively, a contracted supplier and the dominant supplier of the hardware everything runs on. A prospectus must map those relationships and price them. Customer and supplier concentration: how much revenue depends on how few counterparties, and how much cost on fewer still. Governance: the Foundation's control of a listed OpenAI Group PBC would put a non-profit's mission clause and a public float's interests into one document, and the risk-factor section would have to explain who wins when they conflict — including the mechanics of the independent panel that would verify a declaration of artificial general intelligence, a clause with direct financial consequences under the reported Amazon terms.

And one number would simply cease to exist. The day OpenAI lists, the post-money valuation — the number this chapter has been circling — is replaced by a market capitalisation, marked every trading day by strangers, including the sceptical. Chapter two set out why that number has problems of its own. But it is tested, continuously, against audited accounts. The eight hundred and fifty-two billion is tested only at the next round, by participants.

## Down the stack, again

Close the chapter where the book always closes: follow the money down. Of the one hundred and twenty-two billion dollars raised in March 2026, the announcement's own framing — accelerating the next phase of AI — and the contracts already signed tell you the destination. Thirty-eight billion is committed to one cloud over seven years; two hundred and fifty billion, incrementally, to another; the chips those clouds buy come overwhelmingly from a supplier that was itself a reported thirty billion dollar participant in the round. The model layer, for all its valuation, remains what chapter one said the accounts show it to be: a toll plaza where more than half of each revenue dollar, on the reported margins, passes straight through to the floors below.

That is the pattern to hold onto as the next chapters turn to the rival that Amazon backed first. Anthropic reached a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026 — briefly the most valuable private company in the world, per CNBC — by a different route, with different investors and a different commercial posture, but on the same ladder, subject to the same tests. Two private companies, close to a trillion dollars each, neither with a published audited account between them. The tools in this chapter — read the terms, not the headline; trace the circuit of the money; ask what a prospectus would add — are the ones the reader will need again immediately.
