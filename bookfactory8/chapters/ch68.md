# Taiwan

An accelerator installed in a data centre in Virginia has already passed through a short list of buildings that no network diagram shows. Its logic die was printed on a three-hundred-millimetre wafer in a fab in Taiwan, tested, cut, and then joined to stacks of high-bandwidth memory on an interposer in an advanced packaging plant elsewhere on the same island, before being mounted on a board, built into a rack and shipped. As at early 2026, roughly ninety-two per cent of the world's most advanced logic — five nanometres and below — was manufactured in Taiwan, per 2026 industry analysis of advanced-node capacity.

That single number is the reason this chapter exists, and it is the reason this chapter is written more carefully than any other in the book.

## The rule for this chapter

This chapter deals in three things only: what is manufactured in Taiwan, what has actually been built elsewhere, and how long each of those projects has taken. Dates and figures, with sources, and the arithmetic that follows from them.

It contains no scenarios, no assessment of political risk, no discussion of what any government might or might not do, and no estimate of any probability. Those questions are outside the competence of an accountant reading published accounts, and the honest position is to say so rather than to dress a guess in the vocabulary of analysis. Nothing here is investment advice; there is no price target and no recommendation.

What can be established from the public record is narrower and more useful than a forecast. It is the shape of a dependency: which step of the chain sits where, how substitutable each step is, what the substitutes cost, and — the part most often skipped — how many years each substitute has taken to reach production. A reader who finishes this chapter should be able to answer a factual question that is routinely answered with adjectives: how long would it take, on the evidence of projects already under way, to build a genuine alternative to what exists there now.

## What is actually made there

The phrase "chips are made in Taiwan" is too coarse to be useful. Several distinct steps are involved, they are concentrated to very different degrees, and only some of them are concentrated in Taiwan at all.

### Leading-edge logic

The first and most concentrated step is the printing of advanced logic wafers.

TSMC took 72.3 per cent of top-ten foundry revenue in the first quarter of 2026, per TrendForce, up from 70.4 per cent in the preceding quarter. Its own node mix, from its second-quarter 2026 results announced on 16 July 2026, was three-nanometre at thirty per cent of wafer revenue, five-nanometre at thirty-three per cent, seven-nanometre at eleven per cent and the newly ramping two-nanometre at three per cent. High-performance computing — the platform category that contains AI accelerators — was sixty-six per cent of revenue in that quarter.

The newest node is the one that matters for this chapter, because it shows where new leading-edge capacity is being installed rather than where old capacity happens to sit. TSMC's two-nanometre process entered mass production from the second half of 2025 at its Baoshan facility in the Hsinchu Science Park and its Kaohsiung facility, per trade reporting through 2025 and 2026, with combined monthly capacity variously estimated at forty-five to fifty thousand wafers by the end of 2025 and rising towards a hundred thousand or more by the end of 2026. Those capacity numbers are trade estimates, they differ substantially between sources, and the company does not publish capacity by node. The location is not in dispute. Both plants are in Taiwan.

So is the research and development that produces the next node after this one. That is a structural fact rather than a preference: process development requires a development fab, a large body of process engineers and the ability to move a recipe from development to production quickly, and all three sit where they have accumulated.

### Advanced packaging

The second step became a constraint on the industry only recently, and it is concentrated differently.

An accelerator is not finished when the logic wafer is. It must be assembled with stacked memory onto an interposer, and that advanced packaging step has its own tools, its own buildings and its own queue. TSMC's management described its advanced packaging capacity as extremely tight and sold out through 2026, per 2026 trade reporting of its earnings calls — management's own words, which is why they are quoted rather than estimated. Third-party estimates put the company's CoWoS capacity at roughly thirty-five thousand wafers a month at the end of 2024, about seventy-five thousand at the end of 2025 and somewhere between a hundred and twenty-five and a hundred and forty thousand by the end of 2026, with TrendForce reporting in June 2026 that the supply-demand gap was expected to narrow from about twenty per cent to about ten per cent over the year. Treat all of those as trade estimates that vary widely by source.

The expansion is being built largely where the existing capacity is. TSMC's AP7 site at Chiayi in Taiwan is planned across multiple phases carrying its newest packaging technologies, with equipment installation under way and output from phases beginning in 2026 and 2027, per TrendForce reporting in December 2025 and January 2026. In other words, the industry's answer to a packaging shortage caused by concentration was, in the first instance, more capacity in the same place — because that is where it could be built fastest.

Outsourced assembly and test, the older and larger business that sits beside this, is also concentrated in Taiwanese hands, though less extremely. On TrendForce's 2024 rankings, reported by Evertiq in May 2025, the top ten outsourced assembly and test companies had combined revenue of 41.56 billion US dollars, of which ASE Technology Holding took 18.54 billion, roughly forty-five per cent of the top-ten total, with Amkor second at 6.32 billion.

### The rest of the cluster

Two further layers of the chain are Taiwanese by company rather than wholly by geography, and the distinction matters.

Taiwan's server original design manufacturers assemble a large share of the world's AI servers and racks. Foxconn, Wistron and Quanta each recorded revenue above one trillion New Taiwan dollars in 2025 on the strength of AI server demand, per Digitimes reporting in January 2026, and trade estimates in 2026 put Foxconn at roughly forty per cent of global AI server rack assembly. Those share figures are trade estimates rather than disclosures and should be read as such.

But rack assembly is the step that has moved most readily. These are Taiwanese companies operating plants in Mexico, the United States, Vietnam, Thailand and elsewhere, and the work is labour-intensive final assembly rather than capital-intensive fabrication. A reader who conflates "Taiwanese company" with "made in Taiwan" will overstate the geographic concentration at this end of the chain and understate it at the other.

### What is not made there

The chain's other chokepoints sit elsewhere, and naming them keeps the picture honest.

Extreme ultraviolet lithography systems come from one company in the Netherlands, with ASML at a hundred per cent of EUV on 2026 market commentary. High-bandwidth memory comes from three companies headquartered in South Korea and the United States, with the 2026 HBM market estimated at about 54.6 billion US dollars, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, SK Hynix holding the largest share, and capacity reported sold out through 2026 across major suppliers, per 2026 industry analysis. Electronic design automation software is dominated by two American firms. Silicon wafers themselves come largely from Japanese suppliers.

| Step | Where it is concentrated | How concentrated, and the source |
|---|---|---|
| Design software | United States | Two firms around 85 per cent of the EDA market, on 2026 market commentary |
| Lithography tools | Netherlands | ASML at 100 per cent of EUV, 2026 market commentary |
| Silicon wafers | Japan, largely | A handful of suppliers |
| Leading-edge logic manufacture | Taiwan | About 92 per cent of 5 nm and below, early 2026 industry analysis |
| High-bandwidth memory | South Korea, United States | Three suppliers; SK Hynix largest share of a 54.6 bn dollar 2026 market |
| Advanced packaging at volume | Taiwan | Described by management as sold out through 2026; capacity figures are trade estimates |
| Server and rack assembly | Taiwanese firms, plants worldwide | Trade estimates put Foxconn near 40 per cent of AI rack assembly |

The point of the table is not that Taiwan is the only chokepoint. It is that the chain has several, they sit in different jurisdictions, and the one in Taiwan is distinguished by covering two consecutive steps — logic and packaging — that between them determine how many accelerators exist.

## Why it cannot be moved quickly

The instinct of anyone reading the concentration figure for the first time is to ask why the capacity is not simply built somewhere else. It is being built somewhere else, at enormous cost, and the next two sections describe what that has produced. First it is worth being precise about what makes the transfer slow, because the reasons are physical and financial rather than a matter of will.

### Four things a fab needs

A leading-edge fab requires four inputs, and they arrive on different clocks.

The building is the fastest. It is very large, very flat, extraordinarily clean and vibration-controlled, and it takes a construction programme of a couple of years — long, but tractable, and the part that money and attention reliably accelerate.

The tools are the second. A leading-edge fab's equipment list runs to hundreds of machines from dozens of suppliers, the most advanced of which are made by one company at a rate that is itself constrained. Tools must be delivered, installed, connected to ultrapure water, gases, chemicals and power, and then qualified individually and in sequence.

The process is the third, and it is the part outsiders underestimate. A process node is not a specification that can be posted to a new site. It is several thousand steps whose parameters were tuned against the specific behaviour of specific tools, and transferring it means re-tuning until the new site produces the same result. Yield is the measurable output of that work.

The people are the fourth, and they are the slowest. The section below deals with them separately.

Around all four sits a supplier ecosystem: chemicals, gases, photomasks, substrates, parts machining, tool servicing engineers who can be on site within the hour. A fab in isolation is a building full of equipment waiting for a delivery. That density took decades to accumulate where it exists, and replicating it is a separate project from replicating the fab.

### The qualification problem

There is a further delay that has nothing to do with construction. Even where an alternative site exists and works, a customer cannot simply switch to it.

A chip design is qualified for a specific process at a specific fab. Moving it to another site means re-qualifying: new mask sets where the process differs, test silicon, characterisation, reliability testing, and requalification by the customer's own customers where the part goes into regulated or safety-relevant equipment. That is a project measured in quarters at best, and it puts a product schedule at risk while it runs. The lag is the reason concentration persists even after alternatives appear on paper.

### The cost of the same wafer somewhere else

Diversification is bought with margin, and the accounts show the price.

TSMC has said on its 2026 earnings calls that the ramp of overseas fabs dilutes consolidated gross margin by two to three percentage points in 2026, widening to three to four points as those sites reach full-scale production. That is a disclosed figure from the company, and it is the cleanest single measure available of what geographic diversification costs the party paying for it.

Estimates of the underlying wafer cost difference vary by who is doing the estimating. TechInsights analysis reported in 2026 put the production cost of a wafer in Arizona just under ten per cent higher than in Taiwan. AMD's chief executive, reported in 2026, described chips from the Arizona fab as costing five to twenty per cent more. Morris Chang said in March 2023 that Arizona might be "maybe double the cost", an earlier and much larger figure from the person who founded the company. The three are not reconcilable because they measure different things — modelled production cost with the analyst's own capital and subsidy assumptions, prices actually paid by a customer, and a founder's characterisation — and the honest reading is the direction rather than the decimal. The same wafer costs more when it is made somewhere else, and someone in the chain pays that difference.

## The workforce

Of the four inputs, the one that cannot be ordered is people.

TSMC and its subsidiaries employed 90,557 people at 31 December 2025, an increase of 6,732 on the year, per the company's 2025 annual report. That is the scale of the operating organisation behind the concentration figure, and it is worth holding in mind when reading announcements about new sites, because a new fab needs a proportionate share of comparable people at the same time as the existing fabs need to keep running.

The evidence from the largest transfer under way is specific. TSMC's Arizona site employed more than three thousand people in 2026, per 2026 trade reporting, having employed roughly 2,200 in late 2024, of whom about half had come from Taiwan, per Tom's Hardware and other reporting at the end of 2024. The company has said it expects the American share to rise as the site builds out. Engineers have moved in both directions: 2026 reporting described Arizona staff being sent to Taiwan in batches for training on the more advanced processes designated for the newer fabs.

Japan's Rapidus shows the same pattern at the start of the curve rather than the middle. More than a hundred and fifty engineers were sent to Albany, New York, in 2023 and 2024 to train on next-generation process technology, of whom about eighty had returned to Chitose in Hokkaido to work on process tuning, per the company and 2025 trade reporting. That is what building a process workforce from a standing start looks like: a two-year training programme abroad for a group smaller than a single shift at a large fab.

The aggregate position in the country doing the most building is documented. The Semiconductor Industry Association and Oxford Economics, in their 2023 study, put the United States semiconductor workforce at roughly 345,000 in 2023, projected it to reach about 460,000 by the end of the decade, and estimated a shortfall of nearly 67,000 workers by 2030 on then-current graduation rates — of which about thirty-nine per cent were technicians, forty-one per cent engineers and twenty per cent computer scientists. The Taipei Times reported in July 2026 that worker shortages were endangering United States chip production plans. That study is now several years old and its projections have not been re-run here; it is cited for the shape of the gap rather than the precision of the number.

Taiwan has its own version of the constraint. Digitimes reported in October 2025 that declining birth rates and an ageing population were producing structural workforce pressure in the industry, and Taiwan's Industrial Development Administration launched a semiconductor training programme in 2026 aimed at attracting engineers from South-East Asia. Both jurisdictions are short of the same people. Only one of them currently has the fabs running.

### What the pattern shows

Read down the status column and three things stand out.

The first is that the most advanced process in volume production has, so far, stayed in Taiwan, and so has process research and development. Arizona's newer fabs are designated for two nanometres and below, but they are being built while that node is already ramping in Hsinchu and Kaohsiung. Diversifying capacity and diversifying the leading edge are two different projects, and only the first is well advanced.

The second is that the numbers are moving, slowly, in the direction the projects imply. TrendForce put Taiwan at about forty-eight per cent of global foundry capacity in 2022 and projected a decline to about forty-one per cent by 2027. On advanced processes specifically — sixteen and fourteen nanometres and below — TrendForce's 2024 analysis put Taiwan at sixty-six per cent, with South Korea at eleven per cent, the United States at ten and China at nine, and projected Taiwan at fifty-five per cent and South Korea at eight by 2027. Those are projections published by a research firm, not outturns, and they were made before several of the schedule changes in the table above. A shift of roughly ten percentage points over five years, if it happens, is real and is also not a change of regime.

The third is that packaging has begun to follow logic, but later. Amkor's Arizona campus, working with TSMC, is the first large advanced packaging investment outside Asia at this scale, and on its own announced schedule it produces nothing until early 2028 — three and a half years after the shortage it addresses became the industry's binding constraint.

## How long a genuine alternative takes

Now the arithmetic, which is the part of this subject where facts are most often replaced by impressions.

### The record

TSMC announced its Phoenix project in May 2020 as a twelve-billion-dollar fab. It broke ground in April 2021 and reached volume production on an N4 process in the fourth quarter of 2024. That is about three and a half years from ground to production, and roughly four and a half from announcement to production, on a project with a customer queue, government support and effectively unconstrained funding behind it. The second fab on the same site was structurally complete in April 2026 with commercial production expected in the second half of 2027 — a second building on a site that already had the roads, the water, the power and the people, and it still lands about seven years after the original announcement.

Intel's Ohio project was announced in January 2022 with production targeted for the end of 2025. The target moved to 2027–28, and then on 28 February 2025 the company said the first fab would begin operations in 2030 or 2031 and the second in 2032, per CNBC's report of that announcement. Whatever the reasons, the observable fact is a first-fab schedule that moved out by five or more years while the project was under way.

Rapidus was founded in 2022, installed a volume-production-ready EUV system in December 2024, started a pilot line in April 2025 and targets mass production in 2027 — five years from founding to first volume output, at an initial six thousand wafers a month rising towards twenty-five thousand, per its own plan. For scale, TSMC's two-nanometre capacity alone was estimated by the trade press at forty-five to fifty thousand wafers a month by the end of 2025.

Amkor's Peoria campus: ground broken October 2025, first facility complete mid-2027, production early 2028. Dresden: structural completion late 2025, production targeted late 2027. Kumamoto's second fab: construction from October 2025, operation by end-2027.

The distribution is tight. Every one of these projects, regardless of country, company or subsidy, takes between four and seven years from decision to output, and the ones that slip, slip by years rather than quarters.

### The moving target

Now put that beside the pace of the thing being replicated. The leading edge has advanced roughly one full node generation every two years or so, and the node in volume production in Taiwan in 2026 is two nanometres, with research and development already directed at what follows it.

Take an illustrative programme, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. A decision is taken in year zero to replicate the leading edge as it stands at that moment. Site selection, permitting and design take two years. Construction takes two more. Tool installation and qualification take one. Yield learning to production standard takes one. First meaningful output arrives in year six. By then the leading edge has moved on three node generations, and the new site is producing what was state of the art when the decision was taken.

That is not an argument that diversification is pointless. A site producing a node that is three generations old is producing chips that most of the world's electronics actually use, and second-source capacity has value that does not depend on being at the frontier. It is an argument about what the word "alternative" means. Building capacity is a four-to-seven-year project. Building capacity that is at the frontier when it opens requires starting from a position already close to the frontier, which is why the firms making credible attempts are the two or three that were already there.

And a fab is the smaller half of the problem. The illustrative programme above assumes tools arrive on schedule from a supplier base that is itself concentrated, that packaging capacity exists to finish what the fab starts, that substrates, chemicals and photomasks are available locally, and that several thousand qualified people are hired without taking them from somewhere they were already needed. Each of those is its own programme, on its own clock, and the last one is the slowest.

## Where this shows up in the accounts

The accountant's contribution to this subject is not a view. It is knowing which disclosures already contain the answer, so that a reader is not dependent on commentary.

| What you want to know | Where it is disclosed | What to read |
|---|---|---|
| How much a company depends on one manufacturer | Risk factors and supplier discussion in the annual report of any fabless designer | Named dependence on a single foundry, stated by the company itself |
| What geographic diversification costs | TSMC's quarterly earnings calls | The stated gross margin dilution from overseas fabs — two to three points in 2026, widening to three to four |
| Whether new capacity is at the frontier | Foundry quarterly results, revenue by technology | The share of revenue from the newest node, and where the company says that node is made |
| How fast a project is actually moving | Company announcements against their own prior announcements | Changes in stated production dates; the revision is the information, not the level |
| How much of the cost is public money | Government grant and subsidy disclosures, and the notes on government assistance | Grants recognised, and whether they reduce the carrying amount of assets or are taken to income |
| Whether packaging is still the constraint | Earnings calls and third-party capacity estimates | Management's own language about capacity being tight or sold out, dated |

Two habits make those disclosures usable. Date every figure and name its source, because almost everything in this chapter is a snapshot that will have moved. And keep the three states of a commitment separate: announced, contracted, delivered. The table of projects above is full of announcements. Some are contracted. Only a few have delivered wafers, and those are the only ones that have changed the concentration figure at all.

## What is knowable

Strip out everything that cannot be established from the record and this is what remains.

A single island manufactures roughly ninety-two per cent of the world's most advanced logic, per early-2026 industry analysis, and hosts the advanced packaging capacity that determines how many finished accelerators exist, described by the company that owns most of it as sold out through 2026. The largest firm involved employed 90,557 people at the end of 2025. Alternatives are being built on four continents with well over three hundred billion dollars of announced investment and tens of billions of public money behind them. On the evidence of every such project now under way, each takes four to seven years from decision to output, and the schedules move outward more often than inward. Research-firm projections put Taiwan's share of advanced-process capacity falling from about sixty-six per cent in 2024 to about fifty-five per cent in 2027 — a real shift, and not a change in the fundamental position.

Everything beyond that is speculation, and this chapter declines to supply it. What it supplies instead is the timetable. Anyone who wants to know whether the concentration is easing can check it against the dates in the table above as they arrive: whether the second Arizona fab produces commercially in the second half of 2027, whether Dresden and Kumamoto reach production by the end of that year, whether Rapidus ships in volume in 2027, whether Amkor's packaging campus opens in early 2028, and whether the newest node in volume production is still being made in only one place.

Those are five checkable events with dates already published by the parties responsible for them. They will answer the question better than any opinion, including this book's.
