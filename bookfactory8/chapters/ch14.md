# Nvidia III: To the Largest Company on Earth

On 8 August 2022, Nvidia announced its second-quarter results early. Companies do that for one reason: the number is far enough from what the market has been told to expect that waiting three weeks would be worse than the embarrassment of going first. Revenue for the quarter ended 31 July 2022 came in at 6.70 billion US dollars, up three per cent on the year before and down nineteen per cent on the previous quarter, per the preliminary announcement of 8 August 2022 and the full results announcement later that month.

Three and a half years later, on 25 February 2026, the same company reported annual revenue of 215.9 billion dollars and net income of 120.1 billion dollars for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026. In August 2026 it was the most valuable listed company in the world.

This chapter covers the distance between those two announcements. It starts with the bad year, because the bad year is where the mechanics are visible. When everything is going up, the accounts tell you very little that the share price has not already told you. When a business writes down its inventory, you find out what it actually believed about demand.

## The year the demand turned out not to be real

### What was actually being bought

Nvidia sells graphics processors. For most of its history the buyers were people who wanted to play games, and the demand curve behaved the way consumer demand behaves: seasonal, price-sensitive, broadly predictable.

From roughly 2017 the picture became muddier. The same parallel arithmetic that makes a GPU good at rendering also made it good at the proof-of-work computation that mined certain cryptocurrencies. Those buyers did not look like gamers. They bought in volume, they bought on the economics of electricity and token prices, and they disappeared the moment those economics turned. Critically, they mostly bought the same retail gaming cards through the same channel, which meant that in the accounts their money arrived labelled as Gaming revenue.

Nvidia's difficulty with this is a matter of public record. On 6 May 2022 the company agreed to pay a 5.5 million dollar civil penalty and to cease and desist from violating certain federal securities provisions, without admitting or denying the findings, to settle a regulator's charge that during two consecutive quarters of its fiscal year 2018 it had failed to disclose that cryptomining was a significant element of the material revenue growth in its gaming GPU business. The settlement was reported by CNN Business and Al Jazeera on 6 May 2022. The point for a reader of accounts is not the penalty, which was trivial for a company of that size. The point is the underlying fact: a material driver of a reported revenue line was not visible in that line. If the disclosure does not separate two kinds of demand, you cannot tell from the outside which one is going away.

In 2022 one of them went away.

### The collapse, quarter by quarter

The crypto market fell through the first half of 2022. In September 2022 the Ethereum network completed its move from proof of work to proof of stake, which removed at a stroke the largest single source of GPU-based mining demand. Second-hand cards flooded the market. New cards stopped selling.

The line items follow the story exactly.

For the second quarter of fiscal 2023, the quarter ended 31 July 2022, Gaming revenue was 2.04 billion dollars, down thirty-three per cent year on year. OEM and other revenue, the line that carried the dedicated mining products, fell sixty-six per cent, as reported by Benzinga in August 2022. Data Centre revenue in the same quarter was 3.81 billion dollars, up sixty-one per cent from 2.37 billion a year earlier. By the third quarter, reported in November 2022, Gaming revenue had fallen fifty-one per cent year on year to 1.57 billion dollars.

And the second quarter carried a charge: approximately 1.32 billion dollars, primarily for inventory and related reserves, taken on the basis of revised expectations of future demand, per the company's second-quarter fiscal 2023 results announcement of August 2022.

That sentence is the most instructive one in the whole four-year sequence, and it deserves unpacking.

### What an inventory charge actually is

Inventory sits on the balance sheet at the lower of what it cost and what it can be sold for, net of the costs of selling it. That second measure — net realisable value — is not a fact. It is an estimate about the future, made in the present, by the people who run the business.

While demand is strong, the two measures rarely diverge and nobody thinks about it. When demand falls away, cost stays where it is and net realisable value drops, and the difference has to be recognised immediately. It goes through cost of revenue. Gross margin absorbs it in the quarter it is recognised, not spread over the quarters in which the goods are eventually sold.

There is usually a second component, and Nvidia's disclosure named it: related reserves and purchase obligations. A fabless chip company commits to wafer starts, substrates, memory and assembly capacity months or years ahead. Those commitments are contractual. When the demand behind them evaporates, the company is left owing money for goods it no longer wants, and the expected loss on those commitments is recognised too, even though the goods do not yet exist.

Take an illustrative chip designer, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It holds 3 billion dollars of finished goods that cost 3 billion to make, and has committed to a further 2 billion of wafer purchases. Demand halves. Management now believes the finished goods will fetch 2.2 billion and that 600 million of the committed wafers will never be needed at their contracted price. The charge is 800 million on inventory plus 600 million on commitments: 1.4 billion, all of it in this quarter's cost of revenue, and none of it reversible under the accounting rules even if demand returns.

Which introduces the asymmetry that every reader of a cyclical manufacturer's accounts should hold onto. A write-down cannot be reversed to profit. But the written-down goods remain on the shelf, now carried at a much lower cost. If they later sell — and after a demand shock they very often do, one or two quarters later, at prices better than the panic assumption — they sell at the reduced carrying value. The margin on those sales looks extraordinary, because the cost was already expensed in an earlier period.

So a big inventory charge does two things at once: it makes the current quarter look worse than the business is, and it makes some later quarter look better than the business is. Neither distortion is improper. Both are visible only if you read the charge disclosure, and most readers do not.

What a reader should watch, in any chip company in any cycle:

| Signal | Where it is | What a deterioration looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory days | Balance sheet against cost of revenue | Rising while revenue growth slows |
| Purchase commitments | Commitments and contingencies note | Growing faster than revenue guidance |
| Charge disclosure | Results announcement, cost of revenue commentary | Any charge "based on revised expectations of demand" |
| Gross margin | Income statement | A sudden drop, then a sudden recovery above trend |

### And then the government

On 26 August 2022 the US government informed Nvidia that a licence would be required, effective immediately, for any future export to China, Hong Kong and Russia of its A100 and forthcoming H100 processors, and of systems incorporating them. The company disclosed that up to 400 million dollars of expected third-quarter sales to China were affected. Days later, on 31 August 2022, authorisations were granted allowing continued H100 development work, support for existing US customers of A100 until 1 March 2023, and order fulfilment through the company's Hong Kong facility until 1 September 2023. The filings were reported at the time by Tom's Hardware, HPCwire and TechTarget in September 2022.

Note the date. The first serious export restriction on Nvidia's data centre products arrived in the same quarter as the inventory charge, and the same year as the crypto collapse. That is worth remembering when 2022 is described, retrospectively, as merely a gaming problem.

Source: the company's fourth-quarter and fiscal 2023 results announcement, 22 February 2023. Non-GAAP net income for the year was 8.36 billion dollars, down twenty-six per cent from 11.25 billion, per the same announcement — a good example of why the gap between the two measures matters, and why this book quotes GAAP first.

Over calendar 2022 the shares fell roughly fifty per cent, per Motley Fool coverage in January 2023.

There is no way to read that table as a good year. Revenue flat, profit more than halved, the consumer business shrinking by a quarter, the balance sheet cleaned out by a charge, and the export regime tightening. Any account of this company that skips 2022 is not an account of the company.

But look at the composition. Data Centre revenue was already larger than Gaming for the full year — 15.01 billion against 9.07 billion — and it grew forty-one per cent in the year the rest of the business fell apart. The crossover happened during the worst year, not the best one. It was simply invisible at the time, because the headline was flat revenue and halved profit.

## The quarter that changed the register

By early 2023 a different kind of customer was ordering. Large language models had gone from a research curiosity to something with a consumer product attached, and the training runs behind them consumed accelerators in quantities that had no precedent in the gaming channel.

The first time that showed up in a way nobody could argue with was 24 May 2023, when Nvidia reported its first quarter of fiscal 2024 and guided to approximately 11 billion dollars of revenue for the following quarter, against an analyst consensus of about 7.2 billion. The shares rose twenty-four per cent the next day, 25 May 2023, per CNBC's reporting at the time.

Guidance, not results, did that. It is worth being precise about why. A quarterly result is a measurement of the past. Guidance is management telling you what it has already seen in the order book and in its supply arrangements. When a company raises its own next-quarter number by more than fifty per cent above what the market expected, it is not expressing optimism. It is reporting, in the only forward-looking form available to it, that something has changed in the demand it can already observe.

On 30 May 2023 Nvidia traded through a market capitalisation of one trillion dollars for the first time, becoming the first US chipmaker to do so, before giving back some of the gain during the session, as reported by CNBC and Forbes on 30 May 2023.

The results then arrived and exceeded the guidance. Second-quarter fiscal 2024 revenue was 13.5 billion dollars, up 101 per cent year on year, with net income of 6.2 billion dollars, per contemporaneous coverage of the August 2023 announcement. Over calendar 2023 the shares rose 239 per cent, per Motley Fool coverage carried by Yahoo Finance.

The full fiscal year settled the argument about what kind of company this now was:

| Fiscal 2024 (year ended 28 January 2024) | Amount | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 60.9 billion USD | up 126 per cent |
| Data Centre revenue | 47.5 billion USD | up 217 per cent |
| GAAP net income | 29.76 billion USD | — |
| GAAP diluted earnings per share | 11.93 USD | up 586 per cent |
| Q4 revenue (quarter ended 28 January 2024) | 22.1 billion USD | up 265 per cent |

Source: the company's fourth-quarter and fiscal 2024 results announcement, February 2024.

Two years earlier this business had reported 26.97 billion dollars of annual revenue. It now reported 22.1 billion in a single quarter.

The composition change matters more than the growth rate, and it is the reason this chapter sits where it does in the book. A gaming GPU business sells a consumer product through distributors into a seasonal retail channel at consumer margins. A data centre accelerator business sells systems to a small number of very large buyers, on long lead times, at margins that reflect a position no competitor can currently occupy. Those are different businesses with different working capital, different customer concentration and different cyclical behaviour. They happen to share a design team and a foundry. From fiscal 2023 onward, anyone still modelling Nvidia as a gaming company was modelling the wrong company.

## Shortage as the operating condition

Through 2024 the constraint on Nvidia's revenue stopped being demand and became supply. That is an unusual state for a company to be in, and it changes what the accounts mean.

When a business is demand-constrained, revenue is a marketing outcome. When it is supply-constrained, revenue is a manufacturing and allocation outcome: the number is set by how many units can be made and how they are distributed among customers who all want more. Forecasting such a company is not a question of estimating demand at all. It is a question of estimating wafer allocation, advanced packaging capacity, memory supply and yield.

Very little of that appears on the face of the financial statements. Backlog is not an asset. An order book is not recognised until the revenue is earned. What does appear, for the attentive reader, is the forward shape of the supply chain: purchase commitments, prepayments to suppliers, and capacity reservations. Those are the numbers that tell you what management has already paid to secure, and they are usually in the notes rather than the headline.

The market capitalisation kept up. Nvidia passed two trillion dollars in February 2024 and three trillion dollars in June 2024, per contemporaneous market-capitalisation reporting, and reached four trillion dollars on 9 July 2025, the first company to do so, per Morningstar's July 2025 analysis.

The successor architecture, Blackwell, did not arrive cleanly. In August 2024 The Information reported a design flaw that would delay shipments by roughly three months, coverage picked up by HPCwire, Tom's Hardware and SiliconANGLE at the time. In October 2024 the company's chief executive confirmed it publicly, saying there had been a design flaw in Blackwell, that it had produced low yield, and that it was "one hundred per cent Nvidia's fault", as reported by TechRadar.

That episode is worth keeping in view, because supply-constrained businesses have a specific failure mode. If your revenue is set by how much you can build, then a yield problem is a revenue problem, immediately and without any offsetting mechanism. There is no inventory to sell from and no price lever to pull. The company shipped anyway, but the sequence — flaw discovered late, three-month delay, public admission — is a reminder that the bottleneck at this layer is physical.

## Blackwell, and the largest one-day loss in market history

On 27 January 2025 Nvidia shares fell seventeen per cent and the company lost 589 billion dollars of market capitalisation in a single session, the largest one-day loss in US market history, as reported by CNBC, Bloomberg and Forbes on that date. The trigger was the release of a competitive model from the Chinese laboratory DeepSeek, which was reported to reach comparable performance at substantially lower training cost.

The market's reasoning that day was straightforward: if frontier capability can be reached with far less compute, then the forward demand for accelerators is smaller than assumed. Whether that reasoning was correct is a question for later chapters. What it demonstrated immediately is a fact about the whole structure this book describes. A company whose value rests on an assumption about how much compute the world will need can lose more value in six hours than most companies ever create, on a piece of news that changes no current-period line item at all. Nothing in the fiscal 2025 accounts moved on 27 January 2025. Only the assumption moved.

A month later the accounts arrived, and they were the largest in the company's history to that point.

### The same line item, a different cause

Then, in April 2025, the charge came back.

On 9 April 2025 the US government informed Nvidia that a licence would be required to export its H20 processor — a part designed specifically to comply with the previous round of restrictions — to China. In the first quarter of fiscal 2026 the company recognised a 4.5 billion dollar charge associated with H20 excess inventory and purchase obligations, as demand for the product fell away. H20 sales in that quarter, before the new requirement took effect, had been 4.6 billion dollars, and a further 2.5 billion dollars of H20 revenue could not be shipped. Guidance for the following quarter was set at 45.0 billion dollars, plus or minus two per cent, reflecting an expected loss of approximately 8.0 billion dollars of H20 revenue. Excluding the charge and its related tax effect, non-GAAP diluted earnings per share for the quarter would have been 96 cents. All figures per the company's first-quarter fiscal 2026 results announcement of May 2025, reported at the time by Computer Weekly and others.

Read that alongside August 2022 and the symmetry is exact. Same balance sheet line. Same accounting mechanism: inventory carried above what it can now realise, plus committed purchases nobody wants. Same treatment: recognised at once, through cost of revenue.

Only the cause is different. In 2022 the demand disappeared because buyers stopped wanting the product. In 2025 the demand was still there and the company was no longer permitted to serve it. To the income statement those two events are indistinguishable. That is a genuine limitation of financial statements, and a reason not to read a single charge as a verdict on a business. The charge tells you the size of the loss. It does not tell you whether the customer went away or the customer was taken away — for that you need the narrative disclosure, which is why the commentary around the numbers is not optional reading.

The share price did not treat the two the same way. On 29 October 2025 Nvidia became the first company valued at five trillion dollars, closing more than three per cent higher with a market capitalisation of 5.03 trillion dollars — three months after crossing four trillion — as reported by CNBC, Forbes and Al Jazeera on 29 October 2025.

### Visibility is not backlog

On 19 November 2025 the company reported its third quarter of fiscal 2026: revenue of 57.0 billion dollars, up sixty-two per cent year on year and twenty-two per cent sequentially, with Data Centre revenue of 51.2 billion dollars, up sixty-six per cent. The chief executive said that Blackwell sales were "off the charts" and that cloud GPUs were sold out. The chief financial officer said the company had visibility into 500 billion dollars of Blackwell and Rubin revenue from the start of calendar 2025 through the end of calendar 2026, of which roughly 350 billion dollars was expected in the fourteen months between that announcement and the end of 2026.

Half a trillion dollars is a striking figure, and it is worth being precise about what it is.

It is not backlog in any accounting sense. It is not deferred revenue, which is cash already received and shown as a liability until earned. It is not a contract asset. It is not audited, and it does not appear as such on any primary financial statement. It is management's stated view of demand it can currently see, across two product generations, one of which had not yet shipped. Companies use the word "visibility" precisely because it carries no accounting definition.

None of that makes the statement wrong or improper. Investors are entitled to management's view of forward demand, and a company that has been supply-constrained for two years has better information about its order book than anyone outside it. But a reader should file the number in the right drawer. Contracted, non-cancellable orders would be disclosed as such; commitments customers have made would show up in the customer's accounts as obligations. A figure described as visibility is a forecast with a corporate letterhead on it. Treat it as management's estimate — informed, self-interested, and unaudited — rather than as a receivable.

Source: the company's fourth-quarter and fiscal 2026 results announcement of 25 February 2026, as reported by Yahoo Finance, CNBC and Fortune.

Three things in that table are worth pausing on, and none of them is the revenue growth.

The first is the gap between the full-year gross margin of 71.1 per cent and the fourth-quarter figure of 75.0 per cent. The full year includes the first quarter, and the first quarter includes the 4.5 billion dollar H20 charge. The fourth quarter does not. That is what a charge does to a margin series: it puts a notch in the annual figure that the exit-rate quarter no longer carries. A reader who takes the annual margin as the run rate of the business will understate it; a reader who takes the fourth quarter as the annual figure will overstate it. Both numbers are correct and neither is the answer on its own.

The second is operating income of 130.4 billion dollars against revenue of 215.9 billion. The division is arithmetic and this book will not do it for you, but the result is a margin that very few businesses of any size have ever sustained, and it exists because the cost base did not scale with revenue. Research and development is largely a fixed commitment made years earlier; manufacturing is outsourced to a foundry that charges per wafer. When revenue multiplies eightfold in three years against a cost base that does not, almost all of the increment falls to the operating line.

The third is Gaming at 16.0 billion dollars, up forty-one per cent, and now roughly seven per cent of the total. The business that defined the company for its first three decades had its best year ever, in absolute terms, and became a rounding item. That is what a change of composition looks like when the new segment grows faster than the old one can be described.

Detailed dissection of these accounts — segment reporting, customer concentration, the balance sheet, cash conversion, and the investments the company has made in its own customers — is the subject of the next chapter. This one is a history.

## The largest company on earth, as at a date

As at mid-August 2026, Nvidia was the most valuable listed company in the world at roughly 5.3 to 5.4 trillion US dollars, per companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings. For scale, over the same period and from the same sources, Apple stood at roughly 4.41 trillion, Alphabet at roughly 4.20 trillion and Microsoft at roughly 3.66 trillion.

That figure will be wrong by the time you read this sentence.

It is not wrong in the sense of being mistaken; it was accurate on the day. It is wrong in the sense that market capitalisation is a price multiplied by a share count, and the price moves every second the market is open. In the four years this chapter covers, the same company's market value halved over one calendar year, added more than two hundred billion dollars in a single session, and lost 589 billion dollars in another. Any figure quoted here is a photograph of a moving object. If you want the current one, the live market-capitalisation trackers and the company's own quarterly reporting are the places to look, and the two together — price and accounts — are the only honest way to hold the question.

This book does not tell you whether that valuation is right. It has no share price target, makes no recommendation, and offers no forecast. What it can do is set out plainly what the four years contained, because the shape is easy to lose in the size of the final number.

## What the sequence actually shows

Four observations, offered as a way of reading the table rather than as a conclusion about the future.

The bad year was not an aberration in the business; it was an aberration in one segment. Gaming fell twenty-seven per cent in fiscal 2023 while the data centre segment grew forty-one per cent. The company that emerged from 2022 was not the company that went into it, and the change was already recorded in the fiscal 2023 accounts for anyone reading the segment note rather than the headline.

Charges are information, not just damage. Two large ones appear in this period — approximately 1.32 billion dollars in the second quarter of fiscal 2023 and 4.5 billion dollars in the first quarter of fiscal 2026 — and each tells you what management believed about future demand at the moment it was taken. The first said the crypto buyers were not coming back. The second said the China licence was not coming.

The value moved down the stack, and this is where it landed. Every dollar of the 193.7 billion of data centre revenue in fiscal 2026 was spent by somebody one floor up — a cloud operator, a model laboratory, an enterprise, a government — and most of those buyers do not report anything like a seventy-one per cent gross margin. That is the book's central argument, expressed in one company's income statement.

And the position is not self-sustaining. Nvidia does not own a fab. The chips in that 193.7 billion were manufactured by a foundry, packaged with capacity that has been in short supply, and fitted with high-bandwidth memory bought from a small number of suppliers. The largest company in the world sits on top of a supply chain it does not control, and the companies that do control it are the subject of the chapters on either side of this one.

One last note on staleness, since this chapter has quoted more dated figures than most. The results announcements cited here do not change. The market capitalisation does, daily. When those two disagree with something you read elsewhere, the filed and announced numbers win, and the date attached to any market value is not a decoration — it is the most important word in the sentence.
