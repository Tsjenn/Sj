# Sovereign Money

On 22 August 2025 the United States government bought 433.3 million shares of Intel at twenty dollars and forty-seven cents each, an outlay of eight point nine billion dollars for a holding of about nine point nine per cent, as reported by CNBC, CNN and Manufacturing Dive at the time and announced by the company the same week. The money did not come from a new appropriation. It came from five point seven billion dollars of a CHIPS Act award that had been promised but not yet paid, plus three point two billion dollars owed under a Department of Defense programme. A grant became equity. The state stopped being a funder and became a shareholder.

That single transaction contains most of what this chapter is about. Public money had already been flowing into this industry for years. What changed is the instrument.

## Five instruments, one balance sheet

State capital reaches the four layers through a small number of mechanisms, and they behave very differently once they arrive. Confusing them is the commonest error in coverage of this subject, because the headline number attached to each is measured on a different basis and lands in a different place in the accounts.

| Instrument | What it is | Where it lands | What the state gets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subsidy programme | Grant, loan, guarantee or tax credit against qualifying capital spending | Reduces the cost of an asset or is released to income over its life | Capacity built inside its borders, on conditions |
| State equity | Shares, preferred stock or warrants taken directly | Share register and, for the recipient, cash and dilution | An economic interest and, sometimes, a control right |
| Sovereign wealth fund | An investment vehicle deploying national savings or oil receipts | Nothing, unless it changes the company's cost of capital | Return, and in some cases domestic capability |
| Procurement and offtake | The state as customer, sometimes with a guaranteed price | Revenue, backlog and receivables | Assured supply |
| Export control | A licence requirement on what may be sold to whom | Inventory provisions, onerous purchase obligations, lost revenue | A boundary on a market |

The last row is the one readers tend to file under politics rather than finance. It belongs here: an export licence requirement produces one of the largest single charges any company in this book has taken, and that case is set out later in the chapter.

Nothing here takes a position on whether any of these programmes are good policy. The purpose is narrower: to describe how each mechanism works, what the party providing the money is optimising for, and what a reader can see in the accounts afterwards.

## The subsidy ledger

Begin with the scale, because the numbers are large enough that they change the industry's capacity plan rather than merely subsidising it.

| Programme | Headline amount | Announced | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States, CHIPS and Science Act | 52.7bn US dollars of appropriations plus a 25 per cent investment tax credit | August 2022 | Act as passed; summaries by McKinsey and trade press, 2022 |
| European Union, Chips Act | 43bn euros of public and private investment, half public | In force September 2023 | European Commission |
| European Union, InvestAI | 200bn euros mobilised, including a 20bn euro fund for up to five AI gigafactories | 11 February 2025 | European Commission announcement |
| China, National Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund III | 344bn yuan registered capital, about 47.5bn US dollars | Incorporated 24 May 2024 | Caixin and TrendForce, May 2024 |
| Japan, support for Rapidus | 2.35 trillion yen of cumulative government support | As at April 2026 | Japanese trade and financial press, 2026 |
| South Korea, semiconductor support package | 26 trillion won, raised to 33 trillion won | May 2024, raised April 2025 | Korea Herald and 2025 reporting |
| India, Semiconductor Mission 2.0 | 1.27 lakh crore rupees outlay | Cabinet framework approved 15 July 2026 | Indian government and 2026 reporting |

Each of those headline figures is measured differently, and the differences matter more than the ranking.

The American figure is an appropriation, which means money authorised to be spent rather than money spent. Of the fifty-two point seven billion dollars, thirty-nine billion was for manufacturing incentives, of which six billion could take the form of direct loans and loan guarantees rather than grants; eleven billion was for research and development; and two billion was directed at older legacy chips of the kind the automotive industry depends on. The twenty-five per cent investment tax credit sits outside that total and was scored separately at roughly twenty-four billion dollars. A tax credit is not a cheque. It reduces tax payable when the qualifying expenditure has actually been incurred and the return has been filed, which can be years after the announcement.

The awards themselves arrived slowly and were often smaller than proposed. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company concluded its negotiation in November 2024 for six point six billion dollars towards three fabs in Phoenix, Arizona. GlobalFoundries was awarded up to one point five billion dollars on 20 November 2024, and Micron up to six point one six five billion on 10 December 2024. Samsung's award was finalised that December at four point seven billion dollars, having been proposed at six point four billion before the Commerce Department completed its due diligence. All four figures come from the Commerce Department announcements and the award trackers maintained by the trade press through 2024 and 2025.

Read the phrase "up to" carefully. An award of this kind is a maximum, disbursed against construction and production milestones, and reduced or withheld if the milestones are missed. The gap between announcement and cash is measured in years, and that difference is exactly the sort of thing to look for in the cash flow statement rather than the press release.

The European figure is different again. The forty-three billion euros was never a European Union budget line; it was a mobilisation target combining a modest central allocation with member state aid and expected private investment. The measurable part is the aid actually approved, and cumulative approvals reached roughly fourteen point two billion euros on 14 July 2026, when Brussels cleared a further six hundred and fifty-nine million euros for four German projects, per 2026 reporting. The European Court of Auditors published a report in 2025 concluding that the Union was very unlikely to reach its target of twenty per cent of the global market by 2030, against a starting point of around ten per cent — an official body auditing an official programme, and a useful corrective to quoting the announcement figure as though it were an outcome.

The Chinese vehicle is structurally unlike the others. The third phase of the National Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund was incorporated on 24 May 2024 with registered capital of three hundred and forty-four billion yuan, about forty-seven point five billion US dollars, held by nineteen state-owned shareholders led by the Ministry of Finance, per Caixin and TrendForce reporting at the time. Its two predecessors were capitalised at one hundred and thirty-eight point seven billion yuan in 2014 and two hundred and four point one billion yuan in 2019. This is not a grant programme but an investment fund taking equity stakes across design, manufacture, packaging, test, equipment and materials, and it expects a return, at least nominally. The distinction matters because a fund that buys equity dilutes other shareholders and sits on a capitalisation table, where a grant simply reduces the cost of a building.

Japan's approach concentrates on a single company. Cumulative government support for Rapidus, the venture set up to bring two-nanometre logic manufacturing to Japan, reached two point three five trillion yen as at April 2026 according to Japanese reporting, with six hundred and thirty billion yen allocated for the 2026 fiscal year inside a broader package of about one trillion yen. Rapidus announced a one hundred and fifty billion yen funding round from the Japanese government in June 2026, and has said it expects total investment to reach around seven trillion yen and hopes to list its shares around 2031, with mass production planned for late 2027. Those are the company's own statements about its plans, not outcomes.

South Korea announced a twenty-six trillion won package in May 2024, then raised it to thirty-three trillion won in April 2025, explicitly citing trade frictions and tariffs, per the Korea Herald and contemporaneous reporting. India's Semiconductor Mission moved to a second phase when the Union Cabinet approved a framework on 15 July 2026 with an outlay of one point two seven lakh crore rupees; as at that month twelve manufacturing units had been approved with cumulative committed investment above one point six four lakh crore rupees, of which nine were packaging units. The largest project, a Tata joint venture with Powerchip at ninety-one thousand crore rupees, is a mature-node fab in the twenty-eight to one hundred and ten nanometre range expected to ship wafers in 2028.

That last detail is worth pausing on. Most of the capacity these programmes are buying is not at the leading edge. It is mature-node capacity, packaging and test — strategically defensible, and economically far more exposed to competition than the frontier processes described in chapters eight and ten.

## Where a subsidy lands in the accounts

This is the part that is almost never explained, and it changes how two subsidised companies compare.

A capital grant — money received towards the cost of a long-lived asset — is generally accounted for in one of two ways. It can be deducted from the carrying amount of the asset, so that a smaller number is depreciated over the asset's life; or it can be recognised as deferred income and released to profit in instalments over the same life. Both recognise the benefit over the same period, and neither changes the cash. But they put the benefit in different places in the income statement, and one of those places is above the gross margin line.

### An illustrative fab

Take an illustrative fab, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It costs twenty billion in some currency, is depreciated straight-line over ten years, and attracts a capital grant of three billion. It produces revenue of eight billion a year, with cash operating costs of four billion charged to cost of sales.

| Line | Grant deducted from asset | Grant held as deferred income |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 8.0 | 8.0 |
| Cash cost of sales | (4.0) | (4.0) |
| Depreciation in cost of sales | (1.7) | (2.0) |
| Gross profit | 2.3 | 2.0 |
| Gross margin | 28.8 per cent | 25.0 per cent |
| Grant released to other income | — | 0.3 |
| Operating profit | 2.3 | 2.3 |

Identical company, identical cash, identical operating profit — and a gross margin nearly four points apart. Gross margin is the number this industry is judged on, as chapter three set out. Anyone comparing a subsidised fab operator in one jurisdiction with a subsidised fab operator in another, without first reading both accounting policy notes, is comparing presentation rather than performance.

Three further consequences follow, and each is visible in a filing if you look for it.

The first is that a grant deducted from an asset suppresses depreciation for a decade. Reported margin improves not because the plant is more efficient but because part of it was paid for by somebody else — a real benefit, spread thinly, flattering exactly the ratio investors watch most closely.

The second is conditionality. Grants of this size come with obligations: build by a date, produce at a volume, employ a number of people, restrict certain investments in certain jurisdictions, and repay if you do not. Those obligations create contingent liabilities and, occasionally, provisions, and they belong in the commitments and contingencies note.

The third is timing. Announced awards, signed awards, milestone certifications and cash receipts are four different events, sometimes years apart. A company can be described in the press as having received billions while its cash flow statement shows a fraction of it. The cash flow statement is right.

Tax credits behave differently again. A credit that is refundable or payable in cash may be treated as a reduction in the cost of the asset or as income, depending on the framework and the facts, and it arrives on the timetable of the tax authority rather than the construction schedule. A reader who wants to know what a subsidy is actually worth to a company should look for the deferred income balance, the accounting policy note on government assistance, and the reconciliation of the tax charge — not the number in the announcement.

## The state as shareholder

The Intel transaction described at the top of this chapter converted an unpaid grant into equity, and it did so with an additional feature that deserves attention. Alongside the shares, the government received a five-year warrant to buy a further five per cent of Intel's common shares at twenty dollars, exercisable if Intel ceases to own at least fifty-one per cent of its foundry business, per the terms reported in August 2025 and described in the company's own announcement.

Read that warrant as what it is. It is not primarily an investment; it is a control device expressed in the language of equity. A rule requiring a company to retain a business would be a regulation, subject to challenge and process. A warrant that becomes valuable if the company disposes of that business achieves a similar end through the capitalisation table, and costs the issuer nothing until the trigger event occurs. Mechanically it is a contingent instrument whose value depends on a corporate decision, and the fair value of instruments like it is exactly the sort of judgement chapter sixty-one takes up.

The template is not confined to semiconductors. In July 2025 the United States Department of Defense agreed to invest four hundred million dollars in the rare earth producer MP Materials in the form of convertible preferred equity and warrants, taking a holding of about fifteen per cent on an as-converted and as-exercised basis at a conversion and exercise price of thirty dollars and three cents, alongside a ten-year price floor commitment for the company's neodymium-praseodymium output, as reported by CNBC on 10 July 2025. The state became simultaneously the largest shareholder, a customer and the guarantor of a minimum price. Each of those three roles pulls in a different direction in a downturn, and only one of them is on the share register.

The questions raised by a state shareholding are the same as for any other large holder, with two additions. Does the holding carry votes, board representation or consent rights, because that determines whether related-party disclosure is triggered — the mechanics of which chapter fifty-three set out. And what are the trigger events on any attached warrants, because those describe, more precisely than any policy document, what the state is trying to prevent.

## Two kinds of sovereign fund

The phrase "sovereign wealth fund" covers two species that share a legal form and almost nothing else.

### The index owner

Norway's fund is the clearest example of the first kind. It holds equity in effectively every listed company in the world, owning on average around one and a half per cent of global listed markets. As at 30 June 2026 its stake in Nvidia was reported at one point two eight per cent, worth about sixty-two billion US dollars, with Apple at one point two four per cent and about fifty-two billion, and Alphabet at one point one seven per cent and about fifty billion, per 2026 reporting on the fund's half-year disclosure. Those holdings make it one of the largest single owners of the companies described in this book.

But it does not want anything from them beyond return. It is an index-shaped owner of national savings, mandated to diversify, and structurally indifferent to whether the value in this industry sits at the top of the stack or the bottom, because it owns both. Its influence is exercised through voting and engagement rather than through capital allocation. When a fund of this kind appears on a share register, it tells you almost nothing about the company. It tells you the company is large.

### The strategic owner

The second kind wants something specific, and is willing to pay for it.

MGX, established in Abu Dhabi in March 2024 by Mubadala Investment Company and the artificial intelligence group G42, and chaired by the emirate's deputy ruler, closed its first fund on 1 July 2026 at forty-nine billion dollars, four billion above target, according to reporting by Forbes and Tech Times in July 2026. Its disclosed portfolio of fourteen companies spans the stack and includes Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI and SpaceX. It co-led OpenAI's one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round in March 2026 at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, and it is a partner in Stargate. Mubadala, its parent, has been described in 2026 reporting as a three hundred and eighty-five billion dollar fund.

Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund has taken the operating route rather than the portfolio route. Humain, the artificial intelligence company it owns, is building data centres directly: fifty megawatts targeted by the fourth quarter of 2025 and a further fifty megawatts each quarter into 2026, with stated targets of one point nine gigawatts by 2030 and six gigawatts by 2034, per CNBC's reporting of August 2025. It has announced the purchase of eighteen thousand Nvidia GB300 processors subject to United States government approval, a five hundred megawatt arrangement with AMD, and a five billion dollar zone with Amazon Web Services, with the fund's initial commitment reported at around forty billion dollars phased through 2030. Every one of those numbers is an announcement of intention, and chapter fifty-six's warning applies: nobody publishes a correction when an intention changes. In the same region, Stargate UAE is being built by G42 with OpenAI, Oracle, Nvidia, Cisco and SoftBank — a one gigawatt cluster within a campus of up to five gigawatts, reported at a cost of around thirty billion dollars.

None of this is new for the Gulf funds. The Public Investment Fund committed up to forty-five billion dollars to the SoftBank Vision Fund under a memorandum signed in October 2016 and made binding in May 2017, and Mubadala committed fifteen billion, per both funds' announcements at the time. The difference a decade later is that they now invest directly rather than through an intermediary, and build physical capacity at home rather than only buying shares abroad.

The incentives of a strategic sovereign investor are worth stating plainly, because they are not the incentives of a fund manager. It wants domestic capability, so it will pay for local construction that an ordinary investor would site elsewhere. It wants demand for domestic energy, which is why gas-rich states find gigawatt-scale computing attractive on grounds that have nothing to do with computing. It wants technology transfer and skilled employment. And it is prepared to accept a lower financial return than a pension fund would, because the return it is measuring includes things that do not appear in an internal rate of return calculation.

That is the important asymmetry. A competitor bidding against state capital for land, power, engineers or accelerator allocation is bidding against a buyer with a different objective function, and it is the reason capacity now gets built in places a purely commercial calculation would not have chosen.

## National champions and state-backed compute

Two further categories follow from the money.

The national champion is a company that exists because a state decided it should. Rapidus is the purest current example: a manufacturer with a government-funded research phase, a target process, a target year and a hoped-for listing around 2031. Such a business has capital without a cost of capital, an owner indifferent to near-term profitability, and a milestone schedule that functions as its covenant. Judging it on conventional return metrics before it has a product misreads what it is. Never judging it that way would be worse.

State-backed compute is the other category, and it has become a visible revenue line for the layer below. Revenue from sovereign customers exceeded thirty billion dollars in Nvidia's fiscal year 2026 — the year that produced two hundred and fifteen point nine billion dollars of total revenue and one hundred and ninety-three point seven billion from data centre — more than triple the prior year, with announced arrangements in Japan, South Korea and Germany, per 2026 reporting. Jensen Huang has said the company sees more than one trillion dollars of data centre revenue through 2027, with roughly sixty per cent from the large cloud operators and the remaining forty per cent split across neocloud providers, industrial and enterprise use, and sovereign customers, per coverage in 2026. That is a forward-looking statement by a chief executive rather than guidance in a filing, and it should be recorded as such.

The stated reasons for buying compute rather than renting it are consistent across countries: data residency, models trained in the national language and on national records, defence applications that cannot run on foreign infrastructure, and the general preference for owning a capability rather than depending on somebody else's. Whether owning the machines delivers those things is a separate question, since the models, the software and the operating expertise mostly still come from elsewhere.

For the supplier, a sovereign customer changes the shape of the receivable rather than its size. Government credit is generally strong and government procurement is generally slow. Concentration moves from a handful of companies to a handful of countries, which is not obviously an improvement in diversification, and it introduces a variable — a change of government — that no credit model handles well. If sovereign demand becomes a material share of revenue, the geographic revenue note becomes as important as the customer concentration note.

## Export controls as an accounting event

The most instructive example in this chapter of policy becoming a line item involves no subsidy at all.

On 9 April 2025 Nvidia was informed by the United States government that a licence would be required to export its H20 products to China. The company reported the consequences in its first quarter of fiscal 2026: a charge of four point five billion dollars for excess H20 inventory and purchase obligations; four point six billion dollars of H20 sales recognised in the quarter before the requirement took effect; a further two point five billion dollars of H20 revenue it was unable to ship; and an outlook reflecting a loss of approximately eight billion dollars of H20 revenue. GAAP gross margin for the quarter was sixty point five per cent and non-GAAP sixty-one per cent; excluding the charge, non-GAAP gross margin would have been seventy-one point three per cent. All of those figures are from the company's results announcement of 28 May 2025.

Look at what the mechanism did. A licensing decision, taken by a government, produced an inventory write-down, a provision against non-cancellable purchase commitments, and a ten-point movement in the single ratio this industry is judged on. No contract was breached. No customer defaulted. Nothing about the product changed. This is the clearest demonstration available that political risk in this industry is not an abstraction sitting in a risk factors section; it is a debit and a credit.

The sequel is stranger. In August 2025 it was reported that Nvidia and AMD had agreed to pay fifteen per cent of revenue from certain chip sales to China to the United States government in exchange for the export licences permitting those sales, covering Nvidia's H20 and AMD's MI308 products, per reporting by the Associated Press, CNN, Al Jazeera and Fortune on and after 11 August 2025. The President said publicly that he had initially sought twenty per cent and had been negotiated down to fifteen. Trade specialists quoted in that coverage raised questions about linking export licensing to payments.

Set the policy debate aside and consider only the classification question, because it is genuinely difficult. A payment of that kind is not obviously a tax, since it is not levied by statute on a defined base, and not obviously a royalty, since nothing is licensed to the payer. It could be characterised as a cost of obtaining the licence, and therefore a cost of sales; or as a reduction of the transaction price, and therefore a deduction from revenue; or as a levy recognised when the obligating event occurs. The three treatments produce the same net profit and three different revenue and gross margin figures. Which is chosen is a matter for the companies, their auditors and their regulators; what a reader can do is find the accounting policy note and understand that the classification is a judgement rather than a fact.

There is one further mechanism here that is easy to miss, and it closes a circle. An export control defines the boundary of a market. On the far side of that boundary, demand for a domestically produced substitute is created by the control itself — and that demand is what the other jurisdiction's subsidy programme is funding. Restriction on one side and subsidy on the other are not opposing policies. They are complementary inputs into the same capacity decision, and both of them are decisions about a fab that will produce wafers three years from now.

## What state capital wants

Different public actors are optimising for different things, and their behaviour in a downturn follows from that rather than from the size of the cheque.

| Actor | Instrument | What it optimises | How it behaves when things go wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Industry ministry | Grants, credits, loan guarantees | Capacity and employment inside its borders | Enforces milestones, may claw back; exposed to elections |
| Defence buyer | Equity, offtake, price floors | Assured supply at any price | Pays the floor; indifferent to upside |
| Return-seeking sovereign fund | Listed equity at index scale | Total return, diversification | Rebalances quietly; sells nothing in a hurry |
| Strategic sovereign fund | Direct stakes, operating subsidiaries, data centres | Domestic capability plus return | Long-dated; can add capital where a private investor would not |
| Export control authority | Licences and denials | Restriction, not profit | Tightens or relaxes; the counterparty has no recourse |
| State-as-shareholder | Shares and warrants | Both an economic interest and a control right | Holds; may exercise a trigger unrelated to price |

Two general observations follow.

State capital is patient but not permanent. It can outwait a cycle in a way private capital cannot, which is precisely its advantage. It can also reverse in a way private capital rarely does, because the mandate is set by a political process with its own calendar. Patience and durability are not the same property, and treating a government commitment as though it were a signed twenty-year contract is a mistake in both directions.

State capital is also price-insensitive at entry and can be abrupt at exit. It buys because it wants the capability, not because the entry multiple is attractive; it may sell because a policy changed, not because the asset deteriorated. That injects a source of price movement into these securities that has nothing to do with the companies' operations.

## What it changes

Four durable effects are worth carrying out of this chapter, independent of any particular figure.

The marginal capacity decision has moved. In an unsubsidised industry, a fab is built when its expected return clears a hurdle rate. Where public money covers a material share of the capital cost, the hurdle falls, and capacity gets built that a commercial calculation would have declined. That is the intended effect of the policy, and it is also an amplifier of the semiconductor cycle: the industry's worst episodes have followed capacity ordered into a shortage and delivered into a glut, and a subsidy makes ordering easier without making demand more predictable.

The cost of capital has diverged. A national champion funded by its government and a listed competitor funded by shareholders are not competing on the same terms, and the difference does not appear in either company's gross margin. It appears in what each can afford to attempt.

Country concentration has joined customer concentration. For a supplier with tens of billions of dollars of sovereign revenue, the relevant question is no longer only which companies buy, but which governments, and what would change if one of them stopped.

And political variables have entered the depreciation schedule. The useful life of an asset assumes it can be operated and its output sold for that period. A licensing regime can shorten the second half of that sentence overnight, as the 2025 example above demonstrates in cash. Chapter four's argument about asset lives is not purely a technical one.

## A reader's checklist, and the snapshot

Where a company is a recipient of state money, the following are all readable from public filings. Find the accounting policy note on government assistance and establish which method is used, because it determines whether the benefit sits above or below the gross margin line. Find the deferred income balance and how fast it is unwinding. Reconcile announced awards to cash actually received in the cash flow statement, and treat the difference as the real number. Read the commitments and contingencies note for conditions and clawbacks. Check the share register for state entities, and read the terms of any warrants attached, especially the trigger events. Read the geographic revenue disclosure for government-driven concentration. And where an export control has bitten, look for the inventory provision, the onerous purchase obligation and the restated margin, all of which the company will normally quantify because it wants the underlying figure understood.

Every number in this chapter is a snapshot with a date attached, and several are already moving. Award totals change as milestones are certified, fund sizes on each close, sovereign holdings twice a year at most and with a lag, and export control policy between a filing and the day you read it. The current versions live in company filings, in the announcements of the funds and ministries themselves, and in the audit reports official bodies publish on their own programmes — which, as the European example shows, are often the least flattering and most useful source available.

Chapter sixty-one turns to what the auditors see when they examine judgements of this kind: how a government grant is tested, how a contingent instrument is valued, and what a reader can infer from the way a company chooses to disclose it.
