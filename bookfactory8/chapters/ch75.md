# What the Stack Might Look Like in 2030

A heavy-duty gas turbine ordered today will be installed towards the end of this decade. GE Vernova's gas equipment backlog reached one hundred and sixteen gigawatts by the second quarter of 2026, against annualised production running at roughly twenty gigawatts, with delivery slots described as effectively sold out through 2030 and only about ten gigawatts of cumulative availability left across 2029 and 2030, per company statements reported by Turbomachinery Magazine, Utility Dive and Power Engineering in 2025 and 2026.

That single fact does something useful to a chapter about 2030. It rules out the blank page. A large part of the physical stack that will exist in 2030 has already been ordered, financed, permitted or queued, by people who had to commit before they knew whether the demand would hold. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion US dollars, above analyst expectations, in its January 2026 results; the capacity that money buys arrives towards the end of the decade. The interesting uncertainty about 2030 is therefore not how much equipment exists. It is who ends up collecting the margin on it.

## What this chapter is, and what it is not

This chapter contains no forecast. It offers no probabilities, no price targets, no view on any security, and no recommendation to buy or sell anything. Nothing in it is investment advice.

What it offers instead is three scenarios. A scenario, used properly, is not a guess dressed up. It is a story in which the mechanisms are made to agree with each other, so that a reader can ask a much better question than "what will happen": namely, *if this were the world I was in, what would I already be able to see?*

Each of the three is built the same way, in four parts:

- **The mechanism** — the economic logic that would have to hold.
- **The accounting signature** — what the published numbers would look like if it were happening.
- **The distinguishing indicators** — the observations that separate this scenario from the other two, rather than merely being consistent with all of them.
- **The falsifier** — what would show the scenario to be wrong.

The fourth part is the one most commonly left out, and it is the one that makes the exercise honest. A scenario that nothing could disprove is a mood.

Three, rather than two or five, for a plain reason. Two scenarios collapse into optimism and pessimism, which teaches nothing. Five overlap so heavily that the indicators stop discriminating. Three is enough to break the false choice between boom and bust while keeping each story falsifiable.

One more warning before the stories start. Every figure below carries a date and a source, and every one of them will have moved by the time it is read. That is not a defect of this chapter; it is the condition of writing about a fast-moving industry at all. The figures are here to show what the state of play looked like at a known moment, so that a reader can measure drift from it.

## Scenario one: concentration deepens

### The mechanism

In this world, nothing important breaks, and the winners of the first phase compound their positions rather than defending them.

The logic is that each layer's advantage is self-reinforcing. At the silicon layer, the leading-edge process is expensive enough that only volume justifies it, and volume is only available to the leader, which is why the capital expenditure guidance keeps rising rather than being competed away. At the equipment layer beneath, extreme ultraviolet lithography has one supplier and no substitute, and demand for it is set by everybody else's ambitions. At the memory layer, qualification does the work that patents do elsewhere: an HBM stack is bonded permanently beside a specific processor and must be tested for years before it is bought, so capacity without qualification is worth nothing, and the qualified suppliers can be counted on one hand.

Above them, the compute layer concentrates for a different reason — access to capital and to power. A company able to guide to nine or twelve figures of annual capital expenditure can sign the offtake agreements, pre-pay the fabs and win the interconnection studies that a smaller operator cannot. And the model layer concentrates because training is a fixed cost that must be recovered over a user base, which favours whoever already has the largest one.

The most concrete evidence for this scenario is that the concentration is already documented in the filings. In the third quarter of its fiscal 2026, four direct customers each accounted for more than a tenth of Nvidia's revenue — twenty-two, fifteen, thirteen and eleven per cent, sixty-one per cent between them — per the company's quarterly filing as reported by the Motley Fool in November 2025. That is not a market. It is a small number of counterparties who are also, in several cases, each other's competitors, each other's suppliers and each other's investors.

### The accounting signature

If concentration is deepening, the accounts show it in a particular pattern.

Gross margin at the constrained layers holds or rises through the period, rather than eroding as capacity arrives — because allocation, not price, is doing the rationing. Backlogs extend beyond the planning horizon and are disclosed as such. Customers pay in advance: prepayments and long-term supply commitments appear in the notes of the buyer and as contract liabilities in the seller. Customer concentration disclosures stay high or rise. Capital expenditure guidance keeps being revised upward mid-year rather than down. And equity investments flow between the layers — suppliers taking stakes in customers, customers pre-funding suppliers — because when supply is the scarce thing, capital chases access to it rather than the other way round.

Notice how many of those are disclosures rather than measurements. The concentration case can largely be read out of the notes to the accounts, which is convenient, because the notes are published on a timetable and the press is not.

### The distinguishing indicators

The indicator that separates this scenario from the others is *rationing behaviour*. When a supplier allocates rather than prices — when the question a customer asks is "can I have any" rather than "at what price" — concentration is deepening regardless of what the headline growth rates say. Watch for allocation language in customer commentary, for backlog measured in years rather than quarters, and for the persistence of customer concentration disclosures across several filings.

A second indicator is the direction of cross-layer investment. Money moving downward — from application and model companies into compute and silicon — is a sign that the lower layers hold the scarce asset. Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic have been reported as distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them, per CNBC in August 2026; the same instrument read in the other direction tells you where the perceived scarcity sits.

### The falsifier

This scenario fails if a genuine second source appears at any of the constrained layers and takes durable share at similar margins. Not a competitor with a product — competitors with products already exist — but one that a large buyer qualifies, deploys at scale, and keeps buying through a downturn.

It also fails, more quietly, if capacity arrives on schedule and margins hold anyway for a year and then do not. The tell would be a supplier that begins to compete on price while still describing demand as strong.

## Scenario two: commoditisation spreads upward

### The mechanism

In this world, the bottom of the stack does what capital-intensive industries have always eventually done. Capacity ordered at the peak arrives together, shortage turns to sufficiency, and the scarcity premium works its way up the stack until it reaches the only layer that has a customer relationship to defend.

The mechanism has three parts, and each is already partly visible.

The first is capacity. Every constrained layer is currently spending to remove its own constraint: SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, per CNBC in August 2026, and TSMC's 2026 guidance of fifty-two to fifty-six billion dollars was above what analysts expected. Capital expenditure committed during a shortage is, by construction, capacity that arrives after it.

The second is substitution. Custom accelerators designed by the buyers themselves are no longer a research project: Broadcom reported AI semiconductor revenue of ten point eight billion dollars in its second fiscal quarter of 2026, up one hundred and forty-three per cent year on year, with an AI backlog of about seventy-three billion dollars described as deliverable over roughly eighteen months and guidance above one hundred billion dollars of AI semiconductor revenue for fiscal 2027, per the company's June 2026 results and coverage by Qz and Futurum. Estimates of what this does to merchant share disagree sharply. IDC's estimate, reported in 2026 trade coverage, put Nvidia at about eighty-one per cent of data centre AI chip revenue in 2026; other third-party estimates published the same year span roughly seventy to eighty-six per cent, the spread depending almost entirely on whether hyperscaler custom silicon is counted in the denominator. None of those custom parts is sold externally, so nobody outside the buyers knows their revenue. The honest finding is the range, not any number within it.

The third part is the price of the output itself. Epoch AI's work on inference price trends found that the cost of reaching a given capability level has fallen at wildly different rates depending on which level is chosen — around one point seven times a year at the lowest capability threshold measured and about thirty-one times a year at the highest, with the range across tasks spanning roughly nine to nine hundred times a year. Both halves of that sentence matter. Prices are collapsing, and they are collapsing unevenly, which is why "the cost of intelligence is falling" is true and almost useless without saying which intelligence.

Falling unit prices do not by themselves destroy margin; the previous chapters worked through why. They destroy margin when they fall faster than volume rises, and when the falling input price is passed through rather than retained. In this scenario both happen at the lower layers, and the layer closest to the customer — the one that owns the workflow, the data and the billing relationship — retains the difference.

### The accounting signature

The signature here is almost the photographic negative of scenario one.

Gross margin at the silicon and memory layers compresses while revenue still grows, because average selling prices fall faster than volumes rise. Inventory builds, and the ratio of inventory to cost of sales lengthens. Order backlogs shorten, or stop being mentioned. Capital expenditure guidance is trimmed, quietly, in the second half of a year. And one item deserves particular attention: assumed useful lives. When the economics of an asset deteriorate, the accounting response that preserves reported profit is to depreciate it over a longer period. An extension of assumed useful lives during a period of falling prices is not evidence of wrongdoing, and should never be described as such — but it is the single line-item change that most flatters reported margin at exactly the moment margin is under pressure, and it belongs at the top of any reader's list.

Take an illustrative operator, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It buys one hundred million dollars of accelerators and depreciates them over four years: twenty-five million a year of depreciation. Revenue from renting them is sixty million a year. Change the assumed life to six years and depreciation falls to about sixteen point seven million; reported gross profit rises by roughly eight point three million a year with no change whatever in the cash, the machines or the customers. Now suppose rental prices fall by a fifth, to forty-eight million. Under the four-year life, the reported margin falls sharply. Under the six-year life, it barely moves. The same deterioration is either visible or invisible depending on one management estimate — which is why the estimate, and any change to it, is worth more attention than most headline numbers.

Higher up the stack, this scenario shows the opposite pattern: model-layer gross margins improve as compute prices fall, and application-layer companies report improving retention and pricing power rather than the discounting that would accompany a genuinely commoditised product.

### The distinguishing indicators

The cleanest discriminator is the *behaviour of price at the constrained layers*. In scenario one, buyers queue. In scenario two, sellers discount. Contract prices for memory, spot and contract rental rates for accelerator time, and the pricing of frontier model tokens are three independent readings of the same thing, and they are published often enough to be useful.

The second discriminator is where the margin improvement shows up. If gross margin at the model layer is rising while gross margin at the silicon layer is falling, value is moving up the stack — the reverse of the movement this book has traced — and that is the observation that would most directly challenge the book's own argument. It is stated here plainly for that reason.

### The falsifier

This scenario fails if capacity arrives and margins hold anyway. That would mean demand grew into the new supply, which is precisely what did not happen in the memory cycles of previous decades and precisely what the industry currently expects. It also fails if the substitution never becomes real: if custom accelerators remain concentrated in a few internal workloads, and merchant share proves durable across several years rather than one.

## Scenario three: the constraint shifts to energy

### The mechanism

In this world, the chips are available and it does not help, because the binding constraint has moved to something no fab can produce.

The arithmetic is public. The IEA's Energy and AI analysis projects data centre electricity demand at around nine hundred and forty-five terawatt-hours by 2030, more than double the roughly four hundred and fifteen terawatt-hours consumed in 2024. Set against that, the supply side moves at the speed of regulated infrastructure. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's *Queued Up* report, 2025 edition, found the median time from an interconnection request to commercial operation had risen to over four years for projects built between 2018 and 2024, against under two years for those built between 2000 and 2007. And the fastest-to-deploy generation technology is itself sold out, as the turbine backlog at the opening of this chapter shows.

The consequence is a reordering of who is scarce. A company holding an energised, interconnected site with firm power becomes the holder of the bottleneck asset, and the accelerator becomes the commodity waiting for it. This is not a hypothetical inversion. It is already visible in siting behaviour: the industry that used to bring data centres to the demand now brings them to the supply.

There is a second-order version of this scenario that deserves separate mention, because it works through politics rather than physics. Electricity is a regulated product bought by voters. If retail rates rise visibly in a region where large new loads have connected, the constraint stops being engineering and becomes permitting, tariff design and local moratoria. This book takes no position on how any jurisdiction should resolve that. It notes only that the resolution would be made by regulators and legislatures rather than by procurement teams, and that this changes both the timetable and the identity of the decision-maker.

### The accounting signature

Energy constraint has a distinctive footprint in the accounts, and it is not the electricity bill.

The first mark is that power moves from the income statement to the balance sheet. An operator that cannot obtain grid power on its timetable and builds its own generation converts a monthly utility invoice into property, plant and equipment with a depreciation charge of its own. Two campuses with identical computing can then show different asset bases and different margins purely because one had to become, in part, a power company.

The second mark is in commitments. Long-term power purchase agreements are contractual obligations spanning a decade or more, and they show up in the commitments note rather than in this year's costs. A reader who looks only at the cost of revenue will miss the whole thing.

The third mark is utilisation. If power rather than silicon is binding, installed capacity and *usable* capacity diverge: chips are delivered, racked and unable to run at full draw. That divergence surfaces as depreciation on assets that are not earning, which compresses margin without any change in price. It is the most easily missed of the three, because nothing in a set of accounts is labelled "idle".

### The distinguishing indicators

The indicator that separates this scenario from the other two is the gap between *announced* and *energised* capacity. Announcements measure ambition. Interconnection agreements, utility filings and grid operator queues measure fact. When those two series diverge for several consecutive quarters, the constraint has moved.

A second indicator is who is signing the long contracts. In a chip-constrained world, the multi-year commitments are to foundries and memory makers. In an energy-constrained world, they are to generators, and the counterparties named in commitment notes change accordingly.

A third is the price of the input. Wholesale power prices and capacity-market clearing prices in the regions where compute is clustering are published, frequently, by grid operators and regulators, and they respond to load faster than any corporate disclosure does.

### The falsifier

This scenario fails if efficiency outruns demand. Performance per watt has improved substantially with each accelerator generation, and if the improvement compounds faster than usage grows, the projected load simply does not materialise. It also fails if the interconnection queues clear — which reform in several jurisdictions is explicitly attempting — or if on-site generation proves cheap and quick enough at scale to make the queue irrelevant. Notably, total queued capacity in the United States actually fell about twelve per cent in 2024, the first significant decline in years, per the same Berkeley Lab report, partly through reform and partly through a wave of withdrawals. One year is not a trend, but it is the sort of observation that should make a reader hold this scenario loosely.

## The three side by side

| | Concentration deepens | Commoditisation spreads upward | Constraint shifts to energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binding constraint | Leading-edge capacity, qualified memory, lithography | None — capacity is sufficient | Power: generation, interconnection, siting |
| Who holds the margin | The lower layers: silicon, memory, equipment | The layer nearest the customer | Whoever holds energised, interconnected sites |
| Rationing method | Allocation | Price | Queue and permit |
| Gross margin at silicon | Holds or rises | Compresses while revenue still grows | Mixed — falls through under-utilisation, not price |
| Inventory and backlog | Backlog beyond planning horizon | Inventory days lengthen; backlog shortens | Equipment delivered but not earning |
| Capex guidance | Revised upward mid-year | Trimmed quietly in the second half | Redirected from silicon to generation and grid |
| Balance sheet tell | Prepayments, contract liabilities, cross-layer equity stakes | Extended useful lives; rising inventory | Own generation in PP&E; long-dated purchase commitments |
| Clearest single indicator | Customer concentration disclosures persisting | Contract prices falling while volumes rise | Announced capacity diverging from energised capacity |
| What would falsify it | A qualified second source taking durable share at similar margins | Capacity arrives and margins hold | Efficiency outruns demand, or queues clear |

Three cautions about that table.

The scenarios are not mutually exclusive, and the most likely 2030 is a mixture. Memory could commoditise while lithography does not. North America could be energy-constrained while another region is not. A stack can be concentrated at one layer and competitive at the next, which is roughly what it is now.

They are also not evenly spaced in time. Commoditisation follows capacity, and capacity follows lead times, so scenario two is partly a statement about *when* rather than *whether*. A shortage that resolves in 2029 and one that resolves in 2033 produce very different decades from identical mechanisms.

And there is a fourth thing readers usually ask for, which is not in the table: the crash. A sharp disappointment in end demand — enterprises not renewing, consumer subscriptions plateauing, a large buyer cancelling — is not a separate scenario. It is an accelerant of scenario two, which is what a downturn always is in a capital-intensive industry. Demand falls, capacity arrives anyway because it was ordered years earlier, prices break, and the constraint dissolves. The mechanism is the same; only the speed changes.

## What all three have in common

Read the three stories again and something uncomfortable emerges. They disagree about almost everything — which layer is scarce, which margin holds, which company is enviable — and they agree completely about the structure.

In every one of them, somebody owns a bottleneck, and that somebody collects.

In the first, the bottleneck is the leading-edge fab, the lithography tool and the qualified memory stack. In the second, it is the customer relationship — the workflow, the data, the switching cost — which is a bottleneck too, just a commercial rather than a physical one. In the third, it is an energised site with firm power. What moves between scenarios is the *location* of the bottleneck. What does not move is the fact that there is one, and that the returns cluster around it.

This is the durable insight of the whole analysis, and it is worth stating carefully, because it is easy to hear as a platitude and it is not one.

A bottleneck, in this specific sense, is an asset that money cannot instantly reproduce. That is the entire test. Capital can buy almost anything in this industry within a quarter — engineers, land, servers, marketing, even a competitor. What it cannot buy quickly is a process that took a decade to develop, a machine with one maker, a component that requires two years of qualification, an interconnection study governed by regulatory sequence, or a customer's habit. Where money cannot compress the timetable, the holder of the asset sets the terms, and the accounts show it.

They show it in a consistent way, which is what makes the insight practical rather than decorative. The accounting signature of bottleneck ownership is the same wherever the bottleneck sits:

- Gross margin that persists at a level competitors would find attractive, over several years rather than several quarters.
- Rationing by allocation rather than price — a queue instead of a discount.
- Customers financing the supplier: prepayments, deposits, long-term commitments and, in the current cycle, equity.
- Backlogs measured against production capacity rather than against last year's revenue.
- Buyers integrating backwards into the constrained asset, which is the surest sign they believe the constraint is real.

Every one of those is observable from published documents. None requires access, a model, or a view about the technology. That is the point of reading the accounts rather than the announcements: the announcements describe intentions, and intentions are cheap; the notes describe commitments, and commitments are expensive.

## Reading the durable insight backwards

There is a second, less comfortable use for the same idea, and it applies to whichever scenario turns out to describe 2030.

If margin clusters at the bottleneck, then margin *anywhere else* is temporary by construction. That is not a criticism of the companies earning it. It is an observation about where they will have to stand when the constraint moves. A company earning well because it holds the scarce thing is in a different position from a company earning well because the scarce thing has not yet been priced properly by the market — and from the outside, in a single year's accounts, those two look identical.

Distinguishing them takes time series rather than snapshots. Does the margin survive a period when the supplier is not sold out? Does the customer concentration fall, or does the concentration simply change names? Does the backlog convert to revenue, or does it get restructured? Those are questions a reader can answer with three years of filings and no special access at all.

The same logic disciplines any statement about 2030. The question worth carrying is not "which of these three will happen". It is: *what is scarce now, who owns it, what would have to become plentiful for that to end, and how long would making it plentiful take?* The last clause is the one that does most of the work, because in this industry the answer is usually measured in years and is usually published — in capital expenditure guidance, in equipment order books, in construction schedules and in queue statistics.

## Where this leaves the reader

Everything above is a set of tools, not a set of predictions. Nobody writing in 2026 knows which of these worlds arrives, and a reader should be sceptical of anyone who claims otherwise — including of any temptation to read this chapter's three scenarios as a hidden ranking. They are not ranked. They are constructed to be equally arguable, because a scenario you cannot argue for is not doing its job.

What can be said with more confidence is narrower and more useful. Much of the physical stack of 2030 has already been committed, by companies that had to decide before they knew. Those commitments are disclosed. The constraint that binds this decade will be visible in the accounts of whoever owns it, in gross margins that do not erode, in order books that stretch past the planning horizon, and in customers willing to pay before delivery. And when the constraint moves — as it has already moved once, from logic to memory to packaging to power — the same signature will appear somewhere else, on somebody else's balance sheet.

A reader who can find that signature does not need a forecast. That is what the final chapters of this book are for: turning the habit into a short, repeatable set of checks, and then being honest about what even the best-read set of accounts cannot tell anyone.
