# What the Numbers Cannot Tell You

Someone types a question into a chat box at eleven at night, and an answer begins arriving before the sentence has finished being read. This book has spent its length following the money underneath that transaction — down through the application that took the payment, the model that produced the answer, the data centre that ran it and the fab that made the machine possible — and there is one thing none of it established. Nothing in any filing quoted in these pages records whether the answer was any good.

That is not an oversight. It is the boundary of the method, and it is worth stating plainly at the end, because a book that reads accounts for four hundred pages can leave the impression that accounts are where the truth lives. They are not. They are where one particular kind of truth lives, recorded to a standard, examined by somebody with a professional obligation, and strictly limited in what it covers.

## What the book did, in one paragraph

It followed invoices. Who billed whom, for what, on what terms, and which of them was left holding an asset. It organised the industry on four floors and argued, with the margins as evidence, that value has been moving down them rather than up. Every figure it used carried a date and the name of whoever published it, because in an industry that can add or remove half a trillion dollars of market value in an afternoon — Nvidia lost about 589 billion US dollars of market value in the session of 27 January 2025 (CNBC and Bloomberg, 27 January 2025), and closed above five trillion for the first time on 29 October 2025 (CNBC and Forbes, 29 October 2025) — an undated number is not information.

That discipline was not decoration. It was the only way to write a fixed object about a moving one without misleading anybody. But the discipline has a cost that is easy to miss while it is working: it makes the analysis look more complete than it is. A page dense with dated, sourced, checkable figures reads like a page that has settled something. Most of the time it has settled a narrow thing very well and left the wide thing open.

## An audited account is a record of the past

Begin with the strongest document in the entire book, and be precise about what it is.

A set of audited financial statements is a record of transactions that have already occurred, prepared under an accounting framework, and examined by an auditor who then expresses an opinion. The opinion is that the statements give a true and fair view — or, in the American formulation, present fairly in all material respects — of the position and results, in accordance with that framework. Three words in that sentence do most of the limiting work.

*Material.* The audit is not a count of every transaction. It is designed to catch misstatements large enough to change a reasonable user's decisions, and it says so.

*Reasonable.* An audit provides reasonable assurance, not absolute assurance. That is the standard's own language, not a hedge invented here. An audit is a sampling exercise conducted against a threshold, not a guarantee.

*Framework.* The figures are what the framework says they are. Revenue is what the revenue standard recognises, not what a lay reader might mean by money received. Cost of revenue contains what the company has decided belongs there, within the rules, and different companies decide differently.

Set against that, notice what the opinion never asserts. It does not say the business is well run. It does not say the strategy will work. It does not say the assets will still be worth what they cost. It does not say the company will still exist in five years, other than in the narrow going-concern sense of the next twelve months. It is an opinion about a document, given by someone who is not permitted to give an opinion about the business.

| What people think an audit opinion says | What it says |
|---|---|
| The company is sound | The statements are free from material misstatement |
| The numbers are exact | The numbers are right within a materiality threshold |
| Everything was checked | Enough was checked to support an opinion, on a risk basis |
| The assets are worth this | The assets are carried at cost or value under the framework's rules |
| The future is safe | Nothing here addresses the future beyond the going-concern horizon |

And inside those statements, prepared to that standard and examined to that level, sit estimates. This book has returned to one of them repeatedly because it decides more reported profit in this industry than any other judgement: how long a server is expected to last.

Amazon has disclosed the estimate moving in both directions within two years, in its own annual reports. With effect from 1 January 2024 it increased the useful life of servers from five years to six, a change it disclosed as reducing depreciation and amortisation expense by 3.2 billion dollars and increasing net income by 2.5 billion dollars for the year ended 31 December 2024. With effect from 1 January 2025 it reduced the life of a subset of servers and networking equipment from six years back to five, disclosed with its fourth-quarter 2024 results in February 2025 as expected to reduce 2025 operating income by roughly 0.7 billion dollars in respect of assets already held, with a full-year effect of a 1.4 billion dollar increase in depreciation and amortisation and a 1.0 billion dollar reduction in net income, mainly in the cloud segment. The reason the company gave for the reversal was the pace of technological change in artificial intelligence and machine learning.

Every one of those figures was disclosed, quantified and audited. That is the system working exactly as designed, and nothing improper is alleged or implied. The point is narrower and more useful: profit in the largest cloud businesses on earth moves by billions of dollars because a forecast about wear-out was revised, and the forecast concerns years that have not happened yet. An audited profit figure is therefore a historical record that contains, embedded in it, somebody's opinion about the future. The auditor tests whether that opinion is reasonable. Nobody can test whether it is right, because the evidence does not exist yet.

## Three things the numbers settle unusually well

The limits are real, and they are not a reason to stop reading filings. There are questions the accounts answer better than any other available source, and this book leaned on them for that reason.

**Whether the cash arrived.** The cash flow statement is the hardest of the three primary statements to dress up, because it reconciles to a bank balance. Profit rising while cash from operations does not is the oldest warning sign in the trade, and it is visible to anyone who can read two lines. Nvidia's fiscal year 2026, which ended on 25 January 2026, produced revenue of 215.9 billion US dollars, up 65 per cent, with net income of 120.1 billion, per the company's results announcement of February 2026 and its annual report for that year. Those are collected sums for a closed period. They will not be revised by sentiment.

**Where the scarcity sits.** Gross margin is the most informative single line in this industry, because it measures how much of the price the seller keeps, which is a direct reading of how substitutable the thing is. The gap this book kept returning to — roughly 71 per cent GAAP gross margin at Nvidia for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026, against a reported figure of around 44 per cent at Anthropic and around 39 per cent at OpenAI for early 2026 periods, the latter two unaudited and reported through the press rather than filed — is too wide to be a definitional artefact.

**Where the commitments are.** Capital expenditure, purchase commitments, remaining performance obligations and customer concentration are all disclosed, and all describe obligations that already bind. Nvidia's annual report for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026 disclosed two direct customers each exceeding ten per cent of total revenue, at 22 per cent and 14 per cent. That is a fact about the shape of a business, not an accusation, and it cannot be obtained any other way.

Those three are the analytical core of the book, and they are genuinely durable. What follows is what sits outside them.

## A valuation is a statement about expectation

The second family of numbers in this book is entirely different in kind, and confusing the two families is the commonest error in commentary about the industry.

A market capitalisation is the price of the last trade multiplied by every share in issue. It is not money the company holds, it is not a measurement of anything that has happened, and it is not the result of a calculation anyone can audit. It is the aggregated opinion of the people trading that day about what will happen next, expressed as a price. Nvidia stood at roughly 5.3 to 5.4 trillion US dollars as at mid-August 2026 on the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings, with Apple at roughly 4.41 trillion, Alphabet roughly 4.20 trillion and Microsoft roughly 3.66 trillion over the same period and from the same sources. Those figures have moved since. They were opinions, and opinions move.

A private valuation is stranger still. Anthropic closed a 65 billion dollar Series H at a 965 billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world (CNBC, May 2026). OpenAI closed a 122 billion dollar round in March 2026 at an 852 billion dollar valuation, with Amazon reported as investing 50 billion dollars in that round (reporting collated by valueaddvc and Forbes during 2026). Each of those headline numbers is the price of one class of shares, agreed in one negotiation, on terms that are not published, multiplied as though every share were identical. They frequently are not identical, because later investors commonly hold preferences and protections that ordinary shareholders do not.

So the valuation numbers describe expectation: what a set of people, at a moment, believed about a future. They are useful precisely because they are the only forward-looking figure in the system that anybody publishes. They are also the least anchored, because there is no procedure by which they can be shown to be wrong until afterwards.

Here is the consequence that matters for the end of a book like this one. The accounts and the valuations cannot arbitrate between each other. A high multiple is not evidence that the accounts understate the business, and strong accounts are not evidence that the multiple is deserved. They are answers to two different questions — what happened, and what people expect — and the space between them is exactly where every argument in the last part of this book lived. Two competent readers looked at the same filings and reached opposite conclusions in the two chapters on the bubble question, and neither was being careless. They differed on one variable that is disclosed nowhere, because it has not happened yet.

## Neither of them describes capability

Now the harder limit, and the one that ought to be uncomfortable for a book that has spent its length on line items.

The central question about this industry is not financial. It is whether these systems become substantially more capable, how fast, at what cost, and whether the resulting capability is worth what is being spent to obtain it. Not one figure in this book measures that.

Research and development expense measures spending on research, which is an input. A company can spend twenty billion dollars and produce nothing anybody wants; the accounts will record the twenty billion faithfully and be silent on the nothing. Headcount measures employment. Capital expenditure measures buildings and machines. Gross margin measures how much of the price the seller keeps, which moves for reasons that have nothing to do with quality — a price rise, a change in customer mix, a cheaper generation of accelerators, a fall in the cost of serving each request.

Revenue is the closest thing to a capability signal the accounts contain, because customers pay for things they find useful, and they pay repeatedly for things they keep finding useful. That is a real signal and it should not be dismissed. But it lags by quarters, it is contaminated by switching costs and enterprise contract cycles, and in a market growing this fast it is dominated by how many new customers arrived rather than whether the product improved. Revenue tells you a purchase was made. It does not tell you whether the purchaser was right.

Benchmark scores are not a solution to this either, and they are not accounts. They are published by parties with an interest in the result, measured on tests that the systems are increasingly trained in the vicinity of, and revised constantly. This book has not used them as evidence, and the reason is the same reason it dates every financial figure: a number without a defined, stable, independently checkable basis is not evidence, whatever it is a number about.

There is a timing problem underneath this as well. Financial statements describe a period that has closed, and they arrive weeks or months after it closed. In an industry where the state of the art moves within a quarter and capacity is committed years ahead, the most current audited document available is a description of a position that no longer exists. That is not a criticism of the reporting regime, which exists to be accurate rather than fast. It is a reason not to expect the accounts to answer a question about the present, let alone about a technology's trajectory.

Nor can the participants settle it, which is worth saying because their statements are often treated as though they could. A company guiding capital expenditure upward is telling you what it has decided to spend, which is genuine information about its own conviction and about the order books of its suppliers. It is not telling you that the spending will be justified, because it does not know. Guidance is a forecast made by people with an interest in being believed, disclosed under rules that require it to be given honestly and do not require it to be right.

So the honest position is this. The accounts describe money that changed hands in a period that has closed. The valuations describe what people expected while they were trading. Neither of them, separately or together, describes whether the technology at the centre of all this expenditure will do what its buyers hope. That question was never inside the scope of financial analysis, and no amount of care with filings brings it inside.

## What this book could not find out

There is a second category of limit, more mundane and equally worth stating: things that are financial, that would settle real arguments, and that nobody discloses. The book said so where it happened rather than reaching for a plausible figure, and the list is short enough to gather here.

| Question | Why it matters | Status |
|---|---|---|
| What a specific cloud pays for a specific accelerator | Determines how the margin actually splits between the bottom two floors | Not disclosed by any party |
| What it costs to serve one request from a frontier model | The single most important unit economic in the model layer | Not disclosed |
| Which end operators sit behind a chipmaker's disclosed direct customers | Concentration disclosures cover direct customers; distributors and system builders sit in between | Disclosed only in outline |
| The rights and preferences attached to a private round | Determines what a headline valuation actually values | Terms are not published |
| Utilisation of installed accelerator fleets | Distinguishes capacity that earns from capacity that idles | Not disclosed by operators |

Some of that will change. Disclosure practice tends to follow public interest, slowly. Most of it will not, because the information is commercially sensitive to everyone who holds it, and because no rule requires it.

A reader who wants those numbers will find plenty of sources willing to supply them. What those sources are supplying is a model, an inference or an estimate, and the useful habit is to establish which before deciding what weight to give it. An industry estimate with a named house and a stated definition behind it is a legitimate object. The same figure detached from both is a rumour with a decimal point.

## Reading, inference, judgement

Most confusion in public argument about this industry is one of three operations being mistaken for another. Separating them is the practical residue of the whole book.

| Operation | What it is | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Reading | Stating what a document says, with its date and source | Fails on transcription, on misread period ends, on quoting a summary rather than the filing |
| Inference | Deriving something the document implies but does not state | Fails silently, because the reasoning is usually not shown |
| Judgement | Deciding what any of it means for a decision | Cannot fail visibly at all, until the outcome arrives |

Reading is checkable by anybody with the document. Inference is checkable only if the chain is written out — which is why this book flagged its own inferences, including the observation that mapping a chipmaker's disclosed direct-customer percentages onto named cloud operators is an inference and not a reading. Judgement is not checkable in advance by anyone, including the person exercising it.

The three are not ranked. Judgement is not a lesser activity that better data would eliminate; it is the activity the data exists to inform. What the data does is narrow the range within which judgement operates, and make explicit which assumption a disagreement actually turns on. That is a large service and it is not the same as an answer.

## An illustrative case: one filing, two conclusions

Take an illustrative cloud operator, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It spends thirty billion dollars on accelerators and the buildings to hold them in a single year, and depreciates them over five years, giving an annual charge of six billion. It reports operating profit of ten billion.

Two readers open the same statements.

The first reader observes that the machines in question are being run continuously at high utilisation, that each new generation is materially faster per unit of power, and that a five-year life for equipment in that condition is optimistic. On a three-year life, the annual charge would be ten billion instead of six, and the reported operating profit of ten billion would be six. The business, on that view, is roughly forty per cent less profitable than it appears.

The second reader observes that older accelerators are not scrapped when newer ones arrive; they are moved to less demanding work, where they earn a lower rate for several more years. On that view a five-year life is conservative rather than optimistic, the assets keep earning after the newest work has moved on, and the reported figure understates the economics.

Both readers have read the filing correctly. Both have made a defensible inference. They disagree because they hold different views about how long a physical asset will remain economically useful in a market whose demands are changing — and that is a forecast, not a disclosure. No further work in the accounts resolves it. The accounts contain the estimate, quantified and audited; they do not contain the future against which the estimate will be tested. Illustrative figures throughout this example, invented for teaching and belonging to no company.

This is the ordinary condition of financial analysis, not a special weakness of this industry. What is unusual here is the size of the assets and the speed of the technical change, which widens the gap between the two readers until it becomes the whole argument.

## The judgements this book made

A book that spends its length insisting on the difference between evidence and opinion owes an account of its own opinions. There are four worth naming.

The four-layer frame is a choice, not a discovery. Application, model, compute, semiconductor is a filing system that made the argument tractable. It leaves out the memory makers, the power industry, the networking vendors and the assemblers as layers in their own right, and it cuts across corporate boundaries, since no important company in this industry sits on one floor only. A different frame — by geography, by customer, by capital structure — would have produced a different book with different emphases and some different conclusions.

The central argument is bounded, and the book has tried to keep the boundary visible. Value has moved down the stack during this cycle, for reasons that can be read in margins and in the location of scarcity. That is a description of a period, supported by dated figures. It is not a law, it has reversed in other industries, and the part of the book on cycles exists to describe what would make it reverse again. Whether it will is not something the accounts can tell anybody.

The book preferred filed disclosure to reported disclosure wherever both existed, and said which was which. That is a defensible bias and it has a cost: the model layer is the least documented part of the industry and therefore gets the most cautious treatment, which is not the same as the least important part of the industry getting proportionate space.

It also used unaudited figures at all, which was a choice with two sides. The largest companies in the model layer file nothing, and a book that admitted only filed figures would have had almost nothing to say about them — which would have been a cleaner rule and a less useful book. The compromise was to quote what a named source reported, on a stated date, and to label it as reported rather than filed every time, never comparing it directly with an audited figure as though the two were the same species. Readers who think that compromise too permissive have a fair point, and the labels are there so that those readers can discount the passages concerned without having to trust the author's weighting.

And the book declined to give a verdict on the question most readers arrive with. Two chapters, the same facts, opposite conclusions. That refusal was deliberate, and it follows from everything above: the variable the two cases differ on is not disclosed by anyone, and a book that cannot check a claim has no business making it.

## This book will be wrong in places

A work containing several thousand dated figures, drawn from filings, results releases, trade reporting and industry estimates, will contain errors. Some will be transcription. Some will be figures that were correct as published and later restated by the company. Some will be estimates from named houses that those houses subsequently revised. Some will be sources that were reporting in good faith on information that turned out to be wrong.

The dating convention was adopted partly for this reason. A figure stamped with the moment it was observed and the name of whoever published it can be checked, and if it is wrong the error is locatable. A figure written without either is not wrong so much as unfalsifiable, which is a worse condition for a book to be in.

The structural explanations can be wrong too, and that is the more consequential failure mode. It is possible that leading-edge lithography acquires a second credible supplier, that the software switching costs at the bottom of the stack erode faster than a decade of accumulation suggests, that inference economics change enough to move the scarcity upward, or that the demand underneath all of this arrives more slowly than the construction schedules assume. Each of those would falsify part of the argument here, and each would become visible first in a specific, public place — an order book, a segment note, a depreciation policy, a capital expenditure revision, a grid interconnection queue. The claims in this book were written to be testable in that way rather than to be safe.

## The part that is not in the accounts

Which brings the book back to where it started, and to the only thing left to say.

Everything in these pages is a description of things that have already happened, priced by people who were guessing about what comes next. The accounts are the record of the past. The valuations are the record of expectation. Neither is a measurement of what the technology will turn out to be able to do, and no combination of the two produces one.

Between that material and any actual decision there is a gap, and nothing fills it except the judgement of whoever is making the decision. That is true of an investor deciding what to own, and it is equally true of the many readers of a book like this who will never buy a share in any of these companies: an engineer choosing which platform to build a decade of work on, a finance director signing a multi-year enterprise commitment, a utility planner weighing an interconnection request, a graduate choosing an industry, a board deciding whether to spend on capacity that arrives in 2029. Each of those is a decision under uncertainty that the numbers inform and do not make.

Nobody countersigns it. Not the auditor, whose opinion concerns a document and says so. Not the analyst, whose forecast is a professional opinion with a date on it. Not this book, which contains no price target, no recommendation to buy or sell anything, no prediction of what any company will be worth, and is not investment advice — a position stated in its first chapter and held to throughout, for the plain reason that a prediction is a figure with a date in the future and no source at all.

So the honest summary of what has been offered here is modest. A way of organising an industry into four floors. A method for reading the accounts of each of them, in the order an accountant reads them. A discipline about dates and sources that survives the figures going stale. An inventory of what is disclosed, what is estimated, and what nobody publishes. And a reasonably careful account of where the arguments actually diverge, which is usually one assumption deeper than the argument appears to be about.

The question was typed at eleven at night. The answer arrived, assembled by a model, computed in a building on the other side of the world, on a device printed by a machine that only one company knows how to build. Four floors, four sets of accounts, four different claims on the same twenty dollars. Who was paid, and how much, and on what terms, is a matter of record, and this book has tried to set the record out with its dates attached.

What any of it is worth is a different question, and it was always in the hands of the person reading.
