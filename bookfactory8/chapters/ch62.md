# The Map

On 18 November 2025, three companies made a single joint announcement. Microsoft agreed to invest up to five billion US dollars in Anthropic. Nvidia agreed to invest up to ten billion. Anthropic committed to purchase thirty billion dollars of Azure compute capacity, and to an initial commitment of up to one gigawatt of Nvidia's Grace Blackwell and Vera Rubin systems. Anthropic's valuation was put in the range of three hundred and fifty billion dollars, against about one hundred and eighty-three billion the previous September. All of that is from the companies' own announcements of 18 November 2025 and CNBC's and GeekWire's reporting of them the same day.

Count the roles in that paragraph. Microsoft is a shareholder of Anthropic and its cloud supplier, and it is also the largest shareholder of Anthropic's closest competitor. Nvidia is a shareholder of Anthropic and the ultimate supplier of the chips Anthropic will rent from Microsoft, chips Microsoft bought from Nvidia. Anthropic is the investee of two of its own suppliers and the customer of both. One announcement, three companies, and at least seven distinct commercial relationships between them, several running in opposite directions at once.

That is not an anomaly. It is the ordinary condition of this industry, and the previous chapters in this part examined the relationships one pair at a time. This chapter puts them on a single sheet. It is a map: who owns stakes in whom, who supplies whom, and where the same name appears three times in three different roles. It is also, unavoidably, a photograph of a moving object, so it begins with the thing every map needs and most published versions of this one lack.

## The date on this map

This map is drawn as at August 2026.

That sentence is doing more work than it appears to. Every line on it carries its own date, and those dates range from September 2023 to August 2026. A stake reported in a court filing in 2025 has not been re-confirmed since. A carrying value is precise to a single balance sheet date and wrong the next morning. An announced commitment of "up to" a sum may never be drawn. The map is therefore not one photograph but a collage of photographs taken over three years, assembled on one page, with the newest ink in August 2026.

The structure of it — four layers, five kinds of line, the same companies occupying several boxes — has been stable for about two years and will probably be recognisable for some time. The numbers have a half-life of roughly a quarter. By the time this is read, several will be wrong by amounts larger than the annual revenue of most listed companies, and at least one relationship described here will have been renegotiated.

The last section of this chapter explains how to redraw it. That is the section that will still be useful when the rest has aged.

## Five kinds of line

The commonest error in published versions of this map is to draw every connection as the same arrow. They are not the same. Five distinct kinds of relationship run between these companies, they behave differently, they are disclosed in different places, and in a downturn they fail in different ways.

| Line type | What it is | Where it is disclosed | How it behaves under stress |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equity | A stake, held as shares, preferred stock, convertible notes or warrants | Investment note, other income, accounting policy note; a 13F where one is filed | Marked down; non-cash; symmetric with the way it was marked up |
| Supply | Goods or services actually sold and delivered | Revenue, segment note; rarely broken out by counterparty | Volume falls; revenue falls with it |
| Purchase commitment | A promise to buy, over years, sometimes take-or-pay | Commitments and contingencies note, at the back | Binds through the downturn; may become onerous |
| Licence and distribution | Rights to use, resell or launch first | Occasionally in the narrative; almost never quantified | Renegotiated quietly; visible only in behaviour |
| Backstop and guarantee | An undertaking to buy, fund or absorb if someone else does not | Commitments note; sometimes only in an announcement | Crystallises precisely when everything else is failing |

Chapter fifty-three set out why the equity line in particular cannot be read by percentage alone: what matters is what the holder can do, and that is written in documents outsiders do not see. The point to carry into this chapter is narrower. An arrow that stands for a signed multi-year purchase obligation and an arrow that stands for a press release saying "up to" are not comparable objects, and drawing them identically is how the confusion starts.

## Who is on the map, layer by layer

The book's four layers organise the sheet from the bottom up, and each layer has a different disclosure quality. That variation is itself part of the map.

### Silicon: the layer with the best numbers and the fewest players

The bottom of the map is the easiest to draw, because almost everyone on it is listed and files.

Nvidia was the world's most valuable company at roughly five point three to five point four trillion US dollars as at mid-August 2026, on the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings; its fiscal year 2026, ended 25 January 2026, produced revenue of two hundred and fifteen point nine billion dollars, of which one hundred and ninety-three point seven billion was data centre, per the company's February 2026 results and its Form 10-K. Broadcom was around one point six one trillion and AMD around two hundred and sixty billion in early 2026, on the statista and alpha-sense rankings. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company was around two point one seven trillion as at August 2026, per companiesmarketcap, and guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion dollars in its January 2026 results. ASML crossed five hundred billion dollars in market value for the first time in January 2026, per Reuters and Yahoo Finance.

The supply lines beneath those numbers are short and unusually well documented. Nvidia does not own fabs; it buys manufacturing from TSMC. In January 2026, coverage of TSMC's 2025 disclosures by CNBC and TechPowerUp reported that Nvidia had displaced Apple as TSMC's largest customer, at about nineteen per cent of 2025 revenue against twelve per cent the year before, with Apple at about seventeen per cent, down from twenty-two — and that TSMC's ten largest customers together accounted for about seventy-eight per cent of 2025 net revenue. TSMC in turn buys its lithography from ASML, which has no substitute at the leading edge.

Alongside logic sits memory, and it deserves a line of its own because it is the layer's genuine bottleneck. The 2026 high-bandwidth memory market was estimated at about fifty-four point six billion dollars, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, with SK Hynix holding the largest share and capacity reported sold out through 2026 across the major suppliers, per industry analysis in 2026. SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, per CNBC in August 2026.

So the bottom of the map is a chain, not a web: equipment to foundry to chip designer, with memory feeding in from the side, and a small number of very large arrows. Concentration there is a fact anyone can read from filings, and it means a single supplier relationship at the base carries an appreciable share of the value of everything above it.

### Compute: the layer that spends

The middle-lower box holds the clouds. The four largest buyers — Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta — were on course in 2026 to spend approaching seven hundred billion dollars between them on capital expenditure, as reported by CNBC in February 2026, with other compilations of the same guidance arriving near seven hundred and twenty-five billion against a prior-year record of roughly four hundred and ten billion. Those are guidance figures, not outturns.

The results show the money arriving. Amazon Web Services revenue reached forty-two point two billion dollars in the second quarter of 2026, up thirty-seven per cent, with segment operating income of sixteen point six billion, per Amazon's results of 30 July 2026. Google Cloud reached twenty-four point eight billion in the same quarter, up eighty-two per cent, with segment operating income of eight point eight billion, per Alphabet's results of 22 July 2026.

Beside the hyperscalers sits a second population that barely existed three years ago: operators that buy accelerators, put them in buildings and rent them, without a retail business or a search engine underneath. CoreWeave, Nebius, Nscale, IREN and Firmus all appear in this part of the book because Nvidia has taken positions in them, as chapter fifty-six set out. They change the topology. Without them the compute layer is four companies; with them, it is four companies plus a fringe financed substantially by the party selling them their principal input.

### Model: the layer with the biggest numbers and no accounts

The labs sit above the clouds and are the reason the map is interesting.

Anthropic closed a sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, per CNBC in May 2026. OpenAI closed a one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round in March 2026 at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, with Amazon reported as investing fifty billion dollars in that round, per valueaddvc and Forbes in 2026. Neither company files audited accounts. Every figure attached to either of them anywhere in this book is a company statement or a press report of a private document, and chapter five explains at length why a private round price is not a market capitalisation.

The concentration of capital here is extreme even by the standards of the rest of the map. PitchBook reported in 2026 that of a record four hundred and seven billion dollars of artificial-intelligence venture funding in the first half of 2026, about half went to OpenAI and Anthropic alone.

### Application: the layer the map cannot see

The top box is where the user pays, and it is the emptiest part of the sheet.

That is not because it is small. It is because it is fragmented and mostly private, and because almost none of the money at the top is disclosed in a form that connects to the layers below. A consumer subscription to a frontier assistant has been around twenty US dollars a month, with higher business and professional tiers, per published pricing compared across vendors in 2026. From that price, nothing in any public document tells you what share reaches a model provider, what share reaches a cloud, or what share reaches a chip vendor. The chain exists; the accounting for it does not surface.

Two structural observations can be made about the application layer without inventing anything.

The first is that the model layer has begun moving up into it. Both leading laboratories have launched deployment ventures aimed at putting their models into enterprise operations, with Anthropic's applied venture reported in 2026 as having raised one and a half billion dollars for that purpose. A model company that sells implementation services competes with the applications built on it. That line belongs on the map, and it points upwards — the opposite direction from every other line here.

The second is that the top box is where the book's central argument is tested. Value has been moving down the stack, and the application layer is where the customer relationship lives, which is historically the one asset that survives everything below it changing. Whether that holds this time is not something a map drawn in August 2026 can settle.

### Investors: the box that sits beside the stack rather than in it

Finally there is a population that appears at every layer and belongs to none: capital.

Some of it is conventional venture money: Sequoia Capital and Sutter Hill Ventures backed Nvidia in 1993 — not SoftBank, a correction worth making because several popular accounts get it wrong. Some of it is a listed company operating as an investor. SoftBank acquired Arm in 2016 and retained approximately eighty-nine point nine per cent after taking it back to public markets in 2023, and it sold its entire remaining Nvidia stake in November 2025 for five point eight three billion dollars to fund investment in OpenAI and Stargate, per CNBC in November 2025. Some of it is state capital, which chapter sixty treated on its own terms.

And some of it is the chip vendor itself, which is where the map stops being a stack and becomes a web.

The row that repays the most attention is the CoreWeave backstop. An obligation to buy a customer's unsold capacity is not an investment, will not appear in any portfolio table, and does not move with any valuation. It is a contingent commitment with the character of credit support, and it strengthens the customer's position with its own lenders precisely because it comes from the party with the deepest interest in the collateral. Chapter fifty-eight examined that structure and its predecessors. On the map it is a dotted line that becomes solid at the worst possible moment.

## Where the same company appears three times

This is the section the map exists for. Below, five companies are set against the three roles that matter, using only relationships already sourced above.

| Company | As supplier | As customer | As shareholder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nvidia | Sells accelerators and systems to every cloud, every laboratory and every neocloud on this map | Buys manufacturing from TSMC, at about 19% of TSMC's 2025 revenue; buys high-bandwidth memory from a sold-out market | Holds Intel, Nokia, CoreWeave, SpaceX, OpenAI and Anthropic positions, and backstops up to $6.3bn of a customer's unsold capacity |
| Microsoft | Sells Azure to OpenAI ($250bn committed) and to Anthropic ($30bn committed) | Buys accelerators from Nvidia; licenses OpenAI's models and products through 2032 | Holds ~27% of OpenAI and up to $5bn of Anthropic |
| Amazon | Sells AWS and Trainium capacity to Anthropic, including an $11bn purpose-built campus | Buys accelerators from Nvidia alongside its own silicon | Holds Anthropic convertible notes and nonvoting preferred carried at $190.4bn at 30 June 2026, and is reported as a $50bn investor in OpenAI's March 2026 round |
| Alphabet | Sells TPUs and Google Cloud capacity to Anthropic at gigawatt scale | Buys accelerators and manufacturing capacity; competes with its own investee through Gemini | Holds about 14% of Anthropic, non-voting |
| Anthropic | Sells model access, including through the clouds of all three of its largest investors | Buys compute from Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Nvidia | Not a shareholder in its suppliers — the only major node on the map where the line runs one way |

Four of those five rows are occupied simultaneously. A single relationship can therefore appear three times in one set of accounts: as revenue in the segment note, as a commitment at the back, and as a valuation mark in other income — with no rule anywhere requiring the three to be reconciled to each other, and no way for an outside reader to do it.

Two consequences follow, and both are arithmetic rather than accusation.

The first is correlation. Where a supplier holds equity in its buyers, revenue growth and asset values stop being independent pieces of information. In a rising market the good news is counted twice: once when the customer orders, once when the customer's valuation rises. In a falling one the same double-count runs backwards. Chapter fifty-six made this point about one company; the map makes it about the industry.

The second is that the reported profits of some of the largest companies in the world now depend on the pricing of private rounds. In the second quarter of 2026 Amazon carried fifty-three point four billion dollars of non-operating pre-tax other income, primarily from its Anthropic investments, inside net income of sixty-two point six billion against operating income of twenty-seven point five billion, per its results of 30 July 2026; Alphabet carried a net gain of about ninety-eight billion in other income, mostly unrealised marks on equity holdings, inside net income of about one hundred and twelve billion against operating income of forty point eight billion, per its results of 22 July 2026 and coverage by Fortune and CNBC. Microsoft's fiscal 2026 gains on its OpenAI investment added four thousand nine hundred and sixty-three million dollars to full-year net income and sixty-seven cents to diluted earnings per share, per its materials of 29 July 2026.

CNBC reported on 3 August 2026 that these stakes had begun distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them, and carried the index-level figure: earnings growth for the S&P 500 in that quarter topped forty-eight per cent year on year, and excluding the investment gains was closer to twenty-nine per cent. A private funding round moved the reported earnings growth of the largest equity index in the world by nineteen percentage points in a quarter.

In a smaller box that is easy to miss: Microsoft's fiscal 2026 related-party disclosure recorded twenty-four point one billion dollars of revenue from commercial arrangements with OpenAI, inclusive of revenue share, and a six billion dollar receivable at the year end, against total revenue of three hundred and thirty-one point eight billion, per the annual report as reported by Neowin and Techzine in 2026. Roughly seven per cent of the revenue of one of the largest companies on earth arrives from a single private counterparty it also part-owns.

## What the map cannot show

An honest map marks its blank areas rather than filling them.

Price and terms are missing everywhere. Not one of the supply agreements above has been published. The rate behind the two hundred and fifty billion dollar Azure commitment, its year-by-year schedule, and whether it is take-or-pay are not disclosed. Nor is the split of Microsoft's twenty-four point one billion dollars between cloud services and revenue share — analysts said so publicly when the filing appeared.

Counterparty identity is missing at the bottom. Nvidia's concentration disclosure identifies customers only as letters: for fiscal 2026 as a whole, two direct customers at twenty-two and fourteen per cent of revenue. Whether any of those letters corresponds to a company in its own investment portfolio is not disclosed, and no amount of reasoning from outside establishes it. When the answer to a question is that the filings do not say, that is the answer.

The application layer is largely missing, for the reasons given above, and audited accounts are missing for the two largest nodes in the model layer. Motive is missing entirely: disclosure rules exist to make positions and their effects visible, not to explain why a decision was taken. That is a boundary rather than a failing, and the difference between analysis and speculation is knowing where it sits.

One further caution about any version of this map, including this one. The same dollar can appear in several boxes: capital invested by a cloud in a laboratory returns to the cloud as revenue when the laboratory buys compute, and part of it continues down to the chip vendor when the cloud buys accelerators. Adding the numbers on this page together produces a figure that means nothing. The map shows direction and relationship. It is not a balance sheet of the industry, and no such balance sheet exists.

## How to redraw it yourself

This is the part worth keeping. Everything above can be rebuilt from public documents by anyone, in an afternoon, with no special access. Here is the procedure.

**Write the date at the top first.** Not the date of the newest fact, but the date you are drawing. Then write the individual date beside every single line as you add it. A map without dates on the lines is decoration.

**Mark each line with the quality of its evidence.** Three tiers are enough: filed, announced, reported. A figure from an audited annual report is not the same object as a figure from a press release, which is not the same object as a percentage attributed to people familiar with the matter. Keep them visually distinct and never average across them.

**For every listed company on the sheet, open the most recent annual and quarterly filings and go to five places.** The other income line and the note beneath it, for the equity marks. The investment or equity securities note, for what is held and on what basis. The accounting policy note, for which of the three regimes in chapter fifty-three applies. The commitments and contingencies note, at the back, for the purchase obligations and backstops that never make headlines. And the segment note, for where the revenue actually sits.

**Add the related-party note.** Its presence tells you a threshold was crossed. Its absence tells you one was not, which is informative in a different way.

**Read the 13F of anyone who files one, and read the exits.** Positions dropped are a statement of direction and get roughly a hundredth of the coverage of positions added.

**Take both ends of every announcement.** Each relationship has two parties and usually two press releases, and they emphasise different things. Where they disagree, note the disagreement on the map rather than resolving it.

**Separate committed, funded and carried, always.** These three numbers are routinely reported as if they were one. They are not. A ceiling of up to thirty-three billion dollars, about thirteen billion of cash actually in, and a carrying value of one hundred and ninety point four billion dollars are three different facts about one relationship, and each answers a different question: what was negotiated, what was paid, and what somebody else's most recent round says it is now worth.

**Check the merger-control registers.** Regulators publish their reasoning, and it describes these structures in more detail than the parties do. The findings are about the arrangements as they stood, not licences for whatever comes next.

**Finally, do the five-minute arithmetic that settles most arguments.** For each listed company, take pre-tax profit for the last eight quarters, subtract the unrealised investment gains and losses, and plot both series. If the adjusted line has a different shape from the headline line, the headline is partly an investment story rather than an operating one. Then find cash flow from operations and capital expenditure, neither of which a valuation mark can touch.

## The half-life of this page

To see how quickly the ink dries and cracks, take four lines already on the map and read them as a sequence rather than as facts.

Anthropic was valued at about one hundred and eighty-three billion dollars in September 2025, in the range of three hundred and fifty billion in November 2025, and at nine hundred and sixty-five billion in May 2026, per CNBC's reporting on each occasion. Amazon's carrying value for its holding was near seventy-four billion dollars at 31 March 2026 and one hundred and ninety point four billion at 30 June 2026, on the Motley Fool's August 2026 reading of the filings — a change driven by a funding round at another company. Nvidia's Intel position was five billion dollars of stock in September 2025 and about thirty billion in the August 2026 13F. And Nvidia's intention towards OpenAI moved from a non-binding letter of intent for up to one hundred billion dollars in September 2025 to a reported settled position of roughly thirty billion in 2026 — seventy billion dollars between what was announced and what was done.

Four lines, eleven months, and not one of them where it started. That is the argument for the redrawing procedure rather than for memorising the map.

What does not move is the structure. Manufacturing capacity at the bottom is concentrated in a very small number of hands and takes years to add. The clouds sit between the chip vendors and the laboratories and carry the capital risk for both. The laboratories consume most of what they earn in compute and are funded substantially by the companies that sell it to them. The application layer, where the end user actually pays, is the thinnest part of the public record and the part the layers below are all reaching towards. Around all four, a small number of investors hold minority positions almost everywhere and control almost nothing.

Nothing in this chapter is investment advice, no view is offered on any company's shares, and nothing here alleges improper conduct by anyone named. Every relationship described has been disclosed by the parties or reported by a named outlet on a stated date. The claim is only this: an industry in which suppliers own their customers, customers pre-commit to their suppliers, and rivals fund the same laboratory cannot be read one company at a time. It has to be read as a map — and a map is only worth what the dates on it are worth.
