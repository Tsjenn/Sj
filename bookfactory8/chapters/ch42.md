# The Chinese Labs

On Monday 27 January 2025, Nvidia lost roughly 589 billion US dollars of market value in a single trading session — at the time the largest one-day loss any listed company had ever recorded — with the shares closing down about seventeen per cent, per Yahoo Finance and CNBC coverage of that day. The cause was not a filing, a profit warning or a product recall. It was a chatbot app from a laboratory in Hangzhou that most of the market had never heard of, attached to a research paper claiming that a frontier-class model had been trained for a few million dollars on chips the United States had deliberately made second-rate.

Almost everything about that sentence deserves scrutiny, and this chapter will scrutinise it. But the day itself tells you why the Chinese labs matter to a book about money. They are the only part of the model layer whose output has moved the bottom of the stack by half a trillion dollars before lunch. Western coverage tends to handle them badly in one of two directions — either as a menace about to sweep the field, or as a propaganda exercise running on smuggled hardware. The accounts, such as they exist, support neither story. What they support is stranger and more interesting: a group of capable laboratories operating under a hard ceiling on the layer beneath them, and adapting to that ceiling in ways that have changed the economics of the whole industry.

A note before the numbers start. More of this chapter's figures are unverifiable than in any other chapter of this book, because more of this industry segment is private, undisclosed or contested. Where a number cannot be checked, the text says so. That is not a failure of research. It is the finding.

## Who the labs actually are

The phrase "Chinese AI labs" covers at least three different kinds of organisation, and collapsing them into one is the first mistake most coverage makes.

The first kind is the divisions of the giants. Alibaba's Qwen model family, ByteDance's and Baidu's model efforts and Tencent's equivalents sit inside enormous listed or privately held groups, funded from group cash flow, with the model business invisible inside consolidated accounts. Alibaba is the clearest case, because it has attached public money to the effort: in February 2025 the group announced it would invest at least 380 billion yuan — about 53 billion US dollars — in AI and cloud infrastructure over three years, per the company's announcement, and by the quarter ended June 2026 roughly 190 billion yuan of that had already been spent, with cloud and AI revenue up forty-five per cent year on year, the fastest in twenty-two quarters, and quarterly profit down about seventy-five per cent under the weight of the spending, per the company's August 2026 results as reported by Reuters and the South China Morning Post. That is what the strategy looks like from inside audited accounts: real revenue growth, bought with real margin.

The second kind is the venture-backed startups, the group Chinese media took to calling the "AI tigers": Zhipu AI, Moonshot AI (maker of the Kimi models), MiniMax and a handful of others. For most of their lives these were private companies with no published accounts, valued only by their funding rounds — exactly the class of company Chapter five taught you to read with gloves on. Then, in January 2026, two of them did something no American frontier lab has done: they listed. Zhipu AI debuted on the Hong Kong exchange on 8 January 2026, raising about 4.35 billion Hong Kong dollars and rising a modest thirteen per cent on debut to a value near nine billion US dollars; MiniMax followed on 9 January, raised about 4.8 billion Hong Kong dollars — roughly 620 million US dollars — and closed its first day up 109 per cent at a market value of about 13.7 billion US dollars, per CNBC and the South China Morning Post in January 2026. Those market capitalisations are snapshots and will have moved; nothing in this paragraph is a comment on whether either share is worth owning, and this book does not give that kind of advice. The point is structural: two Chinese model companies now publish audited figures under listing rules, which makes them, by the standard of this book, more legible than OpenAI or Anthropic.

The third kind is DeepSeek, which fits no category and is therefore worth its own paragraphs. DeepSeek was founded in 2023 by Liang Wenfeng as an offshoot of High-Flyer, a quantitative hedge fund he co-founded in 2016, per Forbes profiles published in January 2025; Bloomberg reported in January 2026 that the associated fund management business oversees more than seventy billion yuan, around ten billion US dollars. The lab was born, in other words, out of a trading firm that had already accumulated a large cluster of Nvidia accelerators for its own quantitative research and could fund model training from proprietary trading profit. It sells API access cheaply, gives its model weights away, publishes unusually detailed engineering papers, and discloses no accounts of any kind. Its revenue is not public. Its ownership terms are not public. Its chip inventory is not public. Every claim about DeepSeek's economics traces back either to its own papers or to reporting of varying quality, and this chapter will keep the two separate.

The table is the chapter in miniature. The most influential lab on the list is the least legible, and the two most legible are not the most influential. Keep that inversion in mind whenever a headline states a fact about "China's AI industry" with confidence.

## The rationed floor below

Every company in that table operates above a compute layer that is rationed by a foreign government. That is the single structural fact that separates the Chinese labs from every other participant in the model layer, and it arrived in stages.

In October 2022, the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security imposed export controls on advanced computing chips and semiconductor manufacturing equipment destined for China, setting performance thresholds above which a licence was required. Nvidia responded commercially, not politically: it designed the A800 and H800, variants of its flagship accelerators tuned to sit just under the thresholds, and sold them into China in volume. In October 2023 the rules were updated to capture exactly those chips, per the Georgetown CSET and CSIS analyses of the two rule sets published in 2023. Nvidia responded again with the H20, a further-reduced part built for the Chinese market under the new limits.

The controls on chips sit on top of older controls on the machines that make chips. ASML's extreme ultraviolet lithography systems — the only tools in the world that can print the finest features economically — have never been shipped to China; the Dutch government has withheld licences since 2019, per contemporaneous reporting. In September 2024 the Netherlands extended licence requirements to ASML's most capable deep ultraviolet immersion systems as well, per ASML's own statement of September 2024. So the constraint is doubled: China cannot freely buy the best accelerators, and cannot buy the best tools to make its own.

Then came 2025, the year the ration was cut, restored, taxed and finally refused.

The H20 episode in the middle of that table deserves the accountant's reading, because it produced some of the strangest line items in recent corporate history. On 9 April 2025 Nvidia was told a licence would be required to ship the H20 — a chip designed specifically to comply with the previous rules — into China. The company recorded a 4.5 billion US dollar charge in its quarter ended April 2025 for excess H20 inventory and purchase obligations, and guided that the following quarter would lose about eight billion dollars of H20 revenue, per its results announcement of May 2025. Read that charge for what it is: inventory written down not because it failed, not because demand vanished, but because a rule changed between fabrication and shipment. There is no line in a standard risk disclosure that quite prepares a reader for "our product was made illegal to deliver while it sat in the warehouse."

Three months later the position reversed. In July 2025 Nvidia said Washington had assured it that H20 sales could resume, per CNBC; by August, licences were being issued, and Reuters and others reported an arrangement under which Nvidia and AMD would remit fifteen per cent of revenue from certain China chip sales to the US government. Whatever one thinks of the policy, note the accounting novelty: a revenue-share with the state, functioning economically like an export tariff levied on the exporter, attached to specific product lines in a specific market.

And then the customer declined. Through August 2025, Chinese regulators publicly questioned the H20's security and reportedly instructed major domestic buyers to suspend purchases, per Bloomberg and CNBC; by late August, CNBC reported Nvidia had asked suppliers to halt H20-related production. In November 2025, Reuters reported that new Chinese data-centre projects receiving state funds were barred from using foreign AI chips at all, with projects less than thirty per cent complete ordered to remove foreign hardware already installed. Nvidia's chief executive had already summarised the commercial outcome at a Citadel Securities event in October 2025: the company's share of China's AI accelerator market had gone "from 95 per cent to zero", per Fortune's report of the remarks — this from a market that produced about seventeen billion dollars of revenue, roughly thirteen per cent of the company's total, in its fiscal year ended January 2025, per the company's annual report.

Notice what happened by the end of 2025: the ration became mutual. The United States restricted what could be sold; China then restricted what could be bought. Neither side of that is advocacy in this book, and readers will hold their own views on whether either policy is wise. What matters for the analysis is that the constraint on the Chinese labs' compute layer stopped being a temporary irritation to be lobbied away and became, on both sides, policy with concrete poured on top of it.

## The parallel hardware, honestly assessed

If foreign accelerators are rationed and then refused, the labs must run on domestic ones. The flagship is Huawei's Ascend line, and the honest assessment — assembled from disclosed engineering rather than from anyone's press release — is that it is genuinely capable, genuinely behind, and expensive in exactly the dimension China can most afford.

The Ascend 910C is reported to be fabricated on SMIC's most advanced 7-nanometre-class process, achieved with older deep ultraviolet tools driven hard through multiple patterning, per SemiAnalysis's technical coverage in 2025; the same reporting, and a TechPowerUp report citing teardown investigators, found that many 910-series parts have relied on dies made earlier by TSMC, which is a reminder of how porous these boundaries have been in practice and how hard they are to audit from outside. Huawei's answer to the individual chip gap is scale: the CloudMatrix 384 system announced in 2025 wires 384 Ascend 910C processors into a single rack-scale machine. Per SemiAnalysis's April 2025 analysis, that system delivers roughly 300 petaflops of dense BF16 compute, about double Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 rack — while drawing roughly 560 kilowatts against the NVL72's roughly 145, around four times the power for under twice the output.

That power ratio is the whole strategic position in one number. Electricity is the input China has in abundance and can subsidise at will; leading-edge lithography is the input it cannot buy at any price. So the domestic stack substitutes the abundant input for the scarce one. An accountant would call it a factor-substitution problem, and would also note where the cost lands: not in the capital account, where the state can absorb it, but in the operating account of every data centre running the machines, every hour, forever.

The clearest single test of the domestic stack came from DeepSeek itself. In August 2025 the Financial Times reported, per three people familiar with the matter, that after R1's success DeepSeek had been encouraged by Chinese authorities to train its next major model on Huawei's Ascend chips; that months of training attempts failed on unstable hardware, slow interconnects and immature software, even with Huawei engineers dispatched on site; and that the company reverted to Nvidia hardware for training while adapting Ascend for inference, delaying the model's release from its planned May date. None of that is verifiable from outside — no party published logs or confirmed the account in a filing — and it should be carried as credible reporting, not fact. But it matches the engineering picture: training a frontier model is the most unforgiving workload in computing, and it is the last one a maturing chip platform conquers. Inference — serving a trained model — is more tolerant, and it is where domestic accelerators have plausibly found real work.

So the parallel stack, as of the 2026 reporting, looks like this: domestic chips increasingly mandated and increasingly used, especially for inference; frontier training still leaning on whatever restricted hardware the labs already hold or can lawfully or unlawfully obtain; and a gap that is real but narrowing in the only dimension the buyers of chips ultimately price, which is useful work per unit of money. How much restricted hardware sits inside China today — through pre-ban stockpiles, third-country routing or smuggling — has been the subject of extensive reporting and estimation. This book does not repeat any of those estimates, because not one of them rests on disclosure that can be checked. The honest statement is: material, unknown, and unknowable from outside.

## When capacity is capped, efficiency becomes the product

Now to the research consequence, which is where the Chinese labs stopped being a regional story and started rewriting the industry's cost assumptions.

A lab with unlimited compute optimises for capability and lets cost float. A lab with capped compute has no such luxury: every architectural decision is made under a binding constraint, and efficiency stops being an engineering virtue and becomes the strategy itself. The visible result is a portfolio of techniques — sparse mixture-of-experts designs that activate a small fraction of a huge model for each token, aggressive attention and precision engineering, distillation of large models into small ones — pushed harder and published more openly than anywhere else in the industry.

The numbers attached to this story are famous, and they need exact handling, because they are the most misquoted figures in the industry.

DeepSeek's V3 technical report, published in December 2024, states that the model's full training run consumed 2.788 million H800 GPU-hours, and that at an assumed rental price of two dollars per GPU-hour this amounts to 5.576 million US dollars. The report itself says, in plain text that almost nobody quoted, that this covers only the final official training run and excludes all prior research, architecture experiments and ablation studies. It is a marginal cost, priced at a hypothetical rental rate, on hardware the company already owned. In September 2025 the R1 work passed peer review at the journal Nature — reported by CNN as the first mainstream frontier model to do so — with supplementary material putting the incremental cost of R1's reasoning training at about 294,000 dollars on 512 H800 chips, again on top of a base model whose stated cost was around six million dollars.

An accountant reads those disclosures the way an accountant reads any cost figure: what is in it, what is out of it, and who chose the denominator. Out of it: research salaries, failed runs, the data pipeline, the capital cost of the cluster, and every experiment that made the successful run possible. In it: the electricity-and-rent equivalent of one clean pass. The comparison everyone drew in January 2025 — five and a half million dollars against the hundreds of millions reportedly spent on Western frontier models — set a marginal cost against a fully loaded one. It was not a lie; the paper was unusually transparent by industry standards. It was a category error committed by the readers, and it moved half a trillion dollars of market value in a day.

And yet the corrected version of the claim still stands, and still matters. Even fully loaded, the disclosed engineering shows a frontier-class model trained on export-compliant, deliberately weakened hardware for a sum that would have been a rounding error in an American lab's compute budget. In March 2025 DeepSeek published an inference-system paper claiming a theoretical cost-profit margin of 545 per cent — daily inference costs of about 87,000 dollars at the same two-dollar rental assumption against theoretical daily revenue of about 562,000 dollars — while stating in the same document that actual revenue was "substantially lower" because most usage was free or discounted, per Bloomberg and TechCrunch coverage of the March 2025 disclosure. Theoretical margins on hypothetical prices are not accounts. But the direction of the disclosure was credible and deliberate: serving intelligence is cheap and getting cheaper, and anyone pricing it as scarce should look over their shoulder.

The constraint shows up just as clearly in what the labs release. Moonshot AI's Kimi K3, whose weights were released on 27 July 2026, is reported at 2.8 trillion total parameters with only about 104 billion active per token, per CNBC's July 2026 coverage — a design that spends parameters, which are cheap to store, to save compute, which is capped. Moonshot's own published evaluations placed K3 behind the leading closed American models overall while beating them on several coding and agentic benchmarks, per the same coverage; self-reported benchmarks deserve the usual discount, but the shape of the claim — near the frontier at a fraction of the serving cost, weights free to download — is the entire Chinese model-layer strategy in one release.

## What can and cannot be known

It is worth pausing on epistemics, because this is the part of the industry where the gap between what is asserted and what is checkable yawns widest.

What can be verified from outside: the models themselves, because open weights can be downloaded, run and benchmarked by anyone with the hardware; the published papers, which are unusually detailed and have begun passing external peer review; the listed companies' accounts, from January 2026 onwards; the policy documents on both sides; and the audited financials of the foreign suppliers, which record the China business they have lost with a precision the Chinese side never matches.

What cannot: DeepSeek's revenue, costs, ownership economics or chip inventory; the true all-in training cost of any Chinese frontier model; the terms and scale of state support flowing to any individual lab; the size of restricted-hardware stockpiles; and the internal performance of Huawei's platform beyond what benchmarks and reporting reveal. Where this chapter has used the words "reported" or "per people familiar", that is a deliberate downgrade of confidence, and the reader should carry it forward. The temptation, faced with an information vacuum, is to fill it with a narrative — triumphant or dismissive, depending on the teller. The discipline this book asks of you is to leave the vacuum visible.

## A separate ecosystem, forming

Put the pieces in sequence. Export controls capped the hardware. The cap forced efficiency. Efficiency produced models cheap enough to give away, and giving them away became the distribution strategy — open weights travel where sales teams cannot, into every market and every company willing to run a free model on its own machines. Meanwhile the mutual refusal of 2025 — Washington restricting sales, Beijing restricting purchases and mandating domestic chips in state-funded data centres, per the November 2025 Reuters reporting — pushed the two stacks apart at the bottom. Domestic models increasingly run on domestic accelerators, built with domestic tools wherever the tools exist, funded by domestic capital, and listed, when they list, in Hong Kong.

That is not yet a fully separate ecosystem. The training layer still leans on foreign silicon; the tool layer beneath SMIC still cannot make what ASML will not ship; and the open-weight releases are used enthusiastically inside the systems of the very countries restricting the chips. But the direction of travel has been one way since 2022, and every policy action on either side since has accelerated it. An industry that was one stack with a contested market inside it is becoming two stacks with a narrowing interface between them.

For the reader of accounts, the practical consequences are three. First, the cost curve set in Hangzhou is now everyone's cost curve: whatever a Western model company would like to charge per token, a downloadable competitor trained under a compute ceiling bounds the price, and you should read every model-layer margin projection in that light. Second, the losses are legible even where the gains are not — the clearest audited record of this entire divergence sits in the filings of Nvidia, ASML and their peers, in charges taken and markets written to zero, and those filings will keep score of the separation more reliably than any announcement from either government. Third, everything in this chapter is a photograph. The rules of 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025 each rewrote the previous ones within about a year; the figures here will age faster than any others in this book, and the current versions live in the suppliers' latest filings and in each new rule published by the regulators themselves.

The labs were supposed to be the layer that export controls quietly strangled. Instead, constraint did what constraint does in every industry an accountant has ever audited: it forced the constrained party to discover what things actually cost. It turned out that intelligence, at the margin, costs less than almost anyone selling it wanted known. That discovery escaped the ration, because ideas are the one input no licence has ever contained — and the rest of this book's model layer has been repricing itself around it ever since.
