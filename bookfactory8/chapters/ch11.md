# The Machines Behind the Machines

A three-hundred-millimetre silicon wafer enters a leading-edge fab as a mirror-polished disc worth a few hundred dollars and leaves, three or four months later, as several hundred billion transistors. Between those two moments it does not sit under one enormous machine. It travels, in a sealed pod, between hundreds of separate machines, each of which does one narrow thing to its surface and hands it on. Most of those machines were built by four companies. Almost nobody outside the industry can name them.

That is the subject of this chapter. The layer beneath the foundry has already had one chapter to itself, because the lithography chokepoint is extreme enough to deserve it. But lithography is a minority of what a fab buys. The rest — the depositing, the etching, the cleaning, the implanting, the measuring, and eventually the testing and packaging — is a market of its own, roughly comparable in size to the whole of the model layer's revenue, and it is divided between a handful of firms in a way that would attract a competition regulator's attention in almost any other industry.

## What actually happens to a wafer

The best way to understand the equipment layer is to stop thinking of chipmaking as printing and start thinking of it as sculpture in reverse, repeated several hundred times.

A finished logic chip is a stack. Transistors at the bottom, then layer upon layer of metal wiring above them, insulated from one another, connected by vertical plugs. Nothing in that stack is carved out of the original silicon. Almost all of it is built up, one thin film at a time, and then selectively taken away again.

**Deposition** puts material on. A gas is admitted to a chamber, chemistry happens at the wafer's surface, and a film grows — sometimes a few hundred atoms thick, sometimes a few. Variants of this are given their own acronyms in the trade, and the distinctions matter enormously to the customer: chemical vapour deposition, plasma-enhanced versions of the same, atomic layer deposition where the film is grown literally one atomic layer per cycle, epitaxy where new crystal is grown in register with the crystal beneath it, and electroplating where copper is grown out of a bath. Every one of them is a different tool, and a fab needs all of them.

**Lithography** puts the pattern on. This is the step that gets the attention, and the machine that performs the exposure comes from one supplier. Less well known is that the scanner does not work alone. Before exposure, a separate machine spins on the light-sensitive coating — the photoresist — to a thickness controlled within a few atoms and bakes it. After exposure, the same machine develops the pattern. This coater-developer, or track, sits physically bolted to the scanner and hands wafers into it. It is one of the most concentrated positions in the entire supply chain, and it belongs to Tokyo Electron, which has been reported as holding around ninety to ninety-one per cent of the coater-developer market and effectively all of the EUV portion of it (industry analysis and company investor materials, 2025 and 2026; these are estimates by market researchers and by the company, not audited disclosures).

**Etch** takes material away. Where the developed resist leaves the surface exposed, a plasma — an electrically excited gas — chews down into the film beneath, and where the resist remains, the film survives. Modern etch is the step where the physical difficulty has migrated. In three-dimensional memory the tool must cut a hole hundreds of layers deep and only tens of nanometres across, perfectly straight, without wandering. Doing that repeatably is why etch has become one of the two largest equipment categories by spending.

**Cleaning** happens after almost everything else. A single particle in the wrong place destroys a die, and a fab that cannot clean cannot yield. Market researchers put the wafer cleaning equipment market at roughly six point nine billion US dollars in 2026, with SCREEN, Tokyo Electron, Applied Materials, ACM Research and Lam Research together holding an estimated sixty-five per cent of 2024 revenue (Mordor Intelligence and comparable estimates; researcher figures, not company-reported ones).

**Ion implantation** is the one step that changes the silicon itself rather than what sits on top of it. Dopant atoms are ionised, accelerated, and fired into the crystal to set its electrical behaviour. It is a small category relative to deposition and etch and an extremely concentrated one: one 2024 ranking of ion implanter sales put Applied Materials first with about sixty-three per cent and Axcelis second with about twenty-one per cent (market-research ranking of 2024 sales; segment definitions vary between researchers).

**Metrology and inspection** is the step that does nothing to the wafer at all. It measures. Is the film the right thickness, is the pattern in the right place, is there a defect, and if there is, what kind and where did it come from? A fab running a new process at poor yield is losing money on every wafer, and the only way out is to find the killer defect faster than the competition. That is a measurement problem, and it is why the process-control tools — which produce no output themselves — command some of the best margins in the industry.

Then, once the wafer is finished, two more categories take over. **Test** puts electrical probes on the dies while they are still on the wafer, and tests the packaged parts again afterwards, because a defective accelerator that reaches a customer costs far more than one caught on a tester. **Packaging** used to be the cheap end of the business — cut the wafer up, glue the die to a substrate, wire it, encapsulate it. In the AI era it has become one of the hardest steps in the flow, because a modern accelerator is not one die but several, plus stacks of memory, joined together with a precision that used to belong only to the front end.

A wafer will go around the deposit-pattern-etch-clean-measure loop several hundred times before it is done. That repetition is the whole economic point of this layer. Lithography is bought by the exposure step. Deposition, etch, clean and metrology are bought by the layer count, and layer counts have been rising for two decades.

Take them one at a time, because they are not variations on a theme.

**Applied Materials** is the broadest. It sells into more process steps than anyone else, which makes it the closest thing the industry has to a general contractor and its results the least specific read on any single technology. Fiscal 2025, which ended in October 2025, produced record revenue of twenty-eight point three seven billion dollars, up four per cent, with Semiconductor Systems at twenty point eight billion and the services arm, Applied Global Services, at six point four billion (company results announcement, November 2025, and the fiscal 2025 Form 10-K filed in December 2025). Growth of four per cent, in a year when the AI narrative was at full volume, is worth sitting with; the equipment layer was still working through the previous cycle's memory weakness while the accelerator business boomed.

The following year looked entirely different. For the quarter ended in July 2026, reported on 13 August 2026, Applied posted record revenue of nine point one one five billion dollars, up twenty-five per cent year on year, with GAAP gross margin of fifty point three per cent, record operating income of three point zero eight billion, or thirty-three point seven per cent of revenue, and GAAP earnings per share of three dollars seventeen. Semiconductor Systems set a record at seven point zero billion and services at one point eight billion, and the company guided the following quarter to ten point two five billion dollars, plus or minus five hundred million (company results announcement, August 2026).

**Lam Research** is the specialist that became a giant. Its concentration is in etch and in deposition, the two categories that scale with layer count, which made it the most exposed of the four to the memory cycle and the most rewarded when memory turned. Its fiscal year ended in June 2026 produced revenue of twenty-three point two three billion dollars against eighteen point four four billion the year before, with the June quarter alone at six point seven two billion, up thirty per cent year on year, GAAP gross margin of fifty-one point seven per cent and GAAP operating margin of thirty-seven point four per cent (company results, July 2026). Guidance for the September 2026 quarter was reported at about eight point one billion dollars from the same earnings call — that is a company forecast, not a result, and the distinction matters more in this layer than almost anywhere else in this book.

**Tokyo Electron** is the one most Western readers know least and should know best. Its coater-developer position is close to unassailable, it is a serious competitor in etch and deposition, and its results are reported in yen against a fiscal year ending in March, which makes casual comparison with the American firms harder than it looks. The year ended March 2026 was a record on the company's own account: net sales of two trillion four hundred and forty-three point five billion yen, up half a per cent, operating income of six hundred and twenty-four point nine billion yen and net income of five hundred and seventy-four point four billion yen, up five point six per cent (company results, April 2026). The guidance issued alongside those results is the more interesting number: first-half fiscal 2027 net sales of about one trillion five hundred and seventy billion yen, a thirty-three per cent increase year on year, with operating income of four hundred and thirty-one billion, and segment targets of coater-developer growth above fifty per cent, etch above twenty-five per cent and advanced packaging above sixty per cent (company guidance, April 2026).

Read those two paragraphs together. A company reports a flat year, and in the same breath guides the next half-year up by a third. That is not inconsistency. It is what the equipment layer looks like at the moment a cycle turns, and it is the reason this chapter exists.

**KLA** is the odd one out, and the most profitable. It does not add material or remove it. It looks. Inspection tools hunt for defects on a surface where the features are smaller than the wavelength of visible light; metrology tools measure dimensions and overlay. The company has told investors its process-control market share reached about fifty-eight per cent in 2025, up around three hundred and sixty basis points from 2021 (KLA investor presentation materials, reported April 2026), with third-party estimates putting its share of optical wafer inspection above eighty-five per cent. Fiscal 2026, ended 30 June 2026, produced revenue of thirteen point five eight billion dollars and GAAP net income of four point eight three billion; the June quarter set a record at three point six six billion with gross margin of sixty-two point four per cent, and the company guided September to four billion dollars, plus or minus two hundred million, at a gross margin of sixty-two point five per cent (company results, July 2026).

Sixty-two per cent gross margin, for a company whose product ships in a crate, is the number to notice. It is higher than the margin of the foundries that buy the tools, and it is earned by selling information rather than throughput.

Around these four sit others that matter and are rarely named. Advantest is the leader in testing complex logic and high-bandwidth memory; in July 2026 it raised full-year fiscal 2026 guidance to revenue of one trillion seven hundred and fourteen billion yen with operating profit of eight hundred and forty-six billion, and raised its estimate of the whole semiconductor tester market to between thirteen and fourteen and a half billion US dollars (company guidance and earnings call, July 2026). Teradyne competes in test. ASMPT and BE Semiconductor build the machines that assemble and bond packages. SCREEN leads parts of cleaning. Axcelis is the independent in implant. None of them is small, and none appears in general coverage of artificial intelligence.

## Why this layer is a quiet oligopoly

The concentration is real, but the reason for it is often described lazily, as though it were simply a matter of patents or scale. It is neither, quite.

Start with the numbers. A ranking of semiconductor equipment sales for 2023 put ASML at twenty-four per cent of the market, Applied Materials at twenty, Lam Research at eleven, Tokyo Electron at nine and KLA at seven — roughly seven-tenths of the industry between five firms, on that compilation's definitions. Other compilations put the combined figure lower, in the fifty-six to sixty-six per cent range, because they count a wider universe of equipment. Both statements can be true at once, and the gap between them is a lesson in reading market-share claims: always ask what was in the denominator.

Now the mechanism, which has four parts.

The first is qualification. A fab does not buy a tool; it qualifies a process. Getting a new etch chamber from installed to production-worthy means running test wafers, characterising the result, adjusting recipes, and proving the output is stable enough to bet a product on. That takes months and consumes wafers that could have been sold. Once a tool is the process of record for a given layer at a given node, replacing it with a competitor's tool means doing all of that again, and accepting yield risk on a running product line, in exchange for a discount on the purchase price. Almost nobody takes that trade.

The second is that the tools are matched to each other. The coater-developer bolts onto the scanner. The metrology step is calibrated to the deposition step it is checking. Suppliers sell into that, offering integrated flows across several steps, which is a large part of why the broadest supplier keeps its breadth.

The third is research and development scale. Development for the next node begins years before any revenue and must be funded from the margin on the current one. A new entrant would need to fund a decade of that against an installed base it cannot displace, for a market where the entire addressable customer list fits on one page.

The fourth is the installed base itself, and it is the part an accountant notices first, because it shows up in the revenue mix rather than in the strategy deck.

## Reading the accounts of an equipment company

The instinct is to read these firms as capital goods businesses: lumpy orders, cyclical revenue, thin recurring income. That was true once. It is much less true now, and the line item that proves it is service.

Applied Materials' services arm produced six point four billion dollars of fiscal 2025 revenue, up three per cent, against a total of twenty-eight point three seven billion, and reached a record one point eight billion in the July 2026 quarter, up twenty-two per cent year on year (company results, November 2025 and August 2026). Lam's Customer Support Business Group — spares, upgrades, service contracts and equipment for older nodes — reached two point four seven billion dollars in the June 2026 quarter, up forty-three per cent from one point seven three billion a year earlier, which is more than a third of the quarter's total revenue (company results and earnings materials, July 2026). KLA's service revenue rose seventeen per cent to eight hundred and twenty million dollars in the same quarter, and the company has described service as a business exceeding two and a half billion dollars annually growing at a low-teens compound rate (company earnings materials, July 2026).

Why this matters is straightforward. A tool installed today will be maintained, upgraded and supplied with parts for a decade or more. Every tool shipped in a boom therefore raises the floor under the next downturn. The cyclical business has grown an annuity inside it, and the annuity is now large enough to change the shape of the trough. It does not eliminate the cycle. It means the next contraction should look less like the ones in the textbooks.

Four other things repay attention in these filings.

**Gross margin, compared across the group.** The spread is informative.

| Company | Gross margin | Period | Basis |

| Lam Research | 51.7 per cent GAAP | Quarter ended June 2026 | Company results, July 2026 |

The measurement company earns more per dollar of sales than the companies that build the machines doing the work. Both earn less than the foundry and far less than the chip designer. Value in this industry does not simply accumulate at the bottom of the stack; it accumulates wherever substitution is hardest, and substitution is hardest for the tool that finds the defect nobody else can find.

**Deferred revenue and customer deposits.** Take an illustrative tool order, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. A fab orders an etch system for forty million dollars, paying eight million on order. The supplier holds that eight million as a liability, not as revenue. Months later the tool ships; depending on the contract and on the supplier's accounting policy, some of the price may be recognised then and some held back until installation and customer acceptance. Only when the fab signs off does the last slice become revenue. So the money arrives first, the revenue arrives later, and the gap sits on the balance sheet as deferred revenue or customer deposits. That is why the liability line moves before the revenue line does, and why an accustomed reader checks it before checking the income statement. The revenue recognition policy note tells you which tools follow which pattern for the company you are reading; do not assume it is the same across the four.

**Concentration and geography.** These companies sell to a handful of buyers, and where those buyers are matters as much as who they are. China was reported at about twenty-eight per cent of Applied Materials' revenue in the July 2026 quarter, down from thirty-five per cent; Lam reported China at twenty-six per cent of the June 2026 quarter, down from thirty-four per cent in the prior quarter, with Taiwan at twenty-seven per cent (company disclosures and 2026 reporting). Applied Materials has estimated that export restrictions could reduce its fiscal 2026 revenue by between six hundred million and seven hundred and ten million dollars (company statement, reported 2026). This book takes no position on any government's policy. The accounting point is narrow and permanent: a revenue stream that can be removed by a regulation is worth less than an identical revenue stream that cannot, and the risk factors section is where the company tells you which is which.

**Research and development against gross profit.** The conventional ratio is research spending over revenue. Here the more revealing denominator is gross profit, because development is funded out of margin. A supplier whose margin compresses in a downturn is choosing, that year, between the next node's development programme and its reported earnings — and the choice is visible in the accounts long before it is visible in the products.

## Why equipment revenue leads the foundries

Here is the part that makes this layer worth watching even for a reader with no interest in machines.

Follow one decision through time. A foundry concludes it needs capacity for a node two generations out. It commits capital, which appears first as guidance — TSMC guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion US dollars, above analyst expectations, in its January 2026 results. It then places orders. The equipment makers build to those orders: reported lead times for advanced lithography systems have been in the region of eighteen to twenty-four months, with bookings extending into 2028 (industry reporting of ASML's 2026 earnings commentary), and 2026 coverage described equipment delivery lead times across the industry as having roughly doubled. Meanwhile the building itself takes eighteen months to two years to construct and fit out.

Then the tools arrive, are installed, are qualified, and the process is brought to a yield worth running. Only after all of that does a saleable wafer emerge — and only after that does a chip designer have anything to sell, and a cloud provider anything to rack.

So the sequence, in time, is: capex commitment, then equipment revenue, then wafer output, then chip revenue, then compute capacity. Each arrow takes months to quarters. The composite gap between an equipment maker's revenue and the foundry output it enables is, in rough terms, about a year — sometimes less for a tool going into an existing fab with power and cleanroom already in place, sometimes considerably more for a greenfield site.

The consequence is that the equipment layer's revenue is a statement about the future that the rest of the stack has already committed to, in cash, and cannot easily take back. A fab operator can cancel an order, and does. But it cannot conjure capacity it did not order two years ago. Capacity for 2028 is being decided now, in purchase orders, and those purchase orders show up in these four companies' books long before they show up as wafers.

The same logic runs in reverse, which is where the signal earns its reputation. Equipment revenue falls first. In 2023, worldwide semiconductor manufacturing equipment sales slipped one point three per cent to one hundred and six point three billion dollars from the 2022 record of one hundred and seven point six billion (SEMI, reporting on 2023 billings). Inside that modest headline was a collapse: NAND equipment sales were forecast to fall about forty-nine per cent to eight point eight billion dollars, and capital spending analyses covering 2023 recorded cuts of the order of fifty per cent at SK Hynix and forty-two per cent at Micron. The memory glut that produced those cuts was visible in equipment orders before it was visible in most people's understanding of the memory market — and the shortage that followed, when it came, was a direct consequence of capacity that had not been ordered.

Contrast the position in 2026. SEMI's forecast published in July 2026 put wafer fab equipment at one hundred and forty-three point nine billion dollars for the year, up twenty-three point one per cent, with foundry and logic wafer fab equipment up eighteen point nine per cent to seventy-eight billion (SEMI forecast, 2026). Earlier forecasts had been materially lower — SEMI's December 2025 releases put total equipment sales at a record one hundred and thirty-nine billion dollars for 2026 and one hundred and fifty-six billion for 2027 — and the upward revision was attributed to advanced memory, particularly high-bandwidth memory, and to leading-edge logic.

A forecast for the current year revised upwards by roughly a fifth inside eight months is itself the information. It says that the buyers changed their minds during the year, which in this layer means they changed their minds about 2028.

## Watching the cycle without fooling yourself

If equipment orders are the best early signal, the obvious question is where to find them. The honest answer includes a disappointment.

The classic instrument no longer exists. SEMI ceased publishing its monthly North American book-to-bill report in 2017, the December 2016 report being the last, after participants stopped reporting orders. What remains is a billings series — money invoiced, not orders taken — and the Worldwide Semiconductor Equipment Market Statistics report prepared with the Semiconductor Equipment Association of Japan, which carries billings and bookings by equipment segment and end-market region. Beyond that, the disclosures in individual filings.

So the practical toolkit is a composite, and each part has a defect worth naming.

| What to watch | Where it comes from | What it tells you | Its defect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company guidance for the next quarter | Results announcements | Shipments already largely committed | A forecast, not a result; the company controls the framing |
| Deferred revenue and customer deposits | Balance sheet | Customer money received ahead of revenue | Policy-dependent; not comparable between suppliers |
| Suppliers' own estimate of total wafer fab equipment spending | Earnings calls | The seller's read on the whole market | Self-interested, and they disagree with each other |
| SEMI and SEAJ billings and bookings | Industry statistics | Money actually invoiced across the industry | Lags orders; published with delay |
| Foundry and memory capex guidance | Customers' results | The commitment upstream of everything | Revised often, and revised most in the year it matters |

The disagreement in row three is not a flaw to be tidied away. It is the most useful thing on the list. During 2026, Lam raised its estimate of the wafer fab equipment market to about one hundred and forty billion dollars (company earnings call, April 2026), KLA raised its view to the low one hundred and fifty billion range and said it expected second-half revenue to be about twenty per cent above the first half (company earnings call, July 2026), and SEMI's own July 2026 figure sat at one hundred and forty-three point nine billion for wafer fab equipment. Three informed parties, three different numbers, all published within months of each other. Anyone quoting one of them as "the" size of the market is quoting an estimate and calling it a fact.

Two cautions belong here, both of them serious.

The first is that a leading indicator is not a licence to forecast. This book gives no price targets, recommends no security and predicts no share price, and that applies with particular force to a signal as widely watched as this one. What order data gives you is an understanding of when physical capacity will exist. What happens to any company's valuation in the meantime depends on what the market already expected, which is not observable in any filing.

The second is that this layer is exposed to the cycle in both directions. The share-price record of 2026 makes the point: Applied Materials was reported as up about ninety-eight per cent year to date as at mid-August 2026 (24/7 Wall St and Yahoo Finance, 18 August 2026), with market capitalisations around three hundred and ninety-four billion dollars for Applied Materials, roughly three hundred and ninety billion for Lam Research, around two hundred and fifty-eight billion for KLA and about one hundred and sixty-two billion for Tokyo Electron as at August 2026 (companiesmarketcap; all four figures move daily and will be wrong by the time you read them). Those are the numbers of a layer being re-rated. A layer that can be re-rated upward on an order book can be re-rated downward on the same order book, and the second movement is historically faster than the first.

## What could go wrong here

Three things, none of them hypothetical.

Customer concentration is the structural one. The leading-edge buyers are few and their spending decisions are correlated, because they are all responding to the same end demand. When they pause together, as memory did in 2023, the effect on a supplier's revenue arrives within two quarters and there is nowhere to divert the capacity.

Policy is the second. Export controls have already removed identifiable revenue — the six hundred to seven hundred and ten million dollar fiscal 2026 estimate above is a company's own quantification of a rule's effect — and reporting in 2026 described Chinese policy encouraging domestic fabs towards locally made equipment. Without alleging anything about anyone's conduct, the mechanism is simple: watch a supplier's China revenue not only for its size but for its composition, because trailing-edge tools are the ones a domestic competitor reaches first.

The third is the industry's oldest habit. Capacity ordered into a shortage arrives, reliably, into a glut. The lead times cut both ways: the eighteen to twenty-four months that make equipment revenue a leading indicator are the same months that make over-ordering impossible to correct in time. The 2026 order book is a statement about 2028 demand made by people who cannot know it.

None of which changes the position at the bottom of the stack. Every accelerator in every data centre in the world passed through machines built by a handful of firms, most of them founded before the personal computer. The high-bandwidth memory that feeds those accelerators — a market estimated at about fifty-four point six billion US dollars in 2026, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, with capacity reported sold out through 2026 across major suppliers (industry analysis, 2026) — is made on their tools too, and the thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants announced by SK Hynix in August 2026 (CNBC, August 2026) is, in the end, a purchase order addressed to this chapter's four companies.

When you want to know whether the industry believes its own story, do not read what its executives say about demand. Read what they have ordered, and when it is due.
