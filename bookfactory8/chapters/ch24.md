# Microsoft Azure

On 29 October 2025, Microsoft reported a quarterly net income of twenty-seven point seven billion US dollars — and disclosed, in the same materials, that the figure would have been three point one billion dollars higher were it not for its share of the losses of a company it does not control, reported by CNBC in October 2025. That company was OpenAI. The deduction, forty-one cents per diluted share, was the visible tip of the strangest large-company relationship in modern business: a fifty-year-old software incumbent whose earnings, capital spending and competitive position had all become entangled with a loss-making partner it part-owns, hosts, licenses from, collects revenue from, and increasingly competes against.

This chapter is about how that came to be, and what it does to the accounts. Microsoft matters to the compute layer for a simple reason: it is the clearest example of a company that converted an incumbent position — decades of enterprise relationships and a distribution machine no start-up can copy — into an AI position, and paid for the conversion with the largest capital spending programme in its history. Whether the conversion earns its keep is the question the rest of the chapter keeps returning to.

## The second act nobody rated

Microsoft's cloud was announced at a developers' conference in October 2008 and reached general availability as Windows Azure in February 2010 — roughly four years after Amazon began renting out computing, a gap the industry press of the period treated as decisive. The early product was awkward: a platform tuned for Microsoft's own programming stack, sold to a world that was learning to rent generic machines by the hour from Amazon. For its first few years Azure was a subplot in a company whose profits still came overwhelmingly from Windows and Office licences.

Two dates changed the trajectory. On 4 February 2014 Satya Nadella became chief executive and, in his first communications to employees and customers, declared Microsoft's job was to thrive in a "mobile-first, cloud-first" world — the announcement is on Microsoft's own record from February 2014. Weeks later, in March 2014, Windows Azure was renamed Microsoft Azure: a small rebranding with a large meaning. The cloud would no longer be an accessory to Windows; Windows would become one workload among many on the cloud.

What followed was a decade of the least glamorous kind of corporate work: rebuilding a licensing and sales machine, product by product, from perpetual licences towards subscriptions and metered consumption. Office became Microsoft 365. Server products grew cloud twins. Enterprise agreements — the multi-year contracts through which large organisations buy Microsoft — became the channel through which Azure was sold.

The accounting of that transition deserves a paragraph, because it explains why the pivot demanded nerve as well as engineering. A perpetual licence is recognised as revenue largely up front: the customer pays, the software ships, the income statement records the sale. A subscription recognises the same customer relationship as a stream of smaller amounts over years, and a consumption business like Azure recognises revenue only as the metered usage actually occurs. A company moving from the first model to the second and third deliberately depresses its own near-term reported revenue in exchange for durability and compounding. It also changes what the balance sheet must carry: the licence business needed almost no physical assets, while the consumption business needs the buildings and machines this part of the book is about. Microsoft spent the 2010s trading a capital-light, lumpy income statement for a capital-heavy, recurring one. The AI cycle then raised the stakes on exactly that trade.

The result took years to appear in disclosed numbers, partly because Microsoft declined for years to disclose Azure's revenue at all, reporting only growth percentages inside a broader "Intelligent Cloud" segment. The absolute figure finally arrived in July 2025, when the company announced that Azure had surpassed seventy-five billion dollars of revenue in the fiscal year ended June 2025, up thirty-four per cent, within a "Microsoft Cloud" total of one hundred and sixty-eight point nine billion, up twenty-three per cent, per the company's results announcement of July 2025. A year later the company said Azure had passed one hundred billion dollars of annual revenue for the first time, with growth in the June 2026 quarter accelerating to forty-three per cent from forty per cent the quarter before, per the results announcement of July 2026. A business that did not exist in the accounts twenty years earlier had become, on its own, one of the largest technology companies in the world.

For the group as a whole, the fiscal year ended 30 June 2026 produced revenue of three hundred and thirty-one point eight billion dollars, up eighteen per cent, and operating income of one hundred and fifty-five point two billion, up twenty-one per cent, per the July 2026 results announcement. An operating margin in the mid-forties, at that scale, while spending on capacity at the rate described below, is the financial fact that underwrites everything else in this chapter.

## The asset that cannot be bought

Every layer of the AI stack has its scarce thing. The silicon layer has physics. The model layer has research talent. Microsoft's scarce thing is duller and, on the evidence so far, harder to replicate than either: it already sells software to essentially every large organisation on earth, through contracts, relationships and compliance arrangements that took forty years to accumulate.

Consider what a frontier AI laboratory must build to sell to a bank or a government ministry. A sales force that speaks procurement. Security certifications and audit trails. Data-residency commitments, regionally deployed infrastructure, contractual liability terms a general counsel will sign. Billing that flows through an existing budget line rather than a new one. Identity and access integration, so that the new tool obeys the same permissions as everything else. A support organisation. References. None of this is intellectually hard. All of it is slow, and all of it already exists inside Microsoft's enterprise agreements.

| What an enterprise sale requires | The entrant | Microsoft |
|---|---|---|
| A procurement relationship and contract vehicle | Built from zero, account by account | Enterprise agreements already in force |
| Security, compliance and audit posture | Certifications earned over years | Inherited from the existing estate |
| Deployment, identity and permissions | New integration project | The customer already runs Microsoft's identity layer |
| Billing and budget line | A new supplier to onboard | An amendment to an existing invoice |
| Support and field organisation | Hired and trained | In place for decades |

The table is qualitative by design — none of these items carries a disclosed price, which is rather the point. The entrant must spend years of selling time acquiring what the incumbent renews annually as a matter of routine.

This is why Microsoft's AI product strategy took the shape it did: not a destination app, but a surcharge on software the customer already runs. In July 2023 the company announced Microsoft 365 Copilot at thirty dollars per user per month, on top of an eligible Microsoft 365 licence, as reported from its partner conference in July 2023. The pricing logic is worth pausing on, because it is the incumbent's whole argument in one line item. A consumer chatbot must win a user's attention and twenty dollars a month, one user at a time. A Copilot seat is sold to a chief information officer in blocks of ten thousand, attached to a contract that already exists and a deployment that has already happened. The marginal cost of distribution is close to zero; the customer relationship was paid for decades ago and long since expensed.

The same logic runs down the stack. An enterprise that runs its identity, email and databases on Microsoft finds Azure the path of least resistance for its AI workloads, whatever the merits of rival clouds. Distribution, in other words, is not one advantage among several. It is the asset the whole position rests on — the reason a company that did not train the frontier model and does not fabricate the chip nonetheless captures a toll at the point where AI meets the enterprise customer.

An accountant should immediately notice what is odd about this asset: it appears nowhere on the balance sheet. Forty years of enterprise relationships were expensed as incurred — salaries, marketing, support — and carry a book value of nil. The balance sheet records the servers, which depreciate, and not the distribution machine, which appreciates. It is a standing reminder that the statement of financial position records what was paid for, not what is valuable.

## Thirteen billion dollars and what it bought

The relationship that turned Microsoft's cloud position into an AI position began modestly. In July 2019 Microsoft invested one billion dollars in OpenAI and became its exclusive cloud provider, as reported at the time. Further funding followed in 2021, and in January 2023 — weeks after ChatGPT's public launch — Microsoft announced a new multi-billion-dollar investment, reported by CNBC and Bloomberg in January 2023 at around ten billion dollars. Across the phases, Microsoft's total commitment is generally reported at roughly thirteen billion dollars, a figure repeated in CNBC's coverage of the partnership's October 2025 restructuring.

What Microsoft bought, in exchange, was threefold. First, access: the right to use OpenAI's models inside its own products, which became the engine of Copilot across the product estate. Second, workload: OpenAI's training and inference ran on Azure, guaranteeing the cloud a marquee tenant at the frontier of demand. Third, association: for two critical years, the world's most talked-about AI company ran on Microsoft's infrastructure, and enterprise buyers drew the intended conclusion.

The partnership also shaped the capital spending plan, and this is the point most easily missed. A cloud provider ordinarily builds capacity against a statistical forecast of thousands of customers' demand. Microsoft, from 2019 onward, was also building against the concentrated, lumpy, and rapidly compounding demand of a single research laboratory whose appetite for compute doubled on timescales measured in months. The buildout that now shows up as capital expenditure was, in meaningful part, infrastructure commissioned with one customer's roadmap in view. Customer concentration usually appears in the revenue note. Here it appeared first in the capex line.

Then the relationship began, in stages, to loosen — each stage disclosed, dated, and worth recording precisely.

| Date | Event | As reported by |
|---|---|---|
| July 2019 | One billion dollar investment; Microsoft becomes exclusive cloud provider | Contemporary reporting, July 2019 |
| January 2023 | New multi-billion investment, reported around ten billion dollars | CNBC / Bloomberg, January 2023 |
| January 2025 | OpenAI announces Stargate, a plan with SoftBank, Oracle and MGX intending five hundred billion dollars of AI infrastructure over four years; Microsoft participates as a technology partner, its exclusivity loosened | OpenAI announcement / reporting, January 2025 |
| October 2025 | OpenAI completes restructuring as a public benefit corporation; Microsoft holds roughly twenty-seven per cent, valued around one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars; OpenAI commits to purchase an incremental two hundred and fifty billion dollars of Azure services; Microsoft loses its right of first refusal on OpenAI's compute; model access runs to 2032 | CNBC / Data Centre Dynamics, October 2025 |
| April 2026 | Exclusivity and revenue-sharing rewritten: Microsoft ceases paying revenue share to OpenAI; OpenAI continues revenue-share payments to Microsoft through 2030, subject to a total cap; OpenAI free to license models to any third party on any cloud | CNBC / Bloomberg / Forbes, April 2026 |

The January 2025 step deserves a sentence of its own, because it was the hinge. Stargate — announced by OpenAI with SoftBank, Oracle and MGX as equity funders, intending five hundred billion dollars of United States AI infrastructure over four years, per OpenAI's announcement of January 2025 — was OpenAI declaring that its compute appetite exceeded what any single provider would or could build for it. Microsoft, named a technology partner rather than a funder, let it happen; contemporary reporting framed the arrangement as Microsoft loosening the ties in exchange for keeping what mattered most to it. The loosening, in other words, ran both ways. A single laboratory's training demand had outgrown the balance sheet of a three-trillion-dollar company — or at least outgrown what that company's shareholders would tolerate building for one customer at preferential terms. Exclusivity did not so much end as become unaffordable.

Read as a sequence, the direction is unmistakable. In 2019 Microsoft held exclusivity over a dependent laboratory. By mid-2026 it held a minority stake in an eight-hundred-and-fifty-two-billion-dollar company — OpenAI's valuation after its one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round of March 2026, per Forbes and industry reporting in 2026 — that buys compute from Microsoft's rivals, sells products against Microsoft's products, and pays Microsoft a capped, expiring royalty. Each step was rational in isolation. The cumulative effect was to convert a captive supplier relationship into something closer to an armed alliance.

## The bill, and the market's verdict

Now to the spending. The recent record, drawn from the company's results announcements of July 2025 and July 2026, frames it:

| Disclosed figure | Year to June 2025 | Year to June 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Group revenue | Up fifteen per cent on the prior year | Three hundred and thirty-one point eight billion dollars, up eighteen per cent |
| Operating income | — | One hundred and fifty-five point two billion dollars, up twenty-one per cent |
| Azure revenue | Passed seventy-five billion dollars, up thirty-four per cent | Passed one hundred billion dollars for the first time |
| Azure growth, June quarter | — | Forty-three per cent, up from forty per cent the prior quarter |

Where a cell is blank, the company did not disclose a comparable figure in the announcement cited; Microsoft reports segment detail on its own definitions, and those definitions have shifted over the years, which is one reason this book quotes the company's announcements rather than reconstructing series of its own.

Against that revenue stands the outflow. In April 2026, reporting the March quarter, Microsoft told investors to expect about one hundred and ninety billion dollars of capital spending for 2026, with rising memory prices among the pressures cited, per CNBC in April 2026. The June 2026 quarter alone carried roughly forty-one billion dollars of capital expenditure, and the company guided the September 2026 quarter to more than fifty billion, per the July 2026 results and CNBC's coverage of them. For scale: one hundred and ninety billion dollars is more than the fiscal year's entire operating income of one hundred and fifty-five point two billion. Microsoft is, at present, ploughing back more than it earns from operations into physical plant.

The stock market, it should be recorded, approved: the shares rose eight per cent after the July 2026 report in which the company raised its spending plans, citing demand, per CNBC in July 2026. As at mid-August 2026, Microsoft's market capitalisation stood at roughly three point six six trillion US dollars, per the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings — the fourth most valuable company in the world, behind Nvidia at roughly five point three to five point four trillion, Apple at roughly four point four one trillion and Alphabet at roughly four point two trillion, same period, same sources. All four figures move daily and will have moved by the time this is read; the rankings sites update continuously. It is worth letting the ranking register: the company writing the largest cheques for AI infrastructure is currently valued below the company cashing them.

## The return question

An accountant looking at this position asks one question before all others: what does a hundred and ninety billion dollars a year of capital expenditure do to future profit, and what revenue must arrive to justify it?

The mechanism was set out in the previous chapter; here it is applied. Capital expenditure does not touch the income statement when the cash leaves. It becomes property and equipment on the balance sheet, then returns to the income statement as depreciation, spread over an assumed useful life. The assumption is therefore load-bearing, and Microsoft has form on it. In July 2022 the company's chief financial officer announced that the depreciable life of servers and network equipment would be extended from four years to six, effective from fiscal 2023 — a change reported at the time, by The Register in August 2022 among others, as expected to add roughly three point seven billion dollars to fiscal 2023 profit. No machine ran differently. No cash moved. Reported profit rose by billions because an estimate changed.

There is nothing improper in this — asset lives are estimates, estimates get revised, and the change was disclosed. But it defines the question that now hangs over the far larger AI estate. Take an illustrative data centre programme, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company:

| Illustrative programme (invented round numbers) | Six-year assumed life | Four-year assumed life |
|---|---|---|
| Annual infrastructure spend | One hundred and eighty billion dollars | One hundred and eighty billion dollars |
| Steady-state annual depreciation | Thirty billion dollars | Forty-five billion dollars |
| Difference in annual pre-tax profit | Fifteen billion dollars | — |

Nothing in the two columns differs except an estimate of how long a chip stays worth running — yet the columns are fifteen billion dollars of annual pre-tax profit apart. The concern is not symmetrical, either. Buildings, land and power infrastructure plausibly last decades. The doubt attaches to the accelerators, which age not because they wear out but because the next generation makes the same computation cheaper per watt — the economics of the silicon layer, arriving as an expense in the compute layer. A chip can be in perfect working order and still be uneconomic to run.

And depreciation is only the recovery of cost. To earn an economic return, revenue attributable to those assets must exceed the depreciation, plus power, plus staff, plus the cost of the capital itself, over the assets' actual — not assumed — economic life. That is the arithmetic behind every analyst's question on every earnings call of 2026, and it will not be settled by a press release. It will be settled by whether Azure's growth rate stays ahead of its depreciation charge for the rest of the decade.

Microsoft's counter-argument deserves a fair statement, because it is a real one. Its AI revenue is not a hope; some of it is contracted. OpenAI's incremental two-hundred-and-fifty-billion-dollar Azure commitment, per the October 2025 reporting, and Anthropic's thirty-billion-dollar Azure commitment, per CNBC in November 2025, are purchase obligations from the two best-funded model companies in existence. Azure's forty-three per cent growth into a hundred-billion-dollar revenue base, per the July 2026 results, is demand actually billed, not projected. And the capacity being built is fungible across customers in a way one factory for one product is not. The bear's reply writes itself: those commitments are only as good as the buyers' own financing, and the buyers are loss-making companies funding multi-hundred-billion-dollar promises out of venture rounds. The two-hundred-and-fifty-billion-dollar commitment and the eleven-billion-dollar quarterly loss described below belong to the same customer.

## The partner becomes a competitor

Which brings the chapter to its structural risk, the one that cannot be fixed by an accounting policy: Microsoft's most important supplier, tenant and product ingredient is also becoming its rival.

The competition is not hypothetical. OpenAI sells subscriptions and enterprise products into the same organisations Microsoft serves with Copilot — one reason the April 2026 rewrite, under which OpenAI may deploy on any cloud including Google's and Amazon's, mattered so much, per CNBC's April 2026 coverage. Every enterprise seat OpenAI wins directly is a seat Copilot does not, sold to a customer Microsoft spent forty years acquiring. The distribution moat is real, but the model company is now permitted, contractually and strategically, to dig its own.

Meanwhile the stake itself has become a source of earnings noise. Under equity accounting, Microsoft's share of OpenAI's losses lands in Microsoft's income statement each quarter: the three point one billion dollar deduction of the September 2025 quarter implied, at a roughly twenty-seven per cent stake, an OpenAI quarterly loss in the region of eleven and a half billion dollars, as The Register and others worked out in October 2025. By August 2026, CNBC was reporting that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the earnings picture of the companies holding them. Microsoft thus reports, inside its own net income, the burn rate of a competitor it cannot direct — a sentence with no precedent in the company's accounts.

Against this risk Microsoft has built two hedges, both visible and both dated. The first is silicon. Having announced its first custom AI accelerator, Maia 100, alongside the Cobalt processor in November 2023, Microsoft unveiled Maia 200 on 26 January 2026 — an inference-focused chip built on TSMC's three-nanometre process with two hundred and sixteen gigabytes of high-bandwidth memory, already running production workloads including Microsoft 365 Copilot, per GeekWire and TrendForce reporting from January 2026. The strategic point is not that Maia beats Nvidia; the company's own positioning is about cost per unit of inference, not peak performance. The point is bargaining power: a cloud that can serve its highest-volume workloads on its own silicon negotiates differently with every supplier above it in this book and every partner beside it.

The second hedge is the other laboratory. In November 2025 Microsoft announced an investment of up to five billion dollars in Anthropic — alongside Nvidia's up to ten billion — while Anthropic committed to purchase thirty billion dollars of Azure compute, in a deal valuing the company in the range of three hundred and fifty billion dollars, per CNBC in November 2025. The same reporting framed the deal plainly as reducing Microsoft's dependence on OpenAI. Copilot's intelligence, in other words, is being re-plumbed to be supplier-agnostic: OpenAI models, Anthropic models, Microsoft's own, whichever serves the seat cheapest. It is exactly what a company does when it has concluded that its key supplier relationship is temporary, and it is exactly what the supplier's own diversification — onto rival clouds, into rival products — invites.

Whether the hedges are adequate is not knowable from outside, and this book does not predict share prices or recommend any security. What can be said from the record is that Microsoft has already extracted more from the OpenAI relationship than almost any counterparty extracts from any deal: a stake reported at one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars against roughly thirteen billion invested, per the October 2025 reporting, plus seven years of product lead, plus a contracted revenue pipeline. The unresolved question is what it gave up: the training of its most formidable future competitor, on its own machines, at preferential terms.

## What to watch

The reader who wants to track this position needs no privileged information; the disclosures arrive quarterly, and four lines carry most of the story.

First, Azure's growth rate against the capital spending that feeds it — the July 2026 pairing of forty-three per cent growth with a hundred-and-ninety-billion-dollar spending year, per the company's results and CNBC's coverage, is the baseline. If the growth number falls while the spending number does not, the return question sharpens each quarter. Second, the depreciation charge and, above all, any change in assumed useful lives, in either direction; after 2022, Microsoft's estimates have a history worth watching. Third, the equity-method line, which is the closest thing the public has to a quarterly telemetry feed on OpenAI's losses. Fourth, the remaining-performance-obligation and commitment disclosures, where the giant Azure purchase promises of OpenAI and Anthropic will either convert into revenue on schedule, or not.

None of these numbers will stay where this chapter leaves them; every figure here is a snapshot with a date attached, and the current versions live in the company's next results announcement. But the shape of the position is likely to outlast the snapshots. Microsoft entered the AI period owning the one asset the new entrants could not raise money to buy — the enterprise customer base — and spent three years converting it into capacity, contracts and a stake in its own chief rival. The accounts now show an incumbent carrying frontier-scale risk: record margins funding record capex, contracted revenue from uncontracted customers, and a minority stake that moves its net income by billions in quarters it does not control. Incumbency, it turns out, is not a shelter from the AI cycle. It is simply the most expensive seat at the table.
