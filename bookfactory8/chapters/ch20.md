# Memory I: Why DRAM Was Always a Bad Business

In the middle of 1985, Andy Grove stood in his office at Intel with Gordon Moore and asked a question that has since been quoted in half the strategy courses on earth: if the board threw them out and brought in a new chief executive, what would that new man do? Moore answered without hesitation. He would get Intel out of memories. Grove suggested they walk out the door, walk back in, and do it themselves. By Grove's own later account of that year, Intel's net income had fallen from one hundred and ninety-eight million dollars in 1984 to under two million in 1985, and the business doing the damage was the one Intel had effectively created: dynamic random access memory.

Sit with that for a moment, because it is a strange fact. The company that made the first commercially successful DRAM chip, the product that launched the entire merchant memory industry, concluded within fifteen years that the business was not worth being in — and was right. Intel left, prospered, and never came back in any serious way. Most of the companies that stayed are gone. Of the more than twenty firms that fought for the DRAM market at its late-1980s peak, three remain.

This chapter is the history of why. It matters to this book for a specific reason. In 2026, the three survivors of that cull were among the best-performing large stocks in the world, and the next chapter is about the change in the product that made them so. But the change only makes sense against the background: for roughly four decades, DRAM was arguably the worst structural business in semiconductors — a pure commodity, made with some of the most expensive factories on earth, sold into a cycle that repeatedly destroyed the capital that built it. To understand what changed, you first have to understand what it changed from.

## The perfect commodity

DRAM is the working memory of a computer — the fast, temporary storage a processor uses while it is actually doing something. The one-transistor memory cell at the heart of it was invented at IBM by Robert Dennard in 1966, with the patent filed in 1967 and granted in 1968, per IBM's own corporate history and the Semiconductor Industry Association's citation when it honoured him. Intel turned the idea into a mass product: the 1103, released in October 1970, which within about two years had been adopted by most major computer manufacturers and had, per Intel's own corporate timeline and industry retrospectives, become the best-selling semiconductor memory chip in the world. It killed magnetic core memory, the technology it replaced, on price and density.

From the very beginning, though, the product had a property that would define the industry's economics for the next half century: one maker's chip is a near-perfect substitute for another's.

That is by design. Memory is sold against published industry standards so that any module maker or computer builder can source it from anyone. A buyer choosing between two qualified suppliers of the same density and speed grade is choosing on price, delivery and little else. There is no brand premium at the socket. There is no software lock-in, no ecosystem, no developer base — all the things that later chapters show protecting the margins of a logic designer simply do not exist here. In the language this book has been using since chapter one: the gross margin line is the scoreboard of scarcity, and a standardised commodity is the definition of a product that is never scarce for long.

That has a consequence worth stating plainly, because it separates memory from almost everything else in this part of the book. A commodity producer does not set its price. The market sets the price, and the producer's profit is whatever gap happens to exist, this quarter, between that market price and its own cost. When the price is double the cost, memory makers report gross margins that look like a software company's. When the price is half the cost, the same fabs, the same people and the same products report catastrophic losses. Neither number tells you much about the quality of the company. Both tell you where you are in the cycle.

And the cycle is the second thing.

## The arithmetic that makes it brutal

The memory cycle is not a mystery and it is not a failure of intelligence by the people who run these companies. It is the rational outcome of three facts, each of which is individually sensible.

First, capacity comes in enormous, slow, indivisible lumps. A memory fab is a multi-billion-dollar construction project that takes on the order of two years to build and equip. You cannot add a little capacity when prices firm up. You commit billions on a forecast, and the supply arrives — all of it — long after the demand picture that justified it has changed.

Second, once a fab exists, almost all of its costs are fixed. Depreciation on the building and equipment, the engineering staff, the cleanroom running around the clock — these are incurred whether the fab produces at full tilt or half. The extra cash cost of actually pushing one more wafer through is comparatively small.

Third, the product is a commodity, so no producer can hold price by restraint. If you cut output to support the price, you lose the revenue and your competitors gain the share, and the price barely moves.

Put the three together and the machine assembles itself. Take an illustrative memory maker, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Its fab cost six billion dollars, depreciated over six years: one billion a year, fixed, regardless of anything. Cash operating costs at full output are another one and a half billion, of which only about six hundred million is truly variable with volume. At full output the fab produces one hundred million chips a year, so the full cost — cash plus depreciation — is twenty-five dollars a chip, and the cash cost is fifteen.

In the good year, the market price is forty dollars. Revenue of four billion, costs of two and a half billion, and a profit that makes the board feel like geniuses. Every producer's board feels the same, and every producer's board approves a new fab.

Two years later, all of those fabs switch on at once, and the price falls sixty per cent, to sixteen dollars. Now watch what the illustrative company does — because this is the step that outsiders find perverse and accountants find obvious. It keeps running flat out. At sixteen dollars a chip it books revenue of one point six billion against total costs of two and a half billion: a reported loss of nine hundred million. But the depreciation is a sunk cost; the cash picture is one point six billion of revenue against one and a half billion of cash costs, still marginally positive. Shutting down would save only the six hundred million of variable cost while giving up the full one point six billion of revenue. Running at a loss is the profit-maximising choice. Every producer makes the same calculation, so nobody cuts output, and the glut persists until demand grows into it.

What does get cut, immediately and savagely, is capital expenditure — the one discretionary item. And because capacity takes two years to arrive, cutting capex today does nothing to today's glut and everything to supply two years out. The seeds of the next shortage are sown at the bottom of the bust, just as the seeds of the bust were sown at the top of the boom. The industry has ridden this loop for fifty years, and every participant has always understood it perfectly. Understanding a trap is not the same as being able to leave it.

Demand, meanwhile, offered no anchor. Memory demand in bits has grown almost without interruption for fifty years, because every fall in price finds new uses for bits — more memory per computer, then per phone, then per car, then per server. But demand in bits is not demand in dollars. When the price per bit falls faster than bit consumption grows, an industry can ship more product every year than it has ever shipped and collect less money for it. Volume growth in a commodity is not prosperity; it is the treadmill's speed reading. Every producer knew that the cure for a glut was demand growing into the new capacity, and every producer also knew that the mechanism by which demand grew was the collapse of its own selling prices.

One more feature made the DRAM version of this cycle nastier than most commodity cycles: the treadmill. Under the scaling described elsewhere in this part of the book, the cost of a bit of memory fell relentlessly with each process generation. A producer that skipped a generation to conserve cash would, within two or three years, find its cost per bit uncompetitive and its fate sealed. So the capital spending could never actually stop — not even in the busts. The dominant strategy for each individual firm, invest through the cycle or die, was collectively the very thing that guaranteed the next oversupply. It was a game in which the entry fee was billions, the prize was survival, and the survivors' reward was the right to pay the next entry fee.

## Round one: Japan takes it from America

The first great turn of the cycle rearranged the map of the industry. Through the 1970s DRAM was an American business. Then Japan's integrated electronics groups — NEC, Hitachi, Toshiba, Fujitsu, Mitsubishi — entered with deep balance sheets, patient bank finance and formidable manufacturing quality, and the American position collapsed with astonishing speed. Per the studies of the trade conflict later collected by the National Bureau of Economic Research, the United States' share of the DRAM market fell from about seventy per cent in 1978 to about twenty per cent by 1986, while Japan's rose from under thirty per cent to roughly seventy-five per cent over the same period.

The 1985 downturn turned retreat into rout. This was the environment of Grove and Moore's conversation, and Intel was not alone: American firms exited DRAM one after another in the mid-1980s, and by 1986, as Reason magazine's 1992 retrospective on the episode put it, only two American-owned merchant producers were left — Texas Instruments and Micron — with the American industry holding less than a ten per cent share of a market it had monopolised a decade earlier. The dispute reached governments: the United States accused Japanese producers of dumping, and the two countries signed the US–Japan Semiconductor Agreement on 2 September 1986, committing Japan to end below-cost selling in world markets and to improve access for foreign chips.

Two details from this round deserve a moment, because both echo through the rest of the story.

The first is Micron, the odder of the two American survivors. It was founded in October 1978 as a four-person chip-design shop in the basement of a dental office in Boise, Idaho, and its early funding came not from Silicon Valley but from J. R. Simplot, the Idaho billionaire whose fortune was built on frozen potatoes — he put in a million dollars for a forty per cent stake in 1980, per accounts of the founding published by TheStreet and PC Gamer. Micron survived where far richer firms did not, essentially by being the lowest-cost producer and by refusing to die, and in 1998 it bought Texas Instruments' memory operations, per TI's announcements that year — leaving exactly one American DRAM maker.

The second is what victory bought the winners. Japan's producers took three-quarters of a market in which, across the cycle, there was very little profit to take. Their prize was the obligation to keep funding the treadmill, and when the next hungry entrant arrived — with lower costs, newer fabs and a government behind it — they were the incumbents with everything to lose.

## Round two: Korea takes it from Japan

The next entrant had announced itself with unusual formality. On 8 February 1983, Samsung's founder Lee Byung-chul, staying at the Hotel Okura in Tokyo, telephoned Seoul and ordered the announcement of Samsung's full-scale entry into semiconductors — the "Tokyo Declaration", as Korean press retrospectives record it. Ten months later, in December 1983, Samsung had a working 64-kilobit DRAM, becoming, per a Korea Times retrospective published in 2020, the third company in the world to develop one. Hyundai and LG followed into the business, and the Korean producers brought to the memory cycle the one weapon it truly rewards: the willingness, and the group-financed ability, to keep investing straight through the busts, expanding capacity while wounded incumbents retrenched, and emerging from each downturn with a larger share of the recovery.

The 1990s gave them two downturns to use it in. The first was the great glut of 1996. The early nineties PC boom had made memory briefly and spectacularly profitable, and the industry responded as it always responds: per a Fabricated Knowledge history of the decade's cycles, roughly fifty new fab construction plans were announced across 1995 and 1996 alone. The result was one of the most violent price collapses in the industry's history. In October 1996 the Christian Science Monitor reported that the standard 16-megabit DRAM chip, which had sold wholesale for about seventy dollars a year earlier, was selling for about seven — a drop of roughly eighty per cent in a year, on a product whose cost of production had fallen nowhere near that fast.

The second came with the dot-com bust. Gartner Dataquest forecast in mid-2001, as reported by The Register in June of that year, that DRAM sales would fall fifty-five point one per cent in 2001 — a halving of an entire industry's revenue in a single year — and by July 2001, Electronics Weekly reported the benchmark 64-megabit part trading below one dollar, beneath anyone's cost of making it.

Those two busts did to Japan what the mid-eighties had done to America. Fujitsu and Toshiba wound down or sold out of commodity DRAM; NEC and Hitachi merged their DRAM businesses in 1999 into a joint venture that took the name Elpida in 2000 and absorbed Mitsubishi's DRAM operations in 2003, per the company's corporate history — five proud rivals reduced to one consolidated survivor. Korea consolidated too, under the pressure of the Asian financial crisis: Hyundai Electronics agreed in May 1999 to buy control of LG Semicon for two thousand five hundred and sixty billion won, per the announcement preserved in SK Hynix's own newsroom archive, and the merged memory business was renamed Hynix in April 2001.

And in the middle of all this came an episode that tells you more about the economics of the business than any table of prices. Between April 1999 and June 2002, the world's major DRAM producers operated a price-fixing conspiracy against their largest customers — the American PC makers — for which the United States Department of Justice extracted guilty pleas and fines announced across 2004 and 2005: one hundred and sixty million dollars from Infineon, one hundred and eighty-five million from Hynix, and three hundred million from Samsung, at the time the second-largest criminal antitrust fine in American history, with total fines in the investigation exceeding six hundred and forty-six million dollars, per the Department's own announcements. No judgement about individuals is needed here; the companies pleaded guilty, and the structural point stands on its own. Producers with pricing power do not need to fix prices. A cartel is what a commodity industry reaches for when the honest economics have become unbearable — and even the cartel, note, operated during the very years the market halved. It did not work.

## The cull: 2007 to 2013

The cycle's final and most murderous turn began before the financial crisis and ended with the modern industry. Capacity added into the mid-2000s boom collided with slumping demand, and per EDN's reporting of industry analysis in 2009, DRAM suppliers had been selling below their cash costs — not their full costs, their cash costs — since the fourth quarter of 2007. Recall the illustrative fab above: below cash cost, there is no rational price at which running beats stopping, and producers ran anyway, gambling on outlasting each other. For calendar 2008, the same analysis put the DRAM industry's collective operating loss at about seven billion dollars, with an operating margin in the final quarter of minus sixty-five per cent. Governments in Germany and Taiwan spent early 2009 organising rescues for their national producers, as IEEE Spectrum reported at the time.

The rescues largely failed, and the roll of the dead from this one downturn defines the industry to this day.

Qimonda went first. Infineon, Germany's chip champion, had spun its memory business off in May 2006 and listed it in New York that August; by January 2009 Qimonda had filed for insolvency in Munich, blaming financing difficulties and the collapse in memory prices, only weeks after a three hundred and twenty-five million euro rescue package agreed in December 2008, per Forbes' reporting of the filing in January 2009. Europe's presence in DRAM manufacturing died with it. The accounting epilogue is instructive: Qimonda's insolvency administrator sued Infineon in 2010, arguing the business had been transferred into the spin-off at inflated values, and the dispute ran until August 2024, when Infineon agreed to pay seven hundred and fifty-three and a half million euros to the insolvency estate, per Reuters. A memory business can destroy capital for fifteen years after it has stopped making memory.

Elpida went next, and its fall was the larger event. Japan's last DRAM maker — the consolidated remnant of NEC, Hitachi and Mitsubishi's memory ambitions, and by then the world's third-largest producer — filed for bankruptcy protection in Tokyo on 27 February 2012 with about four hundred and forty-eight billion yen of liabilities, roughly five and a half billion US dollars, the largest bankruptcy of a Japanese manufacturer on record, as Bloomberg and Japanese and international outlets reported at the time. A strong yen, Korean competition and the memory cycle had done what nothing else in fifty years had managed: removed Japan — the country that had conquered this market in the 1980s — from it entirely. Micron bought the company out of reorganisation under an agreement announced in July 2012, providing two hundred billion yen, then about two and a half billion dollars, to satisfy creditors, with the Tokyo court approving the plan in February 2013 and the deal closing later that year, per Micron's announcements. For less than the cost of half a new fab, Micron acquired the accumulated remains of the entire Japanese DRAM industry.

Hynix, the third survivor, nearly made the same list — it had spent the 2001 downturn in creditor-led restructuring — but reached 2012 alive and was bought into a new home: SK Telecom completed its acquisition of a twenty-one per cent controlling stake in February 2012 for three point three seven trillion won, about three billion dollars, per the Korea Times' report of the completion, and the company became SK Hynix.

## Three survivors

A SemiAnalysis history of the industry counts twenty-three DRAM suppliers at the market's late-1980s peak. After 2013 there were, at any scale that matters, three. The concentration today is close to total: per TrendForce's February 2026 report on the final quarter of 2025, Samsung held about thirty-six per cent of DRAM revenue, SK Hynix about thirty-two per cent and Micron about twenty-two per cent — a shade over ninety per cent between them, with the remainder split among small Taiwanese and Chinese producers. Each survivor is itself a graveyard: Micron contains Texas Instruments' memory business and all of Japan's; SK Hynix contains Hyundai's and LG's; Samsung, alone, is built of nothing but Samsung.

Economics textbooks would predict that a three-firm industry behaves differently from a twenty-three-firm industry, and to a degree it did. With three players, each large enough to see the whole market and to know its rivals' capacity plans, the incentive to flood a downturn weakens; discipline becomes at least possible. The years after 2013 were, by the industry's own dismal standards, unusually profitable.

But — and this matters for the argument of the next chapter — consolidation did not end the cycle. It merely reduced its cast. In 2018 memory boomed; in 2019 it collapsed on schedule. Per Gartner's January 2020 press release, DRAM revenue fell thirty-seven and a half per cent in 2019, on an average selling price decline of forty-seven point four per cent, dragging the whole semiconductor market down almost twelve per cent with it. Three rational oligopolists, each still riding the treadmill, each still adding capacity in lumps two years ahead of visible demand, produced a bust that would have been perfectly recognisable to anyone who had lived through 1985 or 1996. As late as 2019, the honest conclusion was that DRAM remained what it had always been: the most cyclical major business in electronics, structurally condemned to hand its boom-time profits back.

For the reader of accounts, the condemned-business signature is easy to spot, and it is worth naming the line items because they recur in the next chapter with their meaning inverted. Gross margin that swings through tens of percentage points in eighteen months, in both directions, is the mark of a price-taker. Inventory is the tripwire: memory chips in a falling market must be written down to net realisable value, so a memory maker's inventory line converts the market's future into this quarter's loss before the revenue line has fully caught up — which is why chapter three told you inventory is the earliest warning of a turn. And capital expenditure against depreciation tells you where in the loop you stand: capex far above depreciation is an industry building its next glut; capex slashed below depreciation is an industry starving its way toward the next shortage.

## What the wreckage teaches

Add up the half century. An invention commercialised in America in 1970; an American industry destroyed by 1986; a Japanese industry destroyed by 2012; a European contender dead in 2009, still paying out settlements in 2024; a criminal price-fixing conspiracy that could not stop a fifty-five per cent revenue collapse; an industry that in a single year, 2008, lost seven billion dollars at the operating line while selling below cash cost. Along the way the product itself succeeded totally — memory became as universal as electricity, its price per bit fell by orders of magnitude, and the modern world was built on it. The gap between those two sentences is the whole lesson. A product can create immense value for the world and still destroy capital for its makers, because value flows to whatever is scarce, and standardised bits, made by rivals in expensive fabs that must run flat out, were never scarce for longer than the two years it took the next wave of capacity to arrive.

Why did anyone stay? Partly because governments wanted them to: Japan, Korea, Taiwan and Germany all, at various points, treated memory as strategic and financed it accordingly, and states can absorb losses that shareholders will not. Partly because memory was the ticket that taught the survivors to manufacture at scale — Samsung's position across all of electronics was built on capabilities the DRAM treadmill forced it to acquire. And partly, it must be said, because at the bottom of every cycle the exit price of a fab is close to nothing, so leaving crystallises the loss that staying lets you defer.

But the survivors did, in the end, collect a prize that none of the dead ever saw. In 2026, per IG and industry reporting at mid-year, Samsung's shares were up about one hundred and fourteen per cent for the year to date, Micron's about one hundred and forty-one per cent, and SK Hynix's about one hundred and eighty-six per cent; in August 2026, CNBC reported SK Hynix committing thirty-eight billion dollars to new memory plants. Those are the numbers of an industry that the market has decided is no longer the business described in this chapter — a judgement, note, about the future, of exactly the kind chapter two taught you to treat as a photograph rather than a fact.

Something happened to the product. A commodity that anyone could substitute became a component that customers qualify for years, contract for in advance, and cannot get enough of at any price. Whether that change is durable — whether the cycle is dead or merely resting — is the question the next chapter takes up. What this chapter should leave behind is the standard of proof. Fifty years of history, three bankruptcies of national champions, and a graveyard of twenty companies say the memory cycle always comes back. Anyone claiming this time is different is claiming something extraordinary, and owes you an extraordinary mechanism. The next chapter examines the one on offer.
