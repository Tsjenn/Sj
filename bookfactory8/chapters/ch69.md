# Export Controls

On 9 April 2025 Nvidia was informed by the United States government that a licence would be required to export its H20 accelerator to China. The chip had been designed to comply with the rules then in force. In the quarter that followed, the company recorded a charge of four point five billion US dollars against excess H20 inventory and purchase obligations, disclosed four point six billion dollars of H20 sales recognised before the requirement took effect, a further two point five billion dollars of H20 revenue it was unable to ship, and an outlook reflecting the loss of approximately eight billion dollars of H20 revenue. GAAP gross margin for the quarter was sixty point five per cent; excluding the charge, non-GAAP gross margin would have been seventy-one point three per cent rather than sixty-one. All of those figures come from the company's results announcement of 28 May 2025.

Nothing broke. No customer cancelled. No yield collapsed. A rule changed while the goods sat in a warehouse, and several billion dollars of inventory stopped being an asset.

This chapter is about how that happens. It describes the instruments governments use, how each one works, and what each one does to company strategy, to supply chains and to published accounts. It takes no position on whether any of these measures are wise, effective or justified, and it makes no prediction about what will be controlled next. It contains no investment advice. The subject is mechanism and observable effect, and the useful skill is reading the effect in a disclosure before it reaches a headline.

## What an export control actually is

Most people picture a ban. Very little of this regime consists of bans.

The primary instrument is a licence requirement attached to a classified item, plus a stated presumption about how applications will be decided. The item is identified by a classification code and a technical definition. The licence requirement says a transaction cannot proceed without government permission. The review policy — presumption of denial, case-by-case review, presumption of approval — says how likely that permission is.

That third element is the dial, and it is the one that moves fastest. The classification can stay identical, the licence requirement can stay identical, and a company's addressable market can double or vanish because a sentence about review policy changed. On 15 January 2026 the Bureau of Industry and Security published a final rule shifting the review policy for Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X for China from a presumption of denial to case-by-case review, with conditions attached: applicants must show the export will not reduce semiconductor production capacity available to United States customers, that the purchaser has adopted export compliance and screening procedures, and that the product has passed independent third-party testing in the United States. Reporting on the rule and on a Presidential Proclamation issued the previous day described a twenty-five per cent tariff on advanced semiconductor articles and a volume cap limiting China shipments to half the volume sold domestically. The rule text is a matter of public record in the Federal Register; the tariff and cap details here follow 2026 coverage by Tom's Hardware and the Center for a New American Security, and the reader should check both against the current text, because this is exactly the kind of provision that moves.

Two further mechanics matter, because they explain why these rules reach so much further than the borders of the country writing them.

The first is the foreign direct product rule. It extends a licence requirement to goods made abroad, by foreign companies, when those goods are the direct product of controlled technology or are made with controlled equipment. Since almost every advanced fab in the world contains American tools or runs American software, a chip fabricated entirely outside the United States can still fall under an American licence requirement. This is what turns a national rule into something closer to an industry-wide one.

The second is the entity-based control, which attaches to a named party rather than to a product. Once a company is listed, a licence is required to supply it, whatever the item and wherever it sits on a technical scale.

Everything that follows is a variation on these three: control the machine, control the specification, or control the counterparty.

## Instrument one: equipment restrictions

Restricting manufacturing equipment is the most durable of the three, and the reason is arithmetic rather than politics.

A chip is small, fungible, shipped by the pallet and hard to trace after the first sale. A lithography or etch tool is enormous, made by a handful of firms, installed by their engineers, and dependent on service contracts, spare parts and process support for years afterwards. A restriction on chips affects the chips it stops and nothing else. A restriction on a tool affects every wafer that tool would have processed for the next decade.

The October 2022 package, as summarised at the time by the Center for Strategic and International Studies and by Georgetown's Center for Security and Emerging Technology, went further than the machines themselves: it also restricted the ability of United States persons to support the development or production of certain advanced chips at facilities in China. Equipment without engineers degrades. That is a slower effect than a sales ban and a harder one to reverse.

Because the equipment layer is concentrated in a small number of countries, the instrument only works if those countries align. The Dutch government published new semiconductor export controls on 30 June 2023, effective 1 September 2023, extending licence requirements to certain deep-ultraviolet immersion systems, and in September 2024 extended the requirement to the 1970i and 1980i immersion tools, per contemporaneous reporting of both measures. ASML said in January 2024 that it had been blocked from shipping some machines to Chinese customers, per CNBC's report of 2 January 2024.

The effect on the vendors is visible in their geographic disclosures, and it is large.

That figure is worth pausing on, because it is a company doing the reader's work: quantifying, in dollars, the revenue that a rule removes. Very few risk factors are that specific.

The accounting point is narrow and general at once. Two vendors with the same revenue, the same margin and the same growth rate are not equally valuable if one of them sells a third of its output into a market that requires a licence.

## Instrument two: performance thresholds

The second instrument draws a line through a technical specification and controls everything above it.

The October 2022 rule set two lines. The first was total processing performance: bit length per operation multiplied by processing performance in operations per second, aggregated across the processors on the chip, at or above four thousand eight hundred. The second was an aggregate bidirectional interconnect transfer rate at or above six hundred gigabytes per second. Both figures are from the rule as explained by Georgetown's CSET and by Epoch AI's 2025 analysis of the controls; Epoch's account notes that the six hundred gigabyte per second figure was exactly the interconnect bandwidth of the flagship accelerator then in production.

Set a number in a regulation and you have not just drawn a boundary. You have written a product specification.

What followed is the clearest example in the industry of what a threshold actually produces. Nvidia designed the A800 and H800 — variants of its flagship parts tuned to sit under the lines — and sold them into China in volume. In October 2023 the rules were rewritten to capture exactly those chips: the interconnect criterion was dropped and a performance-density parameter was added, which BIS explained was intended to prevent the workaround of buying a larger number of smaller chips that together matched a restricted one, per CSET's explainer of the update and CSIS's analysis of the same rules. Nvidia responded with the H20, built for China under the new limits. The H20 was then made licensable in April 2025, with the consequences set out at the head of this chapter. In July 2025 Washington signalled that H20 sales could resume, per CNBC; in August 2025 it was reported by the Associated Press, CNN, Al Jazeera and Fortune that Nvidia and AMD had agreed to remit fifteen per cent of revenue from certain China chip sales to the United States government in exchange for licences. In the same month Chinese regulators publicly questioned the H20's security and reportedly told major domestic buyers to suspend purchases, per Bloomberg and CNBC.

There is a structural trade-off inside this instrument, and it is the same trade-off an auditor meets in every rules-versus-judgement debate. A bright quantitative line is administrable: an exporter can measure its own product and know the answer. It is also gameable, because anything measurable can be optimised against. Discretionary case-by-case review is the reverse — much harder to design around, and much harder to plan a three-year product roadmap against, because the answer is not knowable in advance. The January 2026 shift moved one product class from the first regime towards the second.

## Instrument three: entity listings

The third instrument names companies.

Huawei and sixty-eight non-United States affiliates were added to the Entity List effective 16 May 2019, per the Federal Register notice of that month; the Commerce Department extended a foreign direct product rule to Huawei in May 2020 and broadened it in August 2020, per its own announcements at the time. SMIC was added in December 2020, per contemporaneous law-firm analyses of that month's additions. On 2 December 2024 the Bureau of Industry and Security added one hundred and forty entities, including firms in China, Japan, South Korea and Singapore, and in the same package brought high-bandwidth memory under control, applying to United States-origin HBM and to foreign-produced HBM caught by the advanced-computing foreign direct product rule, with a new licence exception for certain shipments; the additions and HBM controls took effect on 31 December 2024, per the BIS announcement and contemporaneous analyses by Covington, WilmerHale and Alston & Bird.

The most consequential recent change to this instrument was not a new name. On 30 September 2025 BIS issued an interim final rule extending Entity List and Military End-User restrictions to any entity fifty per cent or more owned, directly or indirectly, by one or more listed parties, per analyses published by Morgan Lewis, Ropes & Gray, Sidley Austin and others in the weeks that followed. Before that rule, subsidiaries and affiliates of a listed company were not themselves restricted unless separately listed.

Read that as an accountant reads it. Screening against a published list is a cheap, automatable control: match the counterparty name, keep the evidence. Establishing beneficial ownership through indirect chains is a different kind of work — it needs data the exporter does not hold, about entities that are not on any list, refreshed continuously. The cost lands in compliance headcount, in slower order-to-shipment cycles, and in transactions that do not get done because the diligence could not be completed in time. None of that appears as a line item. It appears as a slightly higher operating expense ratio and a slightly longer sales cycle, in companies that will never explain why.

One more feature of the instrument deserves recording, because it shows that announcement alone has effects. The Artificial Intelligence Diffusion Framework was issued on 15 January 2025 with enforcement due to begin on 15 May 2025; the Commerce Department announced its rescission on 13 May 2025, two days before that date, per the BIS press release and law-firm alerts of that month. The rule never operated. It nonetheless sat inside procurement decisions, capacity plans and contract negotiations for four months. A rule that is proposed changes behaviour; a rule that is withdrawn does not fully un-change it.

## How a control appears in the accounts

This is the part of the subject that belongs to the accountant, and it is more tractable than the policy.

| Mechanism | Where it lands | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Licence denied on finished or in-process goods | Inventory provision in cost of sales | Size of the charge; the restated gross margin excluding it |
| Commitments to suppliers for goods now unsellable | Onerous purchase obligations, charged immediately | Whether the charge covers obligations as well as stock |
| Orders that cannot ship | Revenue not recognised; guidance withdrawn or reset | Quantified revenue effect in the outlook statement |
| Licence later granted or product resold elsewhere | Partial reversal of the earlier provision | Reversal amounts; margin overstated in the quarter of release |
| Market permanently closed | Geographic revenue note; segment disclosure | The trend line, not the single period |
| Broader listing rules | Operating expenses; sales cycle length | Rarely disclosed; infer from margin and working capital |

The reversals matter as much as the charges, and they are easier to miss. In its quarter ended July 2025 Nvidia recorded a one hundred and eighty million dollar release of previously reserved H20 inventory, arising from roughly six hundred and fifty million dollars of H20 sales to a customer outside China; reported non-GAAP gross margin that quarter was seventy-two point seven per cent, and would have been seventy-two point three without the release, per the results announced on 27 August 2025. AMD, having expected an eight hundred million dollar charge on the MI308 in April 2025 — which reduced non-GAAP gross margin by ten percentage points to forty-three per cent in the second quarter of that year, with one point five to one point eight billion dollars of revenue indicated as lost for the year — began shipping again in the fourth quarter of 2025 and reversed approximately three hundred and sixty million dollars of those charges, per its 2025 annual reporting.

The asymmetry is the lesson. The charge arrives in one quarter, in one number, and depresses that quarter's margin. The reversal arrives later, in pieces, and flatters a different quarter's margin. Anyone comparing gross margin across those periods without stripping both is comparing two artefacts of policy rather than two quarters of trading.

Take an illustrative accelerator, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Suppose a designer commits to a foundry for four hundred million dollars of wafer starts and to a memory supplier for two hundred million dollars of stacked memory, twelve months ahead, for a part built to sit just under a control threshold. Six months later the threshold is lowered. The part is now licensable and no licence is forthcoming. Finished goods and work in progress are written down to net realisable value, which in a market of one buyer is close to nothing. The purchase commitments do not disappear because the demand did: the obligation is recognised as a cost immediately, whether or not any wafer has yet been started. Two hundred million dollars of orders in hand are never recognised as revenue. Nothing in that sequence involves a failure of engineering, forecasting or credit. It is the ordinary consequence of a business whose product specification is defined by a document it does not control.

## The strategic response you can see from outside

Four responses show up in public information often enough to be treated as the standard playbook.

The first is product bifurcation. A separate part is designed for the restricted market, which means a separate validation programme, a separate software branch, separate documentation and separate inventory. That cost is real and is almost never disclosed separately.

The second is exit from forecasting. When a market becomes unpredictable enough, companies stop including it. Nvidia's chief executive said at a Citadel Securities event in October 2025 that the company's share of China's AI accelerator market had gone from ninety-five per cent to zero, per Fortune's report of the remarks. For context on what was lost, that market produced about seventeen billion dollars of revenue, roughly thirteen per cent of the company's total, in the fiscal year ended January 2025, per the company's annual report. The traffic was not one-way: in November 2025 Reuters reported that new Chinese data-centre projects receiving state funds were barred from using foreign AI chips at all, with projects less than thirty per cent complete ordered to remove foreign hardware already installed.

The third is acceleration of purchases ahead of expected restrictions. China's semiconductor manufacturing equipment imports reached a record fifty-one point one billion US dollars in 2025, up four per cent on 2024 and close to five times the level of a decade earlier, per the Silverado Policy Accelerator's 2026 compilation of Chinese customs data. The same compilation shows the United States share of those imports falling from twenty-three per cent in 2016 to nine per cent in 2025. Both numbers are informative and they point in different directions: the total says the buying continued, and the share says who stopped supplying it.

The fourth is the working-capital consequence of mismatched clocks. A wafer takes months to process and a leading-edge product takes years to design. A rule can change in a fortnight. Any manufacturer whose product cycle is longer than the policy cycle is structurally exposed to owning inventory whose legality changes mid-production, and the only defences available are shorter commitments, more flexible allocation and higher provisions — all of which cost margin.

## Second-order effect one: domestic substitution accelerates

The effect that is most often missed is the one that operates on the other side of the boundary, and it is worth stating as a mechanism rather than as a consequence.

The hardest thing for a challenger in capital equipment or advanced chips to obtain is not capital, and usually not engineers. It is a first customer willing to run an immature product in a production line where a defect costs a wafer lot. Incumbent tools are qualified, understood and supported. A buyer who has an alternative will not take the risk. An export control removes the alternative, and in doing so hands the challenger the one input that money cannot easily buy. The Center for Strategic and International Studies has made this argument directly in its 2025 and 2026 analyses of allied controls and Chinese localisation, and the reader should note that it is an argument about incentives, made by people who study the policy rather than by the companies affected.

Then comes the ratchet. Qualifying a tool into a process flow is slow and expensive; removing a qualified tool is also slow and expensive, and risks the yield that took months to stabilise. So the substitution does not simply reverse when policy loosens. The switching cost that once protected the incumbent now protects the entrant.

Naura's 2024 revenue was twenty-nine point eight billion yuan, up thirty-five point one per cent year on year, per reporting of the company's results; forecasts compiled from securities houses and reported by China Daily Hong Kong in February 2026 put 2025 revenue between forty-six point eight and fifty-two billion yuan. That last range is an estimate, not a filed figure, and should be carried as one.

The same pattern is now visible one layer across, in memory. Chinese producers are reported to be targeting domestic HBM3 production by the end of 2026, with CXMT the named producer and domestic toolmakers supplying assembly equipment, per 2026 reporting by Tom's Hardware; capacity projections circulating in 2026 — roughly ten thousand wafers per month by the end of 2026 rising to forty thousand by the end of 2028 from Morgan Stanley, and higher figures from SemiAnalysis for 2027 and 2028 — are analyst estimates about private companies, unaudited and unconfirmed, and are recorded here only to show the direction of the effort.

Substitution has hard limits, and honesty requires stating them. The furthest upstream tool has no domestic equivalent anywhere. Reporting since December 2025 has described a state-backed consortium operating an extreme-ultraviolet prototype in Shenzhen with a light output of one hundred to one hundred and fifty watts, against the two hundred and fifty watts ASML's source achieved in 2017 and a current benchmark around six hundred; the reported target for production use was 2028, with sources in the same coverage suggesting 2030 was more realistic, per Asia Times in December 2025 and subsequent industry analysis through 2026. Domestic accelerators have likewise proved more convincing at serving trained models than at training new ones. Substitution is real, it is accelerating, and it is not yet complete at the leading edge. All three statements are true at once.

The accountant's summary of this effect is compact. A control converts a foreign vendor's revenue in the restricted market from a growing annuity into a declining one, and simultaneously funds a domestic competitor's climb up its learning curve by guaranteeing it customers. The first effect is visible in the vendor's geographic note. The second is visible in the challenger's revenue line. They are the same policy, recorded in two different sets of books.

## Second-order effect two: the incentive to design to the threshold

The other missed effect is what a number in a rule does to an engineering department.

No one designs to half a limit. Given a bright line, the rational product decision is to build the most capable thing that sits immediately beneath it, because every unit of capability forgone is revenue given away for nothing. The A800, H800 and H20 sequence is the documented case, but the incentive is general and applies to any threshold in any jurisdiction.

Three consequences follow, and each is observable.

The first is that products acquire odd shapes. Whatever is measured gets capped; whatever is not measured becomes the lever. If compute is controlled and memory bandwidth is not, effort moves to memory. If chip-level interconnect is controlled and system-level assembly is not, effort moves to the rack. The controlled dimension stops being the design objective and becomes a constraint to satisfy, which is a different engineering problem with different answers.

The second is that the system substitutes for the chip. If a single large part is restricted, many smaller compliant parts can be wired together to do similar work, at a cost in power, space and efficiency. This is precisely why the October 2023 rule added performance density, per BIS's own stated reasoning as reported by CSET. Note what that tells you about the dynamic: the regulator is optimising against the designer's response, and the designer will optimise against the new parameter in turn. Each iteration produces a more complicated rule and a more peculiar product.

The third is that the product's economic life becomes political rather than technical. A part whose defining specification is a number in a document has a design life equal to the stability of that number. That is an inventory and commitment exposure, and it can be measured: how much of the balance sheet, and how much of the purchase-obligation note, is tied to products whose salability depends on a threshold staying where it is. Very few companies present that figure. A reader can approximate it from the product mix and the geographic disclosure.

There is a fourth consequence on the other side of the line, and it is easy to miss. The threshold also sets the benchmark for the domestic competitor. A restricted-market chip built to just under the limit defines exactly what a domestic alternative has to beat to win the sale. Lower the threshold and the bar for domestic acceptability falls with it. The policy that constrains the foreign product also specifies, precisely, the performance a home-grown substitute needs to be good enough.

## The same instrument, pointed the other way

Export controls are not the property of one government, and the material inputs to this industry are as concentrated as the tools.

China imposed licensing on gallium and germanium exports in 2023 and, in December 2024, issued a directive prohibiting in principle the export of gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard materials to the United States, per analyses by CSIS and the United States International Trade Commission; CSIS has put China's share of primary gallium supply at about ninety-eight per cent. On 9 October 2025 it announced further measures on rare earths that extended to related technologies and know-how and imposed licensing requirements reaching foreign firms, per contemporaneous legal analyses. In November 2025 it suspended a set of those controls for one year and issued general licences covering rare earths, gallium, germanium, antimony and graphite, per Fortune's report of 1 November 2025 and law-firm summaries of the suspension.

The mechanics are identical to those set out above: a licence requirement, a review presumption, an extraterritorial reach, and a counterparty who now has an urgent reason to build a domestic alternative. A suspension with an expiry date has its own second-order effect, which is to compress diversification spending into a known window.

## What to watch, and where to find it

The practical residue of this chapter is a short reading list inside the filings.

Start with the geographic revenue note, and read the composition rather than the total: for an equipment vendor, leading-edge tools and trailing-edge tools face different competitive substitutes, and the trailing edge is where a domestic rival arrives first. Read the risk factors for a quantified estimate of restricted revenue — the six hundred to seven hundred and ten million dollar figure above is what a good one looks like. In the inventory and provisions notes, look for write-downs attributed to licence requirements, and read the purchase-obligation note alongside them, because that is where the commitment to buy goods that can no longer be sold is disclosed. Track reversals as carefully as charges, and strip both before comparing margins. Watch backlog and remaining performance obligations for adjustments rather than growth. Count product variants: a proliferation of market-specific SKUs is compliance cost made visible. And on the other side, watch the challenger's revenue line and its customer-qualification announcements, because that is where substitution stops being a policy argument and becomes a number.

Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot with a date attached, and several will already have moved. Review policies change without notice. Thresholds are rewritten when they stop biting. Entity lists grow between filings. The controlling texts are published in the Federal Register and in the equivalent official gazettes of the other jurisdictions involved; the financial consequences appear first in company results announcements and then in annual reports. Nothing here is a prediction and nothing here is investment advice.

An export control does two things at once. It removes a market from one company's forecast, and it creates one in another company's. Both effects are recorded in accounts, on opposite sides of a border, at different speeds — and the second one, being slower, is usually the one nobody is looking at.
