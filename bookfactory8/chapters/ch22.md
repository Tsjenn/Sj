# Three Companies, One Cycle

On 7 August 2026, the board of SK Hynix approved fifty-four trillion won — about thirty-eight billion US dollars — of spending on two factories that will not ship a single chip before the end of 2028, as reported by CNBC and the Korea JoongAng Daily that week. Nothing about that sentence would surprise anyone who has followed memory for thirty years. It is what memory makers do at the top of a cycle. They did it in 1995, in 2000, in 2017 and in 2021, and each time the capacity arrived into a different market from the one it was ordered for.

The previous two chapters told the story in sequence: first the decades in which DRAM was arguably the worst business in semiconductors, then the arrival of high-bandwidth memory, which turned a commodity into a qualified, contracted, sold-out component. This chapter puts the three companies that survived long enough to enjoy it side by side. They are not interchangeable. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron came through the consolidation with different shapes, different customers and different exposures, and the difference matters most at exactly the moment everyone stops thinking it does — which, on the evidence of 2026, is now.

One number frames everything that follows. Year to date in 2026, Samsung's shares were reported up about 114 per cent, SK Hynix's about 186 per cent and Micron's about 141 per cent, per IG and industry reporting in mid-2026. Those are extraordinary moves for companies of this size, and they will have changed by the time you read this. The task of this chapter is to explain why each company moved the way it did, and then to make the argument the share prices were not making: that a supercycle is still a cycle.

## How three became the only three

The field was not always this small. Through the 1980s and 1990s dozens of companies made DRAM — American, Japanese, Korean, Taiwanese, German. The commodity economics described earlier in this part ground them down one downturn at a time. The last two exits were the definitive ones. Qimonda, the German maker spun out of Infineon, filed for insolvency in January 2009 after a price collapse it could not outlast, a failure covered at the time by IEEE Spectrum as Germany's attempted rescue fell apart; it was the first DRAM maker in the industry's history to go all the way to liquidation rather than being absorbed. Japan's Elpida followed into bankruptcy in February 2012, and Micron picked up its assets the following year, taking the industry from five meaningful DRAM makers to three.

Three is a number with consequences. With dozens of producers, no one's capacity decision moves the market and everyone overbuilds. With three, each company can see the other two, and the textbook expectation is that supply discipline improves. It did, somewhat, through the 2010s — and yet, as we will see below, three disciplined survivors still managed to deliver one of the worst downturns in the industry's history in 2023. Keep that in mind through everything that follows. Concentration changed the amplitude of the cycle. Nothing so far has repealed it.

The three that remain divide the market in a way that is easier to see than to quantify on any single date. In the first quarter of 2025, per Counterpoint Research figures reported by the Korea Herald in April 2025, SK Hynix took 36 per cent of global DRAM revenue against Samsung's 34 and Micron's 25 — the first quarter in which SK Hynix had led the DRAM market since its founding in 1983, ending a Samsung reign of more than three decades. Shares shift quarter to quarter; the point is the shape. Two Korean companies hold roughly two-thirds of the world's DRAM between them, and one American company holds most of the rest.

## Samsung: scale, breadth, and a foundry of its own

Samsung is the conglomerate of the three, and its memory business sits inside a company that also makes phones, televisions, displays and — critically — other people's chips. That breadth cuts both ways, and 2025 showed the cutting edge. For that full year, SK Hynix reported operating profit of 47.2 trillion won against Samsung's 43.6 trillion, the first year in which the smaller, memory-only rival out-earned the whole of Samsung Electronics, as reported by CNBC in January 2026. A company one-fifth Samsung's breadth beat it on absolute profit, because it was concentrated in the one product the AI build-out wanted most and Samsung was late to it.

Late is not absent. Samsung's second-quarter 2026 results, reported in July 2026 by Investing.com and The Tech Outlook among others, showed the recovery in full: revenue of 171.5 trillion won, operating profit of 89.5 trillion won, an operating margin of about 52 per cent, up from 43 per cent the quarter before, with operating profit up roughly eighteen-fold on the same quarter a year earlier per Data Centre Dynamics. Management told investors that HBM4 sales should more than triple in the third quarter and account for well over 60 per cent of its HBM revenue in the second half. DRAM and NAND bit shipments both set records. When memory prices rise, the largest memory maker by capacity is mechanically the largest beneficiary, whatever the league table of technology leadership says.

The strategic asset that distinguishes Samsung from the other two is the foundry. Samsung is the only memory maker that also runs a leading-edge logic foundry, competing — at a distance — with TSMC. For years that business was a drag: underutilised, yield-challenged, and struggling to win marquee customers. The tide began turning before the memory boom did. In July 2025, Tesla signed a multiyear agreement reported at 16.5 billion US dollars, running to 2033, for Samsung to manufacture its AI6 chips at the fab in Taylor, Texas, as reported by CNBC and Bloomberg at the time; KED Global noted the contract was equivalent to about 7.6 per cent of Samsung's entire 2024 revenue, one of the largest single-customer foundry orders the company had ever won. By the second quarter of 2026 the company was describing the foundry business as significantly improved, citing HBM base-die demand and strong US customer orders, per the July 2026 results coverage.

Note what that last clause means for the four-layer argument of this book. HBM4 moves the base die — the logic layer at the bottom of the memory stack — onto a foundry process. A memory maker that owns a foundry can build that die in-house. Samsung's weakness in HBM's early generations and its unique breadth may partially offset each other in the generation now shipping. Whether they do is a question the 2027 accounts will answer; it is not answerable from a share price.

Samsung's exposure, then, is the mirror of its strength. It earns from memory, logic, foundry and consumer products at once, which softens any single blow — and dilutes any single boom. Investors wanting pure exposure to the memory shortage did not buy Samsung in 2026, and the share-price table above shows it: the broadest company posted the smallest rise of the three.

## SK Hynix: the company that owned the shortage

SK Hynix is the concentrated bet. It is overwhelmingly a memory company, and within memory it made the early, expensive, unfashionable commitment to high-bandwidth memory that the previous chapter described. When AI accelerators turned out to be limited by memory bandwidth, SK Hynix was holding the one product that mattered, with years of packaging experience its rivals had to build in a hurry.

The market-share consequence has already been stated: first place in DRAM revenue from the first quarter of 2025, per Counterpoint. Within HBM specifically, Counterpoint Research put SK Hynix's share at 58 per cent in the first quarter of 2026, with industry analysis through the year continuing to rank it first — the same industry analysis that estimated the 2026 HBM market at about 54.6 billion US dollars, up roughly 58 per cent year on year, with capacity reported sold out through 2026 across all the major suppliers. On its October 2025 earnings call, per CNBC's reporting, the company said its HBM, DRAM and NAND capacity was essentially sold out for the following year. There are very few businesses anywhere in industry that can say that sentence.

The profit consequence arrived in stages. Record annual operating profit of 47.2 trillion won in 2025, past Samsung for the first time, reported by CNBC in January 2026. Then a second quarter of 2026, reported in July, that belongs in a textbook: revenue of 79.3 trillion won and operating income of 60.5 trillion won, both records, with operating profit up 557 per cent year on year — an operating margin around 76 per cent, per the results coverage by Investing.com. Stop on that number. Seventy-six per cent is not a semiconductor margin. It is not even a software margin in most years. It is the margin of a company selling a product its customers cannot get anywhere else at a price the shortage sets.

And here the accountant reaches for the cold water, because the market did it first. On the day those record results were published, SK Hynix's shares fell nearly nine per cent, as CNBC reported in July 2026 — record revenue and a 557 per cent profit increase had come in below what an AI-charged market had already priced. When a record quarter disappoints, the share price has stopped measuring the company and started measuring the crowd's expectations of the crowd. Chapter two made this point in the abstract; SK Hynix in July 2026 is the concrete case.

The product roadmap explains why the lead has held so far. The same second-quarter results coverage, via the company's newsroom and PR Newswire in July 2026, recorded that mass-production shipments of HBM4 — the generation now being designed into the newest accelerators — began in the quarter, with a broader ramp planned for the second half, and that samples of the following generation, HBM4E, had already been delivered in the first half of 2026 ahead of volume production targeted for 2027. In high-bandwidth memory the qualification cycle is long and the customer's penalty for a bad part is severe, so shipping the next generation first is worth more than it would be in any commodity: it means the customer's engineering effort, and often the customer's committed volume, lands with you before your rivals have a part to test. That is the treadmill SK Hynix has stayed ahead on since the shortage began. Staying ahead is also the whole of the moat — each generation resets the race, and both rivals are now closer behind than they were a generation ago.

SK Hynix's exposure is the same as its strength, undiluted. It is the purest memory company of the three, which is why it rose 186 per cent while the others rose less, and it is what a 76 per cent operating margin looks like on the way up. The same concentration will decide what it looks like on the way down. A margin set by shortage is repriced by supply, and — as the final section of this chapter sets out — the supply is already being poured, some of it by SK Hynix itself.

## Micron: the American supplier

Micron is the smallest of the three by memory share and the only one headquartered outside Korea, and both facts define its position. Its results in 2026 were, if anything, the most startling of the three. For its third quarter of fiscal 2026, ended in May and announced in June 2026 via GlobeNewswire, Micron reported revenue of 41.46 billion US dollars — up 346 per cent from 9.30 billion a year earlier and its fifth consecutive quarterly revenue record, per TradingKey's coverage — with a gross margin of 84.6 per cent, attributed to HBM demand and multi-year fixed-price agreements. It guided the following quarter to around 50 billion dollars of revenue at an 86 per cent gross margin; TradingKey observed that the single quarter's guidance exceeded the whole of Micron's revenue for fiscal 2024.

Two line items in that paragraph deserve an accountant's pause. The first is "multi-year fixed-price agreements". Memory has historically been sold on spot and short-term contract prices that moved with the cycle; a supplier locking customers into multi-year fixed prices is behaving like a company that believes today's prices are worth insuring. It smooths future revenue — and it also caps the upside if spot prices keep climbing, and turns into a cushion if they fall. Where those contracts sit relative to the eventual market price will be one of the more interesting disclosures of 2027 and 2028. The second is the gross margin itself: 84.6 per cent, in a business that three years earlier had a negative one. We will come back to that.

Micron's strategic position is geographic as much as technological. It is the American supplier in an industry Washington has decided is strategic. In December 2024 the US Department of Commerce finalised up to 6.1 billion dollars of direct CHIPS Act funding for Micron — about 4.6 billion for new fabs in Clay, New York and 1.5 billion for Boise, Idaho, milestone-based, within what the department described as a roughly 125 billion dollar multi-decade US investment programme, per the Commerce Department's announcement and CNBC's reporting of 10 December 2024. In a world of export controls and supply-chain politics, being the domestic memory maker is an asset that appears nowhere on the balance sheet and yet shapes capital allocation, customer relationships and political protection. It is also an obligation: those fabs are commitments to build high-cost capacity in high-cost locations, made for reasons that are not purely commercial.

On technology, Micron spent the HBM era as the fast follower — a distant third in early generations, then closing. Its own investor materials, in the fiscal 2026 first-quarter prepared remarks, reported that combined revenue from HBM, high-capacity DIMMs and LP server DRAM reached 10 billion dollars in fiscal 2025, more than five times the prior year, and management forecast the HBM market growing from roughly 35 billion dollars in 2025 to around 100 billion in 2028 — a company forecast, to be read as one. Industry analysis in 2026 projected the HBM4 generation splitting more evenly across the three suppliers than earlier generations did. If that happens, the shortage premium spreads; it also, by definition, erodes.

That is a reasonable question. It is also the only question, and that should worry a careful reader, because this industry has heard the phrase "this time is different" before, at each of its tops, from people holding charts very like these.

## A supercycle is still a cycle

Now the counterweight, stated as plainly as the boom was.

Memory is, historically, the most cyclical business in semiconductors — more cyclical than foundry, than equipment, than logic design. The mechanics were set out earlier in this part and bear compressing into one paragraph: demand for bits grows steadily, supply arrives in giant, indivisible, multi-year increments, every producer faces the same prices at the same time, and the marginal cost of a wafer already started is low enough that no one stops running fabs even at a loss. The result, over four decades, has been a sawtooth: shortage, spike, investment, glut, collapse, retrenchment, shortage.

You do not need to reach back to the 1990s for evidence. Consider what these same three companies reported barely three years before the quarters celebrated above. For calendar 2023, SK Hynix reported an annual operating loss of 7.73 trillion won and a net loss of 9.14 trillion, per its results published in January 2024 — its first annual operating loss in a decade, after what CNBC in April 2023 had reported as a record quarterly loss. Micron lost 5.8 billion dollars in fiscal 2023 as revenue nearly halved from the year before, having earned 8.7 billion in fiscal 2022, and finished that year with a gross margin of minus 9.1 per cent — selling chips for less than they cost to make — figures the Motley Fool revisited pointedly in August 2026 under a headline noting how the last memory boom ended. The same company that reported an 84.6 per cent gross margin in June 2026 reported a negative one in 2023. That is not a story about management quality changing. It is the cycle, read at two different points.

The bull case says HBM broke the machine: qualified parts, multi-year contracts, capacity sold out years ahead, a component specified into an accelerator rather than a commodity bought by the megabyte. All of that is true, and the previous chapter took it seriously. But walk through what each protection actually protects against. Qualification slows a rival's entry; it does not stop three qualified suppliers from over-building simultaneously. Contracts fix a price for the buyer's benefit as surely as the seller's; they insure Micron against a fall and its customers against a rise, and which party got the better of that insurance will only be known later. "Sold out through 2026" is a statement about 2026 — capacity coming online in 2028 and 2029 is not sold out, because most of it does not exist yet. And conventional DRAM and NAND, which still produce a large share of all three companies' revenue, remain as commodity-priced as ever; the 2026 price surge that Samsung itself warned about, per Network World's reporting, lifted those products too, and what a shortage lifts, a glut lowers.

It is worth asking why three-player discipline did not prevent the 2023 collapse, because the answer bears directly on what discipline is worth now. Consolidation was supposed to end the game the industry calls chicken: everyone builds, everyone loses, the weakest exits. But three rational players still face the same trap in miniature. Cutting your own output supports the price for all three, at your sole expense; the other two collect the benefit without paying. So each waits for the others to cut first, and the downturn runs longer than any of them intended. That is what 2022 and 2023 looked like from the inside — production cuts came, but late, after the losses recorded above. Discipline among three is real, and it is also slow, and slow is expensive in a business where a quarter's delay is measured in trillions of won.

None of this is a prediction that the cycle turns in any particular quarter, and — as this book has said before and will say again — nothing here is investment advice. Margins of 76 and 84 per cent may persist for years or compress next year; the honest answer is that nobody knows, including the companies. What history supports is narrower and more useful: every previous memory shortage has ended the same way, through supply, and the supply that ends it is always ordered during the shortage, by the companies enjoying it, in full knowledge of the history. Which brings us back to the board meeting this chapter opened with.

## Fifty-four trillion won of poured concrete

The details of SK Hynix's August 2026 decision, per CNBC and the Korea JoongAng Daily on 7 August 2026, are worth setting down precisely, because they are a working model of how this industry answers a shortage. The board approved about 54 trillion won — roughly 38.1 billion US dollars at the reported conversion — split between two projects: around 35.2 trillion won for the Y2 fab at Yongin, a DRAM plant whose construction begins in July 2027 with a first cleanroom due in June 2029, and around 19.1 trillion won for the M17 NAND fab at Cheongju, breaking ground in February 2027 with a cleanroom due in December 2028. Both sit inside a larger blueprint, reported at 600 trillion won, for four fabs at the Yongin cluster — a completion target the company pulled forward from 2045 to 2033. As the Motley Fool observed in August 2026, not one chip emerges from any of it before December 2028.

Nor is SK Hynix building alone. TrendForce projected in November 2025 that DRAM industry capital expenditure would rise from 53.7 billion dollars in 2025 to 61.3 billion in 2026, up about 14 per cent:

| DRAM capital expenditure, 2026 projection | Amount | Change on 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| SK Hynix | 20.5 billion US dollars | up 17% |
| Samsung | about 20 billion US dollars | up 11% |
| Micron | 13.5 billion US dollars | up 23% |
| Industry total | 61.3 billion US dollars | up about 14% |

Those are TrendForce's November 2025 projections, not outturns, and the August 2026 fab approvals suggest the eventual totals will be higher, not lower. Data Centre Dynamics separately reported Samsung looking to expand production capacity by around 50 per cent in 2026. Micron's CHIPS-funded fabs in New York and Idaho are part of the same wave. Every player is doing the individually rational thing: prices are extraordinary, capacity is sold out, customers are begging, and a company that declines to build simply hands share to the two that do not decline. The industry has never found a way out of that logic, and it is not obvious one exists. Three players see each other perfectly and build anyway, because the punishment for building too much arrives years later and the punishment for building too little arrives now.

So read the 38 billion dollars twice, the way this book keeps asking you to read numbers twice. Read once, it is confidence: a company at record margins committing to the future its customers keep ordering. Read twice, it is the cycle's oldest sentence being typed out again — enormous capacity, ordered at the top of the price curve, arriving on a fixed schedule into a demand environment nobody can see from here. In 2029, the Yongin cleanroom will open into whatever market 2029 turns out to be. The concrete does not care.

## What to watch, from the accounts

A chapter that ends "it is a cycle" owes the reader something better than a shrug, and the tools from earlier in this book say where to look. Watch inventory first: in every previous downturn, days of inventory at the memory makers rose quarters before prices broke, because chips pile up before anyone admits demand has softened. Watch the gap between capital expenditure and depreciation, which measures how much future supply is being built relative to the present cost base; at all three companies that gap is now historically wide, which is precisely what the top of a memory cycle has always looked like. Watch the mix disclosures — how much revenue is genuinely HBM and contracted, versus conventional DRAM and NAND riding a spot price. And watch the fixed-price contract commentary in Micron's filings, because the first cycle to test those agreements will reveal whether memory really did change its nature or merely its vocabulary.

One more line item deserves a place on that list: customer concentration. The HBM boom is, at bottom, demand from a small number of accelerator designers and the hyperscale buyers behind them, and the customer-concentration notes in each memory maker's annual filings will show how much of the record revenue traces back to how few names. A supplier sold out to a broad market and a supplier sold out to three customers report the same margin in the good year; they do not experience the same bad year. The disclosure is brief, buried and audited, which makes it exactly the kind of number this book prefers to a headline.

Three companies survived forty years of the worst business in semiconductors and were rewarded, in 2026, with the best two years the industry has ever printed. They earned it — through consolidation, through technology nobody else mastered quickly, and in SK Hynix's case through a bet made when it looked foolish. Nothing in that record repeals the sawtooth. Samsung's breadth, SK Hynix's crown and Micron's flag are three strategies for riding the same wave, and the wave, on all the evidence forty years can offer, still comes down. The next chapter climbs one layer up the stack, to the companies buying every chip the three of them can make — and to what all that hardware becomes when it lands on a balance sheet.
