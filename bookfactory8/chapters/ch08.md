# TSMC: The Company Everything Runs Through

A three-hundred-millimetre silicon wafer is about the size of a dinner plate. As at early 2026, roughly ninety-two per cent of the world's most advanced logic chips — those at five nanometres and below — were manufactured in Taiwan, per 2026 industry analysis of advanced-node capacity. Most of them left the same company's fabs.

That company is Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company. It does not sell anything a consumer can buy. It has no consumer brand, no retail presence and no product with a name anyone would recognise. It stood at roughly 2.17 trillion US dollars of market capitalisation as at August 2026, per companiesmarketcap, which made it one of the most valuable companies on earth and, by some distance, the least famous of them.

This chapter asks the same five questions the book asks of every company, in the same order: where it came from, what it is trying to own, what its accounts say, what could go wrong, and what is genuinely open to it.

## Origin: manufacturing as a service

Morris Chang spent a long career in the American semiconductor industry and rose to senior executive rank at Texas Instruments before the Taiwanese government recruited him, in 1985, to run the Industrial Technology Research Institute, per Britannica's biographical entry and the IEEE Spectrum profile of him. He was in his mid-fifties, and the job he had been handed was to build a semiconductor industry in a place that did not have one.

The company opened for business in February 1987 with 220 million US dollars of capital, according to company histories including Britannica's. The National Development Fund — the Taiwanese state — took forty-eight per cent. Philips took just under twenty-eight per cent, contributing cash, production technology and licensed intellectual property. The remainder came from private investors, several of them local manufacturing families. Both Intel and Texas Instruments were approached and declined to invest, which is worth remembering whenever a strategic mistake is described as obvious in hindsight.

The idea being funded was not a technical one. It was an accounting one, though nobody described it that way at the time.

In 1987 a semiconductor fabrication plant was a by-product of designing chips. A company designed a device, and it built a plant to make it, because there was no other way to get it made. The plant was a cost of being in the business — a very large fixed cost, sized to the design pipeline of one company, and idle whenever that pipeline was thin. Chang's proposition inverted the relationship. Manufacturing, he argued, could be the product. A company could exist that made chips it had not designed, for customers whose designs it never saw the inside of, and sold capacity the way a printer sells print runs.

Two consequences followed, and both are still doing the work today.

The first is commercial rather than industrial. A manufacturer that never designs competes with none of its customers. That sounds like a slogan until you consider the alternative: a designer asking a rival to build its most valuable product, hand over the design files required to do so, and be trusted not to learn from them. The pure-play foundry removed the conflict by removing the second business. Neutrality, in this industry, is a balance-sheet position: it is the decision not to own a product line.

The second is financial. The largest fixed cost in semiconductors became a purchasable service. A company with no plant, no clean room and no process engineers could now bring a chip to market. That company did not exist in any number in 1987. It exists in great number now, and it is called a fabless designer. Nvidia, founded in 1993 with backing from Sequoia Capital and Sutter Hill Ventures — not, as several popular accounts have it, from SoftBank — is the largest example, and gets three chapters of its own later.

The early years were unglamorous. The first customers were established manufacturers such as Intel, Motorola and Texas Instruments, handing over production of parts built on technology they had already moved past but still had orders for, per company histories. The foundry began as a place to put work that the owner of the design no longer wanted to house. It ended as the place where nearly all of the difficult work goes. Britannica's company history records that by 2005 it held about half the foundry market.

## Strategy: why the pure-play model won, and kept winning

It is tempting to explain the outcome by saying the company executed well. It did, but execution is not a mechanism, and mechanisms are what a reader can check.

### Pooling

Start with utilisation, because a fab is almost entirely fixed cost. Take an illustrative fab, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Suppose it costs twenty billion dollars to build and is written off over five years. That is four billion dollars a year of depreciation, incurred whether the machines run or not, plus staff, maintenance and materials. Suppose it can produce one hundred thousand wafers a year.

Now suppose three chip designers each need thirty thousand wafers a year. If each builds its own fab, each carries four billion of annual depreciation against thirty thousand wafers — roughly one hundred and thirty-three thousand dollars of fixed cost per wafer — and each fab sits seventy per cent empty. If instead all three buy from one shared fab, the same four billion is spread across ninety thousand wafers, or about forty-four thousand dollars each. The shared plant is three times cheaper per unit before anyone has improved a single process step. Again: illustrative numbers, no real company.

That is the whole argument for the foundry model in one sum. Demand that is lumpy and uncertain company by company is smooth and predictable in aggregate. The foundry sells insurance against its customers' own volatility, and charges a margin for it.

### Learning

The second mechanism compounds the first. Semiconductor manufacturing improves with volume. Yield — the proportion of dies on a wafer that work — climbs as engineers see more silicon, more defects and more failure modes. A foundry running the designs of dozens of customers sees more silicon in a quarter than a single-product manufacturer sees in a year. The learning is not transferable to the customer, because the customer does not run the process. It stays inside the foundry, and shows up as gross margin.

### Escalating cost, and the shape of the result

The third mechanism is the one that turned a good business into a structural position. The fixed cost of the leading edge has risen faster than the number of firms able to bear it. International Business Strategies estimated in 2026 that a 2-nanometre-capable fab with monthly capacity of around fifty thousand wafers would cost roughly twenty-eight billion US dollars to establish, with wafer prices near thirty thousand dollars, as reported by Tom's Hardware in 2026. That is a consultancy's estimate rather than a company disclosure, and should be read as such, but the order of magnitude is not controversial.

Against that, the company's own capital budget. It spent 40.9 billion US dollars on capital expenditure in 2025 and guided 2026 capital expenditure to 52 to 56 billion US dollars, above analyst expectations, with about seventy to eighty per cent of that budget allocated to advanced process technologies (company results and fourth-quarter 2025 earnings call, January 2026). In July 2026 it raised the 2026 range to 60 to 64 billion US dollars alongside its second-quarter results, as reported at the time. Both figures are correct as at their dates, and the gap between them in six months is itself the lesson: capital plans in this industry are revised upward mid-year more often than they are revised down.

The result is a market structure that economists would call a natural monopoly at the leading edge — a market where one supplier can serve total demand at a lower average cost than two could, because fixed costs dominate and volume is the only way to spread them. The phrase carries no accusation. It describes a cost curve, not conduct.

The shares follow the curve. Foundry revenue share in the first quarter of 2026 was reported by TrendForce at 72.3 per cent for TSMC, up from 70.4 per cent in the previous quarter, with Samsung second at 6.5 per cent, SMIC at 5.1 per cent, UMC at 3.9 per cent and GlobalFoundries at 3.3 per cent, against a record top-ten foundry revenue of 47.95 billion US dollars in the quarter. Those are quarterly figures from a research firm rather than audited numbers, and they move; the structural point they describe has been stable for years.

Two companies other than TSMC do operate leading-edge logic processes, and this book treats both seriously and returns to them later. The point here is narrower: at the most advanced nodes, in 2026, the market behaved as though there was one supplier of consequence, and priced accordingly.

### Where the margin comes from

Two things move a foundry's gross margin: utilisation and mix.

Utilisation is the illustrative sum from earlier, applied to real plant. Because so much of cost of revenue is depreciation and other fixed cost, the difference between a full fab and a three-quarters-full fab falls almost entirely to gross profit. This is why foundry margins swing violently through a cycle, and why the same company can look like a wonderful business and a mediocre one within eighteen months without anything changing in its technology.

Mix is the node profile. In the second quarter of 2026, 3-nanometre accounted for thirty per cent of total wafer revenue, 5-nanometre thirty-three per cent, 7-nanometre eleven per cent and the newly ramping 2-nanometre three per cent (second-quarter 2026 results, 16 July 2026). High-performance computing — the platform category containing AI accelerators — was sixty-six per cent of revenue in the quarter.

A new node dilutes margin before it improves it. Guidance for the third quarter of 2026, given on 16 July 2026, was revenue of 44.6 to 45.8 billion US dollars, gross margin of sixty-five to sixty-seven per cent and operating margin of fifty-six to fifty-eight per cent, with management attributing about 1.7 percentage points of margin decline to the 2-nanometre ramp, partly offset by demand and cost improvement. That is the ordinary arithmetic of a fab filling up: the depreciation starts on day one, the yield arrives later.

### Capital expenditure is a forecast written in concrete

The single most important line in this company's accounts is not revenue. It is capital expenditure, because it is the only line that describes the future rather than the past.

Cash spent on a fab in 2026 becomes a fixed asset, and then becomes cost of revenue over the following years through depreciation. Revenue from that fab arrives when it is qualified, filled and yielding. The two are years apart. A reader who wants to know what the industry will be able to produce in 2029 should look at what is being committed now, and a reader who wants to know why margins compress in a downturn should look at how much depreciation was locked in during the boom.

There is a practical test here that costs nothing. Compare the growth rate of depreciation with the growth rate of revenue over several periods. When revenue is outrunning depreciation, capacity built earlier is being filled, and margin expands. When depreciation is outrunning revenue, the plant is arriving faster than the demand for it, and margin compresses. Nothing in that test requires a view about artificial intelligence.

### Customer concentration

TSMC does not name its customers in its filings. It quantifies the exposure instead. Its 2025 annual report on Form 20-F disclosed that its largest customer accounted for nineteen per cent of net revenue in 2025, its second largest for seventeen per cent, and its ten largest for approximately seventy-eight per cent.

CNBC reported in January 2026 that Nvidia was set to supplant Apple as the company's largest customer. The company itself confirms neither identity, and a careful reader should keep the reported attribution separate from the disclosed percentage. The percentage is audited. The name is journalism.

Seventy-eight per cent from ten customers is the number that matters, and it cuts in both directions. It is a concentration risk in the textbook sense: the loss of one relationship would be material. It is also a description of who the customers are. They are the largest and best-capitalised technology companies in the world, several of which have no practical alternative supplier for their most important products. Concentration is dangerous when the concentrated party can leave. Here, mostly, it cannot — at least not quickly, and not at the leading edge.

### One unusually observable company

Taiwanese listing rules require companies to publish monthly revenue by around the tenth of the following month. July 2026 consolidated revenue was NT$467.58 billion, up 44.7 per cent on July 2025, announced on 10 August 2026. Most large companies report four times a year; this one reports twelve. For anyone trying to follow the industry rather than read about it afterwards, that monthly disclosure is the single most useful public number in the supply chain, and it is free.

## Challenge: the geography problem

Everything above describes a strong position. The challenge is that almost all of it sits in one place.

The concentration figure is worth restating precisely, because it is often stated loosely: roughly ninety-two per cent of the world's most advanced logic — five nanometres and below — was manufactured in Taiwan as at early 2026, per 2026 industry analysis. That is not a statement about one company; it is a statement about an island. It is also the reason three governments have been willing to pay a large share of the cost of moving some of that capacity elsewhere.

This book describes export controls, subsidy programmes and Taiwan factually and takes no political position on any of them. Chapter sixty-eight deals with the subject at length. What belongs here is the corporate and accounting question: what does building outside Taiwan actually cost, and what does it actually buy?

Announced investment figures are commitments over many years, not amounts spent, and are among the most frequently revised numbers in the industry.

Two observations before the cost question.

First, note what is being built where. The most advanced process in volume production has, so far, stayed in Taiwan, and so has research and development. Arizona's new fabs are designated for 2 nanometre and below, but they are being built while Taiwan is already ramping that node. Germany's plant is a mature-node facility for European industrial and automotive customers. Diversifying capacity and diversifying the leading edge are different projects, and only the first is well advanced.

Second, note the timescales. Almost every date in that table lands in 2027 or later, for commitments announced in 2024, 2025 and 2026. Nothing in this industry can be moved quickly, which is the single most important fact about it and the one most often missing from the coverage.

Four of those five figures are about different things, which is why they do not reconcile. One is production cost per wafer. One is the price a customer is charged. One is a founder's judgement from before the plant existed. Anyone quoting a single number for "the cost of building in America" without saying which of those it is has skipped the only interesting part of the question.

What the company itself has said is narrower and more useful. Its finance function has guided that the ramp of overseas fabs would dilute group gross margin by two to three percentage points in the early stages, widening to three to four points in the later stages, with two to three points expected for the full year 2026, per its 2026 earnings calls. That is a company estimate of a company effect, given with a period attached, and it is the figure to track. If the actual dilution runs wider than guided, the cost of geographic diversification is higher than management expected. If it runs narrower, it is not.

### The subsidy question, in accounting terms

Roughly half the German plant's cost is public money. A material share of the Japanese plants' cost is public money. Several billion dollars of the American investment is grant money and loan support. This changes reported profitability for years, and not in the way most readers assume.

Government grants related to assets are generally either deducted from the carrying amount of the asset or recognised as income across the asset's useful life. Either way the effect is the same in direction: depreciation charged against future revenue is lower than it would have been had the company funded the whole plant itself. A subsidised fab therefore reports better margins than an unsubsidised one making identical products at identical cost, for as long as the asset is being written off.

This is not a criticism of anyone. It is a reading instruction. When comparing the profitability of plants in different countries, or of this company against a rival receiving different support, find the accounting policy note on property, plant and equipment and the note on government grants, and see how the support has been treated. The answer is disclosed. It is simply never in the headline.

## Opportunity, with the constraint attached

The obvious opportunity is more of what is already happening: high-performance computing at sixty-six per cent of revenue in the second quarter of 2026, a new node ramping, and a customer base that keeps raising its own capital budgets. That case does not need making twice.

Two less obvious points are worth the space.

The first is that the chokepoint has partly moved inside the company. Advanced packaging — assembling logic dies and stacks of memory onto a single substrate — became a binding constraint on accelerator supply, and the company's CoWoS packaging capacity has been described by management as extremely tight and sold out through 2026. Estimates of that capacity from the trade press and research firms in 2026 put it at roughly seventy-five thousand wafers a month at the end of 2025 rising towards one hundred and twenty-five to one hundred and forty thousand a month by the end of 2026, with TrendForce reporting in June 2026 that the supply-demand gap was expected to narrow from about twenty per cent to about ten per cent by the end of the year. Those are third-party estimates, not company disclosures, and they vary widely between sources — which is itself worth knowing before quoting any of them.

The strategic significance is that packaging is a second scarcity, structurally similar to the first: heavy fixed cost, long lead time, few credible suppliers. A company that owns two consecutive chokepoints in the same supply chain has a stronger position than one that owns either alone.

The second is pricing. Trade reporting during 2026 described price increases across advanced nodes affecting the large fabless customers, with Tom's Hardware naming Nvidia, AMD, Apple and Qualcomm among those facing higher wafer costs; Broadcom, which designs the custom accelerators that are the main alternative to buying merchant parts, sits in the same queue. The company does not publish a price list and has not confirmed those reports; treat them as what they are. But the mechanism is visible in the accounts regardless of what any individual report says. Gross margin of 67.7 per cent in the second quarter of 2026, against 59.9 per cent for the whole of 2025, is not achieved by cost reduction alone while the newest and most expensive node is ramping.

Now the constraint, which is not optional.

This is the most capital-intensive business in the world, in an industry with the most reliable cycle in the world. Capacity ordered into a shortage arrives, historically, into a glut, because everybody orders at the same time and the plant takes years. The demand behind the current expansion is concentrated in a small number of buyers who are themselves spending far ahead of their own revenue — a pattern examined in the compute chapters and not resolved there either. The company's own capital plan rose from 52 to 56 billion dollars to 60 to 64 billion dollars within a single year, which is a decision made on a forecast, by people who state plainly that it is a forecast.

None of that is a prediction, and none of it is advice. This book contains no price targets, no recommendation to buy or sell anything, and no view on where any share price goes next. The purpose of setting out the mechanism is that a reader who understands it can watch the disclosures themselves and reach their own conclusion earlier than the headlines will.

Every figure in this chapter carries a date because every figure in this chapter will change. The market capitalisation moves daily. The capital budget moved by eight billion dollars in six months. The margin guidance is revised each quarter. What does not change on that timetable is the structure: a business built in 1987 on the idea that manufacturing could be sold as a service, which turned the largest fixed cost in the industry into somebody else's operating expense, and which now stands between almost every designer of advanced chips and the physical existence of their products.

Most people cannot name it. Almost nothing in this book works without it.
