# Four Previous Times People Were Certain

On 27 March 2000 the shares of Cisco Systems closed at a split-adjusted record of eighty dollars and six cents, and the company passed Microsoft to become the most valuable publicly traded business in the world at a market capitalisation of about five hundred and fifty-five point four billion US dollars. At that price it traded at roughly thirty-nine times sales, and at a price-earnings ratio of about two hundred, per the market-history compilations of the period drawing on ycharts data. Nobody involved thought they were speculating. They thought they were buying the plumbing of a permanent change in how the world communicated, and on that point they were entirely correct.

That is the difficulty with this chapter. The four episodes in it were not manias about nothing. Each was built on a real technology, a real change in behaviour, and a set of arguments that a careful person could hold without embarrassment. Three of the four were followed by a correction severe enough to bankrupt companies that had been national champions. The fourth — the most recent — reversed so quickly that the industry appears to have filed it away as an aberration rather than a lesson.

## What this chapter is and is not

It is not a prediction. Nothing here says that the current build-out of artificial intelligence capacity will end the way the telecommunications build-out ended, and this book gives no price targets and no recommendation to buy or sell anything. The previous chapter set out why the semiconductor cycle is structural rather than a failure of management. This one does something narrower and more useful: it shows what the arguments sounded like the last four times, so that a reader hearing them again can recognise the shape.

It is also not an exercise in hindsight. Smugness about past booms is usually wrong on the facts. Internet traffic really did grow enormously. Fibre really was needed. Graphics processors really did turn out to be general-purpose computers. Laptops really were bought in unusual numbers in 2020 and 2021. In every case the technological thesis survived; what failed was the arithmetic that translated the thesis into an order book, a capital expenditure budget, and a valuation.

So each episode below is told the same way. What was claimed. What turned out to be true. What turned out to be wrong. And — because this is an accountant's book — which line item in the accounts told you first, usually a year or more before the share price did.

## 1999: internet infrastructure

### The claim

The load-bearing statistic of the late 1990s was that internet traffic was doubling every hundred days.

It had a respectable pedigree. A WorldCom press release of 19 February 1997 spoke of traffic over its backbone "almost doubling every quarter", and the claim was amplified through public presentations by executives of WorldCom and its internet subsidiary UUNET, then repeated by government officials, regulators and the press. Andrew Odlyzko, then at the University of Minnesota, spent years documenting where it came from and why it was wrong: the rate had plausibly been reached during the growth spurt of 1995 and 1996 when the web spread, but by the late 1990s traffic was roughly doubling once a year, not once a quarter.

Do the arithmetic on the two rates, because the arithmetic is the whole episode. Doubling every hundred days compounds to roughly twelve and a half times a year, and over three years to something close to two thousand times. Doubling annually compounds to eight times over three years. That is not a difference of degree. It is the difference between a network that must be rebuilt from scratch every eighteen months at any cost and a network that needs a healthy but ordinary programme of capital investment. Both statements were made about the same industry, at the same time, using the same word — "doubling" — and the capital allocated on the strength of the first was allocated to an industry that only ever needed the second.

That is the first recognisable pattern, and it is worth naming plainly: a growth rate quoted without its period, sourced to a participant with an interest in it, and repeated until it stops being checked.

### What people bought

They bought the companies that sold the equipment the traffic would run on, at prices that assumed the equipment would keep being bought.

Scott McNealy, then chief executive of Sun Microsystems, gave the best short lecture in financial history on what those prices required, in an interview published by BusinessWeek in 2002 after his own company's shares had fallen by roughly ninety per cent. At ten times revenues, he pointed out, a ten-year payback requires the company to pay out one hundred per cent of revenue as dividends for ten straight years — which assumes zero cost of goods sold, which is hard for a computer company; zero expenses, which is hard with thirty-nine thousand employees; no tax; no tax on the dividend either; and no research and development for a decade while somehow maintaining the same revenue. "Now, having done that," he asked, "would any of you like to buy my stock at sixty-four dollars?"

Note what that passage is. It is not a market view. It is a statement of what a multiple implies about future cash, worked through in public by the person best placed to know the cost structure. It remains the cleanest test in the industry, and it is available to anyone with the ability to divide.

### What the accounts said

Cisco's own reckoning arrived in the quarter ended April 2001. The company recorded an excess inventory charge of about two point two five billion dollars, restructuring costs and other special charges of about one point one seven billion, and a net loss for the quarter of two point six nine billion dollars, alongside a reduction of around eight thousand five hundred jobs, per the company's announcement of those results and contemporary coverage including The Washington Post in April 2001. The chief executive described the conditions as a hundred-year flood.

The inventory charge is the part to sit with. Inventory is not a fact about the past. It is a forecast, written down in the assets column. A company builds or buys components because it expects to sell the finished product; when it stops expecting that, the accounting standard requires the carrying value to come down to what the goods can actually fetch. Much of what Cisco had built was customised and could not be resold at any price, which is why the write-down was so large relative to the revenue it was meant to support.

So the sequence in the accounts ran: order book strong, inventory building, inventory building faster than revenue, then the charge. The share price followed the charge. The inventory came first, and it was disclosed quarterly, in public, to anyone reading the balance sheet rather than the headline.

### What was right

Internet traffic grew for the next twenty-five years without pause. The equipment industry that survived is large and profitable. The infrastructure thesis was correct; the timing, the growth rate and the price were not. Investors who were right about the technology and wrong about the arithmetic lost their money anyway, which is the least comfortable lesson in this chapter.

## 2000: telecom equipment and the fibre build

The second episode is really the first one seen from the customer's side, and it is the more instructive of the two, because it is where the financing became circular.

### The claim

The claim was that bandwidth demand was effectively unbounded, so the constraint on a carrier's future was the physical network it could build now. Capital markets agreed and funded accordingly. New carriers were financed on the strength of route maps rather than customers, and the equipment vendors — whose revenue depended on those carriers buying — helped to fund the purchases.

### The mechanism worth understanding

Vendor financing is the part of this episode that most repays attention, because it is a structure, not a scandal, and structures repeat.

A vendor sells equipment to a customer and simultaneously lends the customer the money to buy it. Revenue is recognised on the sale. A receivable or a loan sits on the balance sheet. If the customer's business works, the loan is repaid and everyone is right. If it does not, the vendor has booked profit on a sale that was, in substance, financed by itself, and it discovers the fact twice: once when the receivable is provided against, and once when the demand it represented turns out never to have existed.

Lucent Technologies is the documented case. Its disclosure for the period ended 31 December 1999 recorded commitments or agreements to extend credit to certain customers, including wireless operators, of approximately eight point four billion dollars in aggregate. When the carriers began to fail, the provisions followed: additional provisions of roughly two hundred and fifty million dollars in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2000 as conditions among competitive local exchange carriers deteriorated, then bad-debt provisions reported at about two point two billion dollars in 2001 and one point three billion in 2002, per Lucent's own disclosures as summarised in later reviews of the period. A single customer, Winstar, was reported to owe more than eight hundred million dollars when it failed in April 2001 after Lucent declined a final extension of credit.

Lucent's market value at its late-1999 peak has been put at roughly two hundred and fifty-eight billion dollars in compiled historical series, and its fiscal 2001 loss at around sixteen billion dollars. Both figures come from market-history compilations and contemporary press retrospectives rather than from a filing this book has inspected, and they should be read as orders of magnitude rather than audited quantities. The direction is not in dispute.

### The carriers

Global Crossing, founded in 1997, had built a fibre network reaching some two hundred cities across twenty-seven countries. On 28 January 2002 it filed what was then the fourth-largest Chapter 11 bankruptcy in United States history, listing assets of twenty-two point four billion dollars against debts of twelve point four billion, per contemporary reporting including The Washington Post and Deseret News in January 2002. Among the practices scrutinised afterwards was the swapping of network capacity between carriers — two operators exchanging rights of similar value, each recognising revenue on the sale while capitalising the purchase. Congressional hearings and regulatory scrutiny followed across the sector. This book takes no position beyond the public record. The mechanism is the point: a transaction in which no cash moves and both parties book revenue is a transaction a reader should be able to find and discount.

Nortel Networks is the scale of the thing in one company. At its 2000 peak it accounted for something on the order of a third of the total value of Canada's benchmark stock index — retrospectives put the figure between roughly a third and thirty-eight per cent, which is itself a comment on how such statistics are compiled — and its shares peaked around one hundred and twenty-four Canadian dollars in July 2000. It filed for bankruptcy protection in the United States, Canada and Europe on 14 January 2009, per CBC's compilation of key dates in the company's history. Nine years elapsed between the peak and the filing. Booms do not always end with a bang; sometimes they end with a decade of restructuring.

### What the accounts said

Three tells, all disclosed, all ahead of the collapse.

First, the customer-financing commitments, disclosed in the notes rather than on the face of the accounts. Eight point four billion dollars of credit extended to buyers of your own product is a sentence about future revenue quality, and it was printed.

Second, the composition of receivables. When days sales outstanding rise while revenue growth is celebrated, the company is selling on terms rather than selling more.

Third, the industry-level number. The Semiconductor Industry Association reported that global semiconductor sales closed 2001 at one hundred and thirty-nine billion dollars, down from two hundred and four billion in 2000 — a fall of about thirty-two per cent, the sharpest annual decline in the industry's recorded history, as reported by EE Times. The chip industry did not cause the telecom bust. It simply sat two floors below it, and took the whole force of it.

### What was right, and the timing problem

The fibre was needed. Every video call and every model training run in the present decade travels over infrastructure that traces back to that build. But estimates compiled in later accounts of the period suggest only a small fraction of the fibre installed in the boom — on the order of a tenth by around 2004 — was actually carrying traffic; the rest sat dark for years. No authoritative census of that exists, and estimates vary widely, which is itself worth knowing before quoting one.

The asset had a useful life measured in decades. The debt that financed it had a maturity measured in single-digit years. That mismatch, not the technology, is what bankrupted the builders — and the buyers of the assets in the wreckage, who paid cents on the dollar, made the returns that the original thesis had promised. A correct thesis financed with the wrong duration transfers wealth from the people who were early to the people who were patient.

## 2018: crypto GPU demand

The third episode is smaller, more recent, and directly about the company at the centre of the present cycle. Chapter fourteen covers it in detail; what follows is the part that belongs to this argument.

### The claim

From roughly 2017 the same parallel arithmetic that made a graphics processor good at rendering made it good at the proof-of-work computation used to mine certain cryptocurrencies. Demand appeared, it was large, and it was widely read as a permanent new market for a product previously sold to gamers. Cards were scarce, prices rose above list, and the language used was that of a structural expansion in the addressable market.

### What happened

On 15 November 2018 Nvidia reported third-quarter fiscal 2019 revenue of three point one eight billion dollars against analyst expectations of about three point two four billion, and guided the following quarter to two point seven billion dollars, plus or minus two per cent, against a consensus of about three point four billion. The company attributed the shortfall to excess channel inventory following the post-cryptocurrency boom. The shares fell eighteen point eight per cent the next day, on 16 November 2018, per CNBC's coverage.

On 28 January 2019 the company issued an update cutting its fourth-quarter guidance again, to approximately two point two billion dollars. The quarter, reported on 14 February 2019, came in at two point two one billion dollars, down twenty-four per cent year on year and thirty-one per cent sequentially, with gaming revenue of nine hundred and fifty-four million dollars, down forty-five per cent year on year and forty-six per cent sequentially, per the company's results announcement. Full-year fiscal 2019 revenue was eleven point seven two billion dollars, still up twenty-one per cent on the prior year.

Read those two facts together, because their combination is the trap. The year was up twenty-one per cent. The quarter was down twenty-four per cent. An annual figure can be a record and a wreck at the same time, and the reader who quotes only the annual figure will miss a demand collapse that had already happened.

### The accounting problem underneath it

The reason this episode is instructive is not the size of the fall. It is that the demand which disappeared had never been separately visible.

Miners largely bought the same retail gaming cards through the same channel as gamers, so their money arrived in the accounts labelled as Gaming revenue. On 6 May 2022 Nvidia agreed to pay a five point five million dollar civil penalty and to cease and desist from violating certain federal securities provisions, without admitting or denying the findings, to settle a regulator's charge that in two consecutive quarters of fiscal 2018 it had failed to disclose that cryptomining was a significant element of the material revenue growth in its gaming business, as reported by CNN Business and Al Jazeera on 6 May 2022. The penalty was trivial for a company of that size. The underlying fact was not: a material driver of a reported revenue line had not been visible in that line, and outsiders therefore could not tell which kind of demand was leaving.

There was a second-order effect too, specific to hardware that retains value. When mining stopped being profitable, the cards did not vanish; they were resold. Second-hand supply competed with new product for months, which is why the correction outlasted the event that caused it. Whether that applies to today's data-centre accelerators is a genuine question rather than a rhetorical one, and it belongs to the depreciation argument in chapter thirty-two: an asset with a deep second-hand market has an observable residual value, and an asset without one has an assumed residual value.

### What was right

General-purpose computation on graphics processors was real, permanent and, as it turned out, the foundation of everything that followed. The specific demand that was mistaken for it was not. The lesson is uncomfortably precise: being right that a new class of buyer exists tells you nothing about that buyer's persistence, and if the accounts do not separate the buyer, you cannot measure the persistence even after the fact.

## 2022: the post-pandemic correction

The fourth episode is different in kind and it is the most recent, which makes it the most easily dismissed.

### The claim

The certainty in 2021 was not about a new use for computers. It was about a new level of demand for them. Households and employers had bought laptops, monitors, webcams and network equipment in unusual volumes during the lockdowns, and the industry set its capacity plans on the assumption that the new run rate was a floor. Shortages during 2021 reinforced the belief and produced the classic amplifier: when lead times stretch, buyers order more than they need, and order from several suppliers, so the order book grows faster than the demand it represents.

### What happened

The correction arrived across the industry within about four quarters.

Gartner reported worldwide personal computer shipments of two hundred and eighty-six point two million units for 2022, down sixteen point two per cent on the year, with fourth-quarter shipments of sixty-five point three million units, down twenty-eight point five per cent — the largest quarterly decline since Gartner began tracking the market in the mid-1990s. IDC's count of the same quarter was sixty-seven point two million units, down twenty-eight point one per cent. Two respected counts of the same quarter differ by nearly two million units, which is a reminder to check whose definition of a shipment is in use before comparing anything.

Nvidia's fiscal 2023, ended 29 January 2023, produced revenue of twenty-six point nine seven billion dollars, flat on the prior year, with gaming revenue down twenty-seven per cent to nine point zero seven billion, per the company's results announcement of February 2023; the fiscal second quarter of that year carried inventory-related charges of approximately one point three two billion dollars.

Memory took it hardest, as it always does — chapter twenty explains why the economics of that industry guarantee it. On 21 December 2022 Micron announced a ten per cent reduction in its workforce and the suspension of bonuses, with fiscal 2023 capital expenditure guided to about seven to seven point five billion dollars against roughly twelve billion in the prior fiscal year, and management describing memory prices as having deteriorated significantly and the supply-demand imbalance as the worst in thirteen years, per CNBC and the company's release of that date. It had already announced in September 2022 that it would cut spending on new chipmaking equipment by around half. In the first quarter of 2023 Samsung Electronics reported operating profit of six hundred and forty billion Korean won against fourteen point one two trillion won a year earlier, its lowest since the first quarter of 2009, with a four point five eight trillion won loss in its semiconductor division, per CNBC's report of 27 April 2023.

### And then

This is why the 2022 episode matters more than its size suggests.

By 2026 the same memory industry was the tightest link in the chain. The high-bandwidth memory market for 2026 was estimated at about fifty-four point six billion US dollars, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, with SK Hynix holding the largest share and capacity reported sold out through 2026 across major suppliers, per 2026 industry analysis. SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, reported by CNBC in August 2026. Year to date in 2026, Samsung was reported up about one hundred and fourteen per cent, SK Hynix about one hundred and eighty-six per cent and Micron about one hundred and forty-one per cent, per IG and related industry reporting in mid-2026.

Roughly three and a half years separate "worst supply-demand imbalance in thirteen years" from "sold out". That is the actual tempo of this industry. It is short enough that the same executives, the same analysts and the same capital allocators lived through both ends of it, and it is short enough that the capacity ordered in response to the shortage may well arrive into the next correction, because a memory fab takes longer to build than the cycle takes to turn.

## What the four have in common

| Episode | The argument at the time | What it got right | What it got wrong | The line item that showed it first |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1999 internet infrastructure | Traffic doubling every hundred days; the network must be rebuilt continuously | Traffic grew for decades | The rate, sourced to interested parties and quoted without its period | Inventory rising faster than revenue, then a write-down |
| 2000 telecom equipment | Bandwidth demand is unbounded; build now or lose the route | The fibre was eventually needed | The financing duration and the creditworthiness of the buyers | Customer-financing commitments in the notes; then bad-debt provisions |
| 2018 crypto GPU demand | A permanent new class of buyer for parallel compute | Parallel compute was permanent | That particular buyer was not, and was not separately disclosed | Channel inventory, then a guidance cut, then a second guidance cut |
| 2022 post-pandemic | The higher level of device demand is the new floor | Demand was genuinely pulled forward | A pull-forward is not a new baseline; orders were duplicated | Shipments diverging from end demand; then inventory charges and capex cuts |

Four arguments recur across all of them, and each is being made now. Stating them is not a refutation. It is a request that the reader notice the familiarity and then check the evidence rather than the confidence.

The first is that this is infrastructure rather than a product, so ordinary cyclicality does not apply. Fibre was infrastructure. So were switches and routers. Being infrastructure changes the useful life of the asset; it does not change whether anyone can pay for it while it is being built.

The second is that the constraint is supply, not demand. This is always true at the top of a cycle, because it is what the top of a cycle means. It stops being true when the capacity ordered at the peak arrives, which by definition happens after the peak. The lag between deciding capacity and having it — years, at the leading edge — is the whole engine described in the previous chapter.

The third is that the buyers are creditworthy this time. In 2000 they were not: the equipment vendors were lending them the purchase price. This is the argument with the most force in the current cycle, because the largest buyers of accelerators today are among the most cash-generative businesses in the world, funding capital expenditure substantially from operating cash flow. It is a real difference. Its limit is that the cycle does not require anyone to go bankrupt — it only requires spending to pause, and a healthy company can cut capital expenditure faster than a distressed one, because it has no lenders forcing it to keep building.

The fourth is that demand is contracted years ahead, so the risk is already sold. A backlog is an order book, not revenue. Orders can be cancelled, deferred and renegotiated, and the disclosure of what deposit sits behind an order is usually thin. The telecommunications order books of 2001 looked excellent shortly before they did not.

## What is genuinely different now

Symmetry requires this section, and the two chapters after this one make both cases properly, at length and at their strongest.

Three differences are real. The buyers are cash-generative incumbents rather than start-up carriers financed by their own suppliers. The revenue at the top of the stack, while much smaller than the capital expenditure below it, is being paid by customers who are re-purchasing rather than by investors. And the capability of the underlying technology has continued to improve during the build, which was not true of fibre — a strand of glass laid in 1999 did what it did on the day it was laid.

Two things temper each of those. Cash-generative buyers can stop buying without any distress at all. Re-purchasing customers are only a floor at the price they currently pay, and enterprise software prices are not fixed by nature. And improving capability increases demand for compute only if the improvement is worth paying for, which is a judgement made one procurement committee at a time.

There is also a structure in the present cycle that was not present in 1999 or 2018, and is closer to 2000: significant equity investment by suppliers in their own customers, and by cloud providers in the model companies that rent their clouds. Chapter fifty-six sets out what is disclosed about it, and alleges nothing improper, because nothing improper is alleged in the public record. The point here is narrower. Vendor financing in 2000 was not a scandal either, at the time. It was ordinary commercial practice, disclosed in the notes, and it made the eventual downturn worse because it had inflated the apparent demand on the way up.

## The tells worth watching

If the four episodes have a practical residue, it is a short list of things a reader can check without access to anyone's forecast.

Inventory and purchase commitments, quarter by quarter, against revenue growth. Inventory is a forecast in the assets column, and purchase commitments are a forecast that has not reached the balance sheet yet.

Any provision, reserve or credit facility connected to a customer. If a company is helping to fund its own buyers, that is disclosed, usually in a note that nobody reads, and it tells you something about revenue quality that the revenue line does not.

The gap between what suppliers ship and what end customers buy. Sell-in and sell-through are different measurements, and the difference accumulates in a channel until it is corrected all at once.

Whether the disclosure separates the kinds of demand. If two very different buyers arrive through the same revenue line, the accounts cannot tell you which one is leaving, and neither can anyone else.

The order book's cancellation terms and the deposits behind it. A backlog with no deposit and free cancellation is a statement of intent.

And the residual value assumption behind the depreciation charge, which is the subject of chapter thirty-two and the single estimate that decides more reported profit in this industry than any other.

Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot from the date given, drawn from the source named, and the recent ones will have moved by the time you read it; the historical ones are settled but were compiled by others, and where this chapter has had to rely on retrospective compilations rather than filings it has said so. None of it is investment advice, and none of it is a forecast.

The conclusion is deliberately modest. Booms do not always end badly, buyers are not always wrong, and an industry that has been certain four times and mistaken four times may perfectly well be right the fifth. What the record establishes is smaller and firmer than that: the arguments now being made for the permanence of this demand have all been made before, by serious people, about real technologies, and in each case the accounts showed the strain before the market did. A reader who can find the strain in the accounts does not need to know how the story ends.
