# Broadcom: The Quiet Trillion

In September 2025, a chief executive told analysts that an unnamed customer had placed orders worth more than ten billion US dollars for custom artificial-intelligence chips, and the company's shares rose about nine per cent on the news, as reported by CNBC on 5 September 2025. For three months the market traded on guesses about who the customer was. In December 2025 the same chief executive ended the game: the ten-billion-dollar order was from Anthropic, and it was for racks of Google-designed tensor processing units — with a further eleven-billion-dollar order from the same customer landing in the following quarter, per CNBC's report of 11 December 2025.

Sit with how strange that sentence is. A company most consumers have never knowingly bought anything from took a twenty-one-billion-dollar pair of orders, for chips designed by one of its customers, sold to a company that competes with another of its customers, and the identity of the buyer was material enough to move one of the largest market capitalisations in the world. The company is Broadcom. It stood at roughly 1.61 trillion US dollars in early 2026, per the statista and alpha-sense rankings of the world's most valuable companies — behind only a handful of names, every one of which is a household word. Broadcom is not a household word. There is no Broadcom phone, no Broadcom app, no Broadcom shop. Its customers are perhaps a few hundred organisations, and its most important ones number fewer than ten.

This chapter is about how a company gets to a quiet trillion, and it is a chapter an accountant enjoys writing, because Broadcom is not really a semiconductor story or a software story. It is a capital-allocation story that happens to be denominated in silicon.

## Assembled, not grown

Most companies at the top of this industry can point to a founding product. Nvidia has the GPU. TSMC has the foundry model. Broadcom's founding product is the acquisition itself.

The corporate ancestry runs through three names. The business began life inside Hewlett-Packard, as a components division dating to the early 1960s. In 1999 it was spun out with the rest of HP's measurement businesses as Agilent Technologies. In 2005, Agilent sold its semiconductor products group to the private-equity firms KKR and Silver Lake for 2.66 billion US dollars, a carve-out completed on 1 December 2005 that created a standalone company called Avago Technologies, per Agilent's announcement at the time. Within months, Avago had a new chief executive: Hock Tan, who has run the company ever since.

What followed was two decades of serial acquisition, each deal larger than the last, each digested on the same playbook. The record is worth laying out in one place, because the pattern is the argument.

Two things stand out from the table. The first is the 2016 deal, in which Avago, the smaller and younger firm by lineage, bought Broadcom Corporation for thirty-seven billion dollars and took its target's name. The acquirer wearing the acquired company's name is a small telling detail: the name was an asset, and assets are for using.

The second is the deal that is not in the table. In late 2017 Broadcom launched a hostile bid for Qualcomm worth about 117 billion US dollars, which would have been the largest technology acquisition ever attempted. On 12 March 2018 the President of the United States blocked it by executive order, on the advice of the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, citing national-security concerns centred on 5G leadership, as reported by CNBC at the time. Broadcom was then domiciled in Singapore; it completed a move of its legal home to the United States shortly afterwards. The blocked bid marked the boundary of the acquisition strategy — and the pivot that followed is instructive. Denied the biggest chip deal in history, Broadcom bought software instead: CA within months, Symantec's enterprise business the next year, VMware after that. The playbook did not change. The shopping list did.

## What the playbook does to the accounts

Broadcom's acquisition method is consistent enough that industry coverage has described it the same way for a decade: buy an established franchise with entrenched customers, cut its costs hard, narrow its focus to the products those entrenched customers cannot easily leave, raise prices where the franchise allows it, and use the resulting cash flow to fund the next purchase. Whether one admires this or winces at it, the accounting consequences are worth understanding, because they shape every number the company reports.

A serial acquirer's balance sheet fills up with things you cannot drop on your foot. When one company buys another for more than the fair value of its identifiable net assets — and strategic acquirers almost always pay more — the excess is recorded as goodwill, and a large slice of the price is allocated to acquired intangible assets: customer relationships, developed technology, trade names. Those intangibles are then amortised, charged against profit over a period of years, exactly as a machine is depreciated. The result is that a roll-up's GAAP profit is persistently depressed by amortisation charges that relate to prices paid years ago, and management will usually invite you to look instead at a non-GAAP profit figure with those charges added back.

Neither number is a lie. The GAAP figure faithfully spreads the cost of past deals; the non-GAAP figure approximates the cash economics of the businesses as they run today. But the gap between them is where judgement lives, and the reader of a roll-up's accounts should always ask two questions. First, what would the amortisation have been called if the company had built the asset instead of buying it? (Answer: mostly ordinary operating expense, which is why adding it all back flatters the comparison with organically grown rivals.) Second, is goodwill ever impaired? A roll-up that never writes anything off is asserting that every deal it ever did is still worth what it paid. Sometimes that is even true.

The other place the playbook shows up is the margin line. Cost discipline after acquisition is the strategy's engine, and it is visible in the spread between what the acquired businesses earned before and after. It is also visible in the dividend: Broadcom's board raised the quarterly dividend by ten per cent to sixty-five cents a share for fiscal 2026, per the company's results announcement of December 2025 — the behaviour of a company that regards its cash flow as dependable, which is itself a claim about how firmly its customers are held.

## The software floor

The clearest recent demonstration of the method is VMware, and it deserves a moment because it shows what "franchise" means in practice.

VMware's virtualisation software is the plumbing of a very large share of corporate data centres — the layer that lets one physical server behave as many. Ripping it out is a multi-year project with real operational risk, which is another way of saying its customers are, in the medium term, captive. After completing the sixty-nine-billion-dollar acquisition in November 2023, Broadcom moved VMware from perpetual licences — pay once, use forever — to subscriptions, and consolidated a sprawling product list into a small number of bundles. Customers reported very large price increases. AT&T sued Broadcom in 2024, alleging in its filing a proposed annual price increase of roughly 1,050 per cent, as reported in trade coverage of the suit; CISPE, an association of European cloud infrastructure providers, reported aggregate increases among its members ranging from 800 to 1,500 per cent, per Network World's reporting of the dispute. Broadcom's position, broadly, has been that customers were moving from under-priced legacy licences to subscriptions that reflect the product's value. None of this is alleged to be improper; it is what pricing power looks like when it is exercised rather than merely possessed. The accounting reader simply notes the mechanism: a perpetual licence is revenue once, a subscription is revenue forever, and a captive customer is the difference between a price list and a suggestion.

The results are in the segment note. Broadcom's infrastructure software segment produced twenty-seven billion US dollars of revenue in fiscal 2025, up twenty-six per cent, about forty-two per cent of the company's total revenue of 63.9 billion for the year, per the results announcement of December 2025. That software floor matters to everything else in this chapter, because it throws off steady, high-margin, subscription-shaped cash in an industry where the other revenue lines swing with the capital-spending cycle. The accelerator business gets the headlines; the software pays for the patience.

It is not unconditional. In the quarter ended 3 May 2026, infrastructure software revenue of 7.18 billion dollars grew nine per cent but came in below the 7.32 billion analysts polled by StreetAccount had expected, and the shares fell in the days after the report even though the company's AI numbers were records, as reported by CNBC in June 2026. A franchise being milked and a franchise being drained look identical for several quarters. The line to watch is the renewal, not the press release.

## Selling the alternative

Now to the business that turned a large, well-run acquirer into a trillion-dollar company: custom accelerators.

Recall the shape of the market from earlier chapters. Nvidia sells merchant GPUs — general-purpose accelerators, one design sold to everyone, wrapped in a software ecosystem, at gross margins that made it the world's most valuable company at roughly 5.3 to 5.4 trillion US dollars as at mid-August 2026, per the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings. If you are a hyperscaler spending tens of billions a year on those chips, every line of your capital budget is an invitation to ask: could we design our own?

Designing your own is called an ASIC — an application-specific integrated circuit, a chip built for one workload rather than every workload. Broadcom's term is XPU. The proposition to the hyperscaler is straightforward: you know your own workload better than any merchant vendor; a chip stripped of everything your workload does not need can do more useful work per watt and per dollar; and the designer's gross margin you are currently paying can be substantially reclaimed. What the hyperscaler cannot easily do is the unglamorous heavy engineering at the bottom of chip design — the high-speed input-output circuitry, the packaging that marries logic dies to stacks of high-bandwidth memory, the discipline of getting a two-nanometre-class design through a foundry on schedule. That is what Broadcom sells. The customer brings the architecture; Broadcom brings the parts of the job that take twenty years to learn.

The longest-standing example is Google. Broadcom has been Google's design partner on the tensor processing unit family across seven generations, a relationship dating back to around 2014, per Tom's Hardware's May 2026 survey of the custom-accelerator market; The Register reported on the partnership's scale as early as September 2023. Broadcom itself rarely names its accelerator customers — they appear in its disclosures as anonymised large customers — but trade reporting has consistently identified Google, Meta and ByteDance among them, per the same Tom's Hardware survey. Hence the theatre of September 2025's mystery customer, and the December 2025 reveal quoted at the top of this chapter: the customer was Anthropic, and the product was racks of Google's Ironwood-generation TPUs, per CNBC in December 2025. Note what that transaction implies. The chip is Google's design; Broadcom co-developed it and builds the racks; the buyer is a model company that Google has separately invested in. Layer boundaries, again, are activities rather than companies.

Then there is OpenAI. On 13 October 2025, OpenAI and Broadcom jointly announced a collaboration to deploy ten gigawatts of OpenAI-designed accelerators — OpenAI doing the design, Broadcom developing and deploying the systems, with the racks networked entirely over Ethernet using Broadcom's parts, deployment beginning in the second half of 2026 and completing by the end of 2029, per the joint announcement. Broadcom's shares rose close to ten per cent that day, per CNBC. Ten gigawatts is a measure of electrical capacity, not of money, and the announcement did not carry a purchase price; treat the figure as a statement of ambition with a delivery schedule attached.

The revenue consequences of all this are the fastest-moving numbers in the company's accounts, so date-stamp everything. Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue was twenty billion US dollars in fiscal 2025 (the year ended in November 2025), up sixty-five per cent, per the company's December 2025 earnings call as transcribed by the Motley Fool. In the second quarter of fiscal 2026, ended 3 May 2026, AI semiconductor revenue was 10.8 billion dollars, up 143 per cent year on year — a single quarter running at more than half the whole prior year — with AI bookings above thirty billion dollars, per the company's results announcement of June 2026. The company guided third-quarter AI revenue to about sixteen billion dollars, held its full-year fiscal 2026 AI forecast at fifty-six billion — roughly 180 per cent above fiscal 2025 — and reiterated that fiscal 2027 AI revenue should exceed one hundred billion dollars, per the same announcement and CNBC's coverage of it. Every one of those figures will be stale by the time you read this; the company re-guides quarterly, and the current numbers are in its latest results announcement.

The trajectory into those quarters is worth recording too, because it shows how fast the slope changed. In the fourth quarter of fiscal 2025, reported in December 2025, total revenue was 18.0 billion dollars, up twenty-eight per cent, with GAAP net income of 8.52 billion for the quarter, and the company told investors that first-quarter fiscal 2026 AI revenue would double year on year to 8.2 billion dollars, per the results announcement of December 2025 and CNBC's coverage of it. Two quarters later the AI line was 10.8 billion and the next quarter's guide was sixteen. Growth like that in a business this capital-adjacent is not a smooth curve; it is a queue of specific, named-but-unnamed programmes reaching volume production on their own schedules.

### Bookings are not revenue

One accounting habit will serve you well whenever this business is discussed: keep bookings, backlog and revenue in separate mental columns. A booking is an order taken; a backlog is the pile of orders not yet delivered; revenue is recognised only when the goods are delivered or the service performed. The thirty-billion-dollar AI bookings figure from June 2026, the ten-billion-dollar switch backlog cited in December 2025, the twenty-one billion of Anthropic orders, the ten-gigawatt OpenAI programme — none of these is revenue, and they overlap each other in ways the disclosures do not fully untangle. Orders in this industry can be rescheduled, resized and, with penalties, cancelled; a gigawatt commitment is a plan, not a receivable. The reason companies announce orders rather than waiting to announce revenue is that orders are the more impressive number sooner. That is not dishonest — it is disclosure — but the reader should always ask when an announced figure will become audited revenue, over how many years it spreads, and what has to keep going right in the meantime for all of it to arrive.

A worked example makes the economics concrete. Take an illustrative accelerator programme, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. A merchant accelerator sells for 30,000 dollars a unit and carries a seventy-per-cent gross margin, so about 21,000 dollars of each unit is the vendor's margin. A hyperscaler needing 500,000 units a year is therefore contributing about ten and a half billion dollars a year to its vendor's gross profit. Suppose a custom chip of equivalent usefulness for the specific workload can be had for 15,000 dollars a unit all-in — design partner's fees and margin, foundry wafers, memory, packaging — after a two-billion-dollar, three-year development programme. The arithmetic is not subtle. What the arithmetic hides is the risk transfer: the hyperscaler now owns the schedule risk, the utilisation risk if the workload shifts, and the software burden the merchant vendor used to carry. Custom silicon is cheaper the way owning a house is cheaper than renting. It is, provided nothing goes wrong, and provided you stay.

## The network is half the computer

The accelerators get the attention. Broadcom's other AI business may be the more defensible one.

A modern training cluster is tens of thousands, and increasingly hundreds of thousands, of accelerators that must behave as one machine. The chips spend a meaningful fraction of their time exchanging results with each other, and while they wait on the network, the most expensive capital equipment on earth sits idle. Once a cluster passes a certain size, the binding constraint stops being how fast each chip computes and becomes how fast the chips can talk. The network stops being cabling and becomes part of the computer.

This is Broadcom's oldest neighbourhood. Its Ethernet switch silicon has been the standard part inside data-centre networking gear for decades — much of the equipment sold under other companies' brands has Broadcom inside it. In June 2025 the company announced it was shipping Tomahawk 6, the first Ethernet switch chip with 102.4 terabits per second of capacity, double any Ethernet switch then on the market, per the company's announcement of 3 June 2025. On the December 2025 earnings call, management put the order backlog for AI switches alone above ten billion dollars, per the Motley Fool transcript. In October 2025 the company followed with a version of the same chip integrating co-packaged optics — the optical transmitters mounted directly beside the switch silicon rather than in pluggable modules at the faceplate — per its announcement of 8 October 2025. The engineering detail carries a financial signal: at these speeds, moving data electrically across even a circuit board wastes enough power that the economics favour converting to light as close to the chip as possible. Power, as the compute-layer chapters showed, is the binding constraint of the whole build-out, and a networking vendor that saves watts is selling capacity in disguise.

The strategic point is bigger than any one part. Nvidia sells its accelerators with its own preferred interconnect and networking; a cluster built the Nvidia way is coherent, and correspondingly committed. Broadcom's counter-position is Ethernet — the open standard the entire industry already runs on — scaled up to AI dimensions. Every custom-accelerator deal reinforces it: a hyperscaler that designs its own chip cannot use the merchant vendor's proprietary fabric, so the XPU sale pulls the switch sale along behind it. The OpenAI announcement made this explicit — racks scaled entirely with Ethernet and Broadcom connectivity, per the October 2025 joint announcement. For every dollar the industry redirects away from merchant GPUs, some of it lands at Broadcom twice: once in the accelerator, once in the network that stitches the accelerators together. The diagram at the head of this chapter is, in that sense, a picture of one machine sold in pieces.

## Customer or competitor?

Now the strategic question the brief of this industry keeps posing, and which Broadcom's business model states in its purest form: is a customer that designs its own chip a customer, or a future competitor?

Ask it first from Nvidia's side, because there Broadcom is the instrument of the threat. Every hyperscaler and every frontier lab that succeeds with a custom accelerator programme is revenue subtracted, at the margin, from the merchant vendor — and Broadcom is the enabling contractor for most of the credible programmes. The eventual scale of that subtraction is genuinely unknown; what is knowable is the direction, and the fact that the merchant vendor's largest customers are funding the alternative.

Now ask it from Broadcom's side, where it is more uncomfortable than the share price suggests. Broadcom's custom-silicon customers are not helpless licensees. They are Google, Meta and their peers — organisations with world-class silicon teams of their own, whose dependence on Broadcom is a choice about where to draw the make-or-buy line, revisited annually. A customer that today buys the full service could tomorrow take more of the design in-house and buy only the input-output blocks; trade reporting through 2025 and 2026 has regularly carried accounts of hyperscalers spreading design work across multiple partners to reduce single-supplier dependence. Broadcom's moat is that the bottom of chip design is brutally hard and its practitioners scarce. That is a real moat. It is also a moat made of employees, and employees are the one asset class that can resign.

The accountant's way into this question is the customer-concentration note, which is where strategic risk is required by regulation to stop being abstract. Broadcom's annual report discloses that its top five end customers accounted for approximately forty per cent of net revenue in both fiscal 2025 and fiscal 2024; the fiscal 2023 filing disclosed that a single customer, Apple, accounted for roughly twenty per cent of net revenue in each of fiscal 2023 and 2022, reflecting the older wireless-components business. Forty per cent in five hands is a serious number. It means the difference between Broadcom's decade and its disappointment is a handful of renewal negotiations conducted by counterparties who employ their own chip designers and read the same margin disclosures you do. The concentration note is updated every year in the annual report; watch whether the percentage rises as the AI business grows, because custom silicon is, by its nature, a concentrating business — each incremental dollar comes from a customer large enough to fund a chip programme.

And note, finally, the vertical entanglement that this book keeps finding at every layer. Broadcom builds Google-designed chips, bought by Anthropic, which Google has invested up to forty-three billion dollars in, per 2026 reporting — while also building OpenAI-designed systems for the company Google competes with most directly, and selling networking to nearly everyone involved. When one firm is the contractor to all sides of a war, its revenue is not a bet on any combatant. It is a bet on the war continuing.

## Reading the quiet trillion

Pull the threads together and Broadcom resolves into something simpler than its acquisition history suggests: it is a collection of toll booths, assembled at a rate of roughly one large purchase every two years, on roads its customers find expensive to leave. Mainframe software, virtualisation, switch silicon, custom accelerators — each is a business where the customer's cost of switching exceeds the vendor's cost of serving, and that spread, compounded across two decades and funded by each franchise's cash in turn, is what a quiet trillion is made of.

For the reader of accounts, the watch-list is short. The split between the semiconductor and software segments, and the growth rate of each, is in every quarterly results announcement. The AI revenue figure and its guidance are restated quarterly and will have moved by the time you read this — fifty-six billion dollars guided for fiscal 2026 and more than one hundred billion indicated for fiscal 2027, per the June 2026 results announcement, are forecasts, not facts, and forecasts in this industry have been revised in both directions. The customer-concentration percentage is in the annual report once a year. The gap between GAAP and non-GAAP profit tells you what the acquisition history still costs. None of this is a recommendation to buy or sell anything — this book does not give those — and a company this concentrated, this acquisitive and this exposed to a single capital-spending cycle can disappoint from any starting price.

The larger lesson belongs to the book's argument. The application layer is where the money enters; the model layer is where the fame lives. But when the world's model companies and hyperscalers decided to reduce their dependence on the most valuable company on earth, the alternative they reached for was not another famous name. It was a quiet, disciplined, serially acquisitive engineering firm two layers down the stack, which had spent twenty years buying the positions everyone else found boring. Value moving down the stack does not always announce itself. Sometimes it compounds in silence, one unfashionable franchise at a time, until the ranking tables force everyone to learn the name.
