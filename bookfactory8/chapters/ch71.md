# Where the Bottleneck Goes Next

In late 2023, the research firm Omdia reported that lead times for servers built around Nvidia's H100 accelerator ran from thirty-six to fifty-two weeks, and that Meta and Microsoft had each taken delivery of as many as one hundred and fifty thousand of the chips that year, against roughly fifty thousand each for Google, Amazon, Oracle and Tencent, per Tom's Hardware's reporting of the Omdia analysis in November 2023. A year of waiting for a component, and the waiting list itself became news.

Tom's Hardware later reported those same lead times easing from up to four months to eight to twelve weeks. Nothing about the boom had cooled. Demand for AI compute in the intervening period rose rather than fell. What happened was narrower and more useful to understand: the queue moved. It moved to memory, then to the packaging step that joins memory to processors, and then out of the semiconductor industry altogether, to substations and switchgear and the people qualified to wire them.

That movement is the subject of this chapter, and it is the single most portable idea in this book. Every figure printed here will go stale. The mechanism will not.

## One rule, stated once

In any chain of production, output is set by the tightest stage. Add capacity anywhere else and you do not get more output; you get more inventory sitting in front of the stage that is still tight. This is not a theory about artificial intelligence. It is the oldest observation in industrial engineering, and a factory manager in 1950 would have found it unremarkable.

The economic corollary is what matters here, and it is worth stating precisely, because the loose version of it is wrong.

At the binding stage, and only at the binding stage, price can rise without volume falling. Everywhere else in the chain, a supplier who raises price loses the order, because the buyer has alternatives and, more importantly, because buying more of a non-binding input produces nothing the buyer can sell. So the rent — the excess of price over what it costs to produce, sustained beyond the point where competition would normally erode it — accumulates at the constraint. Not at the largest company in the chain, not at the most technically impressive, and not at the one closest to the customer. At the tight one.

Take an illustrative production chain, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Five stages, each producing part of a finished AI system: logic wafers, one hundred units a month; memory stacks, seventy; advanced packaging, fifty-five; rack assembly, ninety; connected and powered floor space, forty. The chain produces forty systems a month. Not fifty-five, not seventy, and certainly not one hundred.

Now spend a billion dollars doubling the logic wafer stage to two hundred. Output: still forty. The billion has purchased inventory, depreciation and a write-down waiting to happen. Spend the same billion adding ten units of powered floor space, and output rises to fifty — up a quarter, from the stage nobody's marketing department wanted to talk about. And notice what happens to prices in that chain if the buyers are rational: they will bid aggressively for floor space and packaging slots, and they will negotiate hard on wafers, because a wafer they cannot power is not an asset.

Everything in the rest of this chapter is that example with real names attached.

## Where it has already moved

The constraint in this cycle has moved at least four times in four years, and each move is legible in the accounts of the companies on either side of it.

**Accelerators, roughly 2023 to 2024.** The tight stage was the chip itself: design, leading-edge wafers and allocation. The evidence was the lead time above, the emergence of allocation as a topic in earnings calls, and the margin. Nvidia's fiscal year 2026, ended 25 January 2026, produced revenue of two hundred and fifteen point nine billion US dollars with GAAP gross margin of seventy-one point one per cent for the year and seventy-five point zero per cent in the January quarter, per the company's February 2026 results and its Form 10-K. Those margins are the printed record of a period in which the buyer's alternative to paying was not buying.

**Memory, roughly 2024 to 2026.** As chapter twenty-one set out, high-bandwidth memory became the ceiling on what an accelerator could do, and it could not be made faster simply by wanting it. HBM consumes roughly three times the wafer capacity of standard DDR5 per gigabyte, per Tom's Hardware reporting in 2026, so every unit of relief at the memory stage cost three units of ordinary supply elsewhere. Bank of America estimated the 2026 HBM market at about fifty-four point six billion US dollars, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, per 2026 industry reporting; by January 2026 trade coverage reported all three major suppliers pre-sold for the calendar year. The margin followed on schedule. SK Hynix reported second-quarter 2026 revenue of about seventy-nine point three trillion won and operating profit of about sixty point five trillion won — an operating margin near seventy-six per cent — per its July 2026 results as reported by Investing.com and PR Newswire.

**Advanced packaging, overlapping and continuing.** A memory stack and a processor are useless until they are married on an interposer, and the dominant process for doing so has been TSMC's CoWoS. Epoch AI's 2025 analysis of the accelerator supply chain concluded that advanced packaging and HBM, rather than logic wafers, were the binding constraints on AI chip production in that period. The relief has been enormous and is still not complete: trade reporting in 2026 put TSMC's CoWoS capacity on a path from roughly thirty-five thousand wafers a month in late 2024 towards something in the region of one hundred and thirty thousand by the end of 2026, and TrendForce reported in June 2026 that the CoWoS supply-demand gap was expected to narrow from about twenty per cent to about ten per cent by the end of that year. Narrowing, not closed. A ten per cent gap is still a queue.

**Power, from roughly 2025.** Chapter thirty-one is the long version. The short version is three numbers. The capacity auction for the largest American electricity market cleared at three hundred and twenty-nine point one seven US dollars per megawatt-day in July 2025 for the 2026/27 delivery year, against twenty-eight point nine two dollars two auctions earlier, and hit a new cap of three hundred and thirty-three point four four dollars in December 2025, per Utility Dive and Canary Media. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's 2025 Queued Up report counted roughly 2,600 gigawatts of generation and storage waiting for transmission interconnection at the end of 2024, with median time from request to commercial operation now over four years. And GE Vernova's gas equipment backlog reached one hundred and sixteen gigawatts by the second quarter of 2026 against annualised production of roughly twenty gigawatts, per company statements reported by Turbomachinery Magazine, Utility Dive and Power Engineering.

Set the four moves side by side.

| Period | Binding stage | How you could tell early | Where the rent showed up |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023–24 | Accelerators and leading-edge wafers | Server lead times of 36–52 weeks (Omdia, November 2023); allocation discussed on earnings calls | Nvidia gross margin 71.1% FY26, 75.0% in the January 2026 quarter |
| 2024–26 | High-bandwidth memory | "Sold out" language from suppliers; three-to-one wafer consumption versus DDR5 | SK Hynix operating margin ~76%, Q2 2026 |
| 2024–26 | Advanced packaging (CoWoS and relatives) | Packaging named as the constraint by supply-chain analysts (Epoch AI, 2025) | Foundry packaging revenue and priority allocation; supply-demand gap ~20%, narrowing to ~10% by end-2026 (TrendForce, June 2026) |
| 2025– | Connected, powered floor space | Capacity prices at the regulatory cap; interconnection queue median over four years | Capacity payments, gas turbine backlogs, twenty-year nuclear contracts |

Read the right-hand column downwards and the book's spine appears in a different form. Value has moved down the stack because the constraint has moved down the stack. The two statements are the same statement.

## Why it moves

Constraints are not permanent features of an industry. They move for four reasons, and the four are worth separating because they have different timescales and different warning signs.

**The rent calls in the capital that destroys it.** A stage earning seventy-five per cent gross margins is an advertisement. Capital arrives — from the incumbent, from its competitors, and from its customers, who would rather fund capacity than bid against each other for it. That capital takes years to become output, which is why the rent persists long enough to be capitalised into share prices, and it arrives all at once, which is why the relief usually overshoots. This is the memory cycle of chapter twenty, restated: shortage, record prices, record capital expenditure, simultaneous arrival, normalisation. SK Hynix announcing a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, as reported by CNBC in August 2026, is that mechanism in progress, whatever it eventually proves about timing.

**The workload changes what binds.** This is the least appreciated of the four. The industry's centre of gravity moved from training models to serving them, and the two workloads stress different things. Training is arithmetic-heavy and comparatively kind to memory; inference, as chapter twenty-one showed, must read a model's entire weights for every token it produces, and is therefore rationed by memory bandwidth rather than by arithmetic. The constraint did not move because memory got worse. It moved because the customer started doing something else with the machine. Any change in what the industry is actually selling — longer context, more reasoning steps, video rather than text, agents that run for hours rather than seconds — re-ranks the stages behind it.

**The buyer engineers around it.** A constraint is an incentive to redesign. Nvidia's H200 was, instructively, the same processor as the H100 with more and faster memory attached, per the company's published specifications: when memory bound the product, the product was rebuilt around memory. Liquid cooling arrived because air stopped working at the new rack densities. Co-packaged optics are being developed because moving data between racks over conventional pluggable modules costs power the campus does not have. Every one of those responses relieves one stage and loads another. Design-around does not remove a bottleneck. It relocates it, usually to something less glamorous.

**The next-longest lead time inherits.** When a stage is relieved, the constraint does not go somewhere random. It goes to whatever now takes longest to replicate relative to the demand growing against it. That gives the reader a rough ordering, and the ordering is the practical heart of this chapter.

| Stage | Roughly how long to add meaningful capacity | Can money accelerate it? |
|---|---|---|
| Software and model serving efficiency | Weeks to months | Largely yes |
| Rack assembly and integration | Months | Yes |
| Accelerator supply, given wafers and packaging | Quarters | Partly, through allocation |
| Advanced packaging lines | One to two years | Yes, with equipment lead times |
| HBM capacity plus customer qualification | Two years plus qualification | Only partly — tests cannot be bought |
| Leading-edge fabs | Three to four years | Partly |
| Large power transformers and medium-voltage switchgear | Multi-year backlogs, reported at three to four years for the largest units in 2026 | Barely |
| Gas turbines from the major Western makers | Delivery slots reported effectively sold out through 2030 | No |
| Transmission interconnection | Median over four years | No |
| New nuclear generation | Roughly a decade | No |
| Licensed electricians and specialist trades | An apprenticeship, several years, per worker | No |

Read that table from top to bottom and one property stands out. The constraint has been drifting steadily towards the stages where money is least effective — where the limiting factor is a regulatory process, a physical build, or a human being who has to be trained. It has also been drifting out of the AI industry's ownership. Nvidia can decide how many accelerators to order. Nobody in this book decides how many transformers exist, or how quickly an interconnection study is completed, or how many people finished an electrical apprenticeship four years ago.

## Who actually collects the rent

Here the framework needs a qualification, and skipping it is the commonest way to misapply the whole idea.

Being the constraint is not the same as capturing its value. The rent goes to whoever holds the tight stage *and* is free to price it. Three conditions have to hold together: the stage must be tight, the holder must face few enough competitors to price rather than take a price, and no regulator or contract must cap what can be charged.

Check the four moves above against that test and the results differ sharply. Accelerators and HBM passed all three, which is why margin appeared where it did. Advanced packaging passed them, though the pricing has been quieter, because it sits inside a foundry that sells the whole package rather than the scarce step alone.

Power mostly fails the test, and this is the interesting case. Electricity in most developed markets is sold by regulated utilities earning an allowed return on capital, or into markets with price caps — which is precisely why the American capacity auction that opened chapter thirty-one cleared "at the cap" rather than at whatever the market would have paid. The scarcity was real, and the price was not permitted to express it fully. Where scarcity meets a regulated seller, the rent does not become shareholder profit. It becomes a queue, a political argument, and a line on household electricity bills — the market monitor attributed sixty-three per cent of one auction's price increase to data centre demand, per Monitoring Analytics as reported by Utility Dive.

The unregulated parts of the power chain are where the profit collects instead: the turbine makers with sold-out order books, the merchant generators signing twenty-year contracts at negotiated prices, the owners of already-connected sites. Amazon's expanded agreement with Talen Energy for one thousand nine hundred and twenty megawatts from the Susquehanna nuclear plant through 2042, announced in June 2025 per Talen and Utility Dive, is the shape of it: the scarce thing was not electrons, which are everywhere, but a connected, firm, contracted claim on them.

And a constraint held by a fragmented, unincorporated workforce produces no equity value at all. If licensed electricians are the binding stage — and Microsoft's president Brad Smith has been reported describing the shortage of electricians as the single biggest challenge for data centre expansion in the United States — the rent shows up as wages, overtime and poaching between sites, not as a margin line in anyone's accounts. The Information Technology and Innovation Foundation estimated the data centre construction workforce shortfall at roughly four hundred and thirty-nine thousand workers as of November 2025 against more than four hundred facilities under development, and the US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects a need for roughly eighty-one thousand new electricians a year through 2034. There is no security to buy that captures that. There is only a cost that every builder pays and a schedule that every builder misses.

So the framework has two questions, not one. Where is the tight stage? And who, at that stage, is permitted to charge for it?

## Seven tells

The point of all this is to see the next move before it is a headline. The tells below are ordinary, public and mostly free, and every one of them appeared before the corresponding shortage was widely discussed.

**Lead times lengthening in the trade press.** Not the financial press — the trade press, which reports delivery weeks because its readers have to plan around them. Server lead times of thirty-six to fifty-two weeks were reported in 2023 by outlets covering hardware procurement. Transformer and switchgear lead times were being reported by data centre development specialists and by Power Magazine in 2026 at three to four years for the largest units, against roughly a year in 2020 and 2021, with medium-voltage switchgear described as effectively sold out through 2028 in many channels. Those figures come from procurement advisers rather than audited filings, and should be treated as directional. The direction is the signal.

**The phrase "sold out through".** When a supplier says it on an earnings call, it is telling you it has stopped competing on price for that period. SK Hynix said it of DRAM, NAND and HBM for the following year, per TechSpot's reporting of the remarks; TSMC's chief executive said it of CoWoS; GE Vernova has said it of turbine slots through 2030. The phrase is a gift, and it is usually said with some pride.

**Backlog running far ahead of annual output.** One hundred and sixteen gigawatts of orders against roughly twenty gigawatts a year of production is not a strong order book; it is a five-year queue, and a queue is a constraint with a customer list. Compute the ratio, not the absolute number, and remember the caution from chapter thirty-one: a backlog is an order book, not revenue, and order books taken at a peak of enthusiasm have been renegotiated before.

**Customer money arriving before the product does.** This is the accountant's tell, and the most reliable one, because it is audited. When buyers prepay to reserve capacity, the cash lands on the supplier's balance sheet as a contract liability or customer deposit long before any revenue is recognised. A memory maker's swelling contract liabilities were among the earliest hard evidence of the HBM shortage, and a shrinking balance is an equally early warning in the other direction. Look for it in the current liabilities note, and look for the matching prepayment sitting in the buyer's other assets.

**The buyer investing in its supplier.** The loudest signal of all, because it means a company with the best information in the chain has concluded that money alone will not get it to the front of the queue. Nvidia's four billion dollar investment in the photonics companies Lumentum and Coherent, announced on 2 March 2026 and reported by CNBC, came with multi-year purchase commitments and future capacity rights attached — a chip designer buying into laser manufacturing because lasers were becoming the tight stage. Amazon's roughly five-hundred-million-dollar anchor investment in the small modular reactor developer X-energy in October 2024 is the same move against a different constraint, as is the decision by the developers of the Abilene campus to install their own gas turbines rather than wait for the grid. When a buyer starts writing cheques to the people it buys from, the bottleneck has already moved; the press catches up a quarter or two later.

**Price rising with no change in specification.** The same part, the same performance, a higher number on the invoice. In a competitive stage this cannot happen for long. Watch also for terms hardening in the buyer's disfavour — take-or-pay clauses, non-refundable deposits, shorter price-protection windows. Contract terms tighten before prices do, and they tighten quietly.

**The constraint entering public policy.** By the time a scarcity is being legislated about, it is fully priced, but the policy record is a good confirmation and an excellent early warning about the *next* jurisdiction. Ireland's grid operator stopped accepting new data centre connection applications in the Dublin region in 2022, with the restriction only lifted under conditions in early 2026, per Data Center Dynamics and 2026 policy analysis. Any market where large-load connection policy is being rewritten is a market where power has already become the binding stage.

One negative tell deserves equal weight, because it prevents a great deal of wasted attention. A component whose price is falling, whose vendors advertise availability, and whose lead times are shortening is not the constraint, no matter how central it is to the technology or how often it appears in coverage. Importance and scarcity are different properties. The most discussed part of a system is rarely the tightest one.

## What might bind next

What follows is a list of candidates with the evidence attached, not a prediction, and certainly not advice about any security. Some of these will be relieved before they ever bind.

*Optical interconnect.* TrendForce reported in April 2026 that the market for AI optical transceivers would reach about twenty-six billion US dollars in 2026, from roughly sixteen and a half billion in 2025, and named component shortages as the primary bottleneck on capacity expansion. The scarce item within the scarce item is reported to be the electro-absorption modulated laser, made by a handful of firms — which is exactly what Nvidia's March 2026 investment was aimed at. A constraint two levels down inside a component most readers have never heard of is the characteristic shape of these things.

*Electrical equipment.* Transformers, switchgear, breakers and uninterruptible power supplies. The lead times quoted above come from procurement trade sources rather than filings, and the manufacturers are mostly divisions of large industrial groups whose disclosures do not break out the product lines. That opacity is itself worth noting: a constraint can be real and still be nearly invisible in published accounts.

*Skilled trades.* Discussed above. The distinguishing feature is that no amount of capital compresses an apprenticeship, and the rent goes to labour.

*Memory again, at the generational hand-off.* Chapter seventy-two takes this up properly. The move from HBM3E to HBM4 doubles the interface width, resets the qualification process, and re-divides a sold-out market. Generational transitions are when shares in a constraint change hands.

*Capital itself.* If credit tightens for the financed buyers, the binding stage stops being anything physical and becomes the balance sheet: who can still borrow to build. That constraint would not show up in lead times at all. It would show up in spreads, in covenant terms and in withdrawn guidance.

*And, finally, demand.* The candidate least discussed and most consequential. Every constraint in this chapter is a supply constraint, and supply constraints coexist with the happy assumption that whatever is built will be sold. If the tight stage becomes the customer's willingness to pay — if end demand grows more slowly than the capacity being committed against it — then nothing in the chain is scarce, and the rent disappears from every stage at once rather than moving to the next one. The model layer's reported gross margins are the place this would appear first: around forty-four per cent for Anthropic and around thirty-nine per cent for OpenAI on figures reported in 2026, both unaudited and both private. A supply bottleneck moves. A demand shortfall does not move; it drains.

## Using this

The framework reduces to four questions, asked in order, of any part of this industry at any date.

First, what is the tight stage right now — the one whose capacity sets the total? Second, how long does it take to replicate, and can money shorten that? Third, who holds it, and are they free to price it? Fourth, what is the next-longest lead time behind it, because that is where the constraint goes when this one is relieved?

Answer those four honestly and most of the industry's news becomes legible in advance. A capacity announcement at a non-binding stage is a press release. A prepayment at a binding stage is information. A record margin at a constraint is a fact about the present and a warning about the future, because the same margin is at that moment financing the capacity that will end it.

Every figure in this chapter carries a date because every figure in it will move, most of them within a year of publication. The lead times will shorten or stretch; the auction will clear somewhere else; the packaging gap will close or it will not. What a reader can do instead of memorising them is repeat the exercise: read the trade press for delivery weeks, the earnings calls for the phrase "sold out through", the balance sheets for customer prepayments, and the announcements for buyers investing in their own suppliers. Those four sources, checked quarterly, will identify the binding constraint of any given year more reliably than any forecast, including any in this book.

The constraint has moved four times in four years. It will move again, and when it does the pattern will look obvious in hindsight and will have been visible, in delivery schedules and contract liabilities, for several quarters beforehand. The accounts notice first. They usually do.
