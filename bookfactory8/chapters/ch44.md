# Microsoft

On 29 July 2026, on its results call for the year just ended, Microsoft told investors that Microsoft 365 Copilot had passed thirty million paid seats — up from twenty million a quarter earlier, its fastest quarterly addition since the product launched in November 2023, as reported by No Jitter and CIO Dive from the call in July 2026. Thirty million people paying for artificial intelligence is a large number. But the accountant's eye goes to the other number disclosed alongside it: Microsoft 365 had roughly four hundred and fifty million commercial subscribers, per the company's January 2026 results as compiled by Directions on Microsoft. Divide one disclosed figure by the other and the arithmetic — simple division, nothing more — says that after two and a half years of the most heavily marketed enterprise software launch in memory, about one commercial seat in fifteen carried the AI surcharge.

That single ratio is the best short description of where Microsoft stands. It is the company with the most to sell to, and it has so far sold to a fraction of it. Whether that fraction is a disappointment or a beginning is the argument of this chapter — and the argument, in miniature, of the whole application layer. Microsoft is where this book's four floors meet in a single set of accounts: it sells applications, part-owns and increasingly builds models, operates the second-largest cloud, and designs its own silicon. No other company holds a materially profitable position at every layer. The question the accounts must answer is whether holding every floor is a fortress or simply four exposures.

An earlier chapter, in the compute part of this book, read Azure on its own: the cloud pivot, the buildout, the depreciation mechanics. This chapter reads the whole company from the top of the stack down — from the seat where a customer pays — and takes up the three matters the earlier chapter deliberately left open: distribution as the deepest moat in the industry, capital expenditure as the largest open question in the accounts, and the unresolved strangeness of building your future on a partner who is building theirs on your customers.

## The whole company, on one page

Begin with the position as the market prices it. As at mid-August 2026, Microsoft's market capitalisation stood at roughly three point six six trillion US dollars, per the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings — the fourth most valuable company in the world, behind Nvidia at roughly five point three to five point four trillion, Apple at roughly four point four one trillion and Alphabet at roughly four point two trillion, same period, same sources. All of these figures move daily and will have moved by the time this page is read; the ranking sites update continuously, and the reader should treat every number in this chapter as a dated snapshot rather than a standing fact.

The operating results behind that valuation, per the company's results announcement of July 2026 for the fiscal year ended 30 June 2026: revenue of three hundred and thirty-one point eight billion dollars, up eighteen per cent; operating income of one hundred and fifty-five point two billion, up twenty-one per cent. The final quarter of that year, the three months to June 2026, showed where the money now sits. Total revenue of ninety billion dollars, up eighteen per cent. The Intelligent Cloud segment — Azure and the server estate — produced thirty-nine point three billion, up thirty-two per cent. Productivity and Business Processes — Office, Microsoft 365, LinkedIn, the application layer proper — produced thirty-seven point eight billion, up fourteen per cent. Azure passed one hundred billion dollars of revenue for the fiscal year, up forty-one per cent, the first time the company has disclosed a nine-figure Azure year, per the same July 2026 announcement and CNBC's coverage of it.

Two things in that segment table deserve a pause. The first is that the cloud segment has overtaken the applications segment in size — a crossing point, given that the applications business is the one built on Word, Excel and Outlook, the franchise the company was for its first four decades. Value inside Microsoft has been moving down its own internal stack, which is the book's thesis playing out within a single set of accounts. The second is the growth gap: thirty-two per cent against fourteen. The layer where Microsoft rents machines is growing at more than twice the rate of the layer where Microsoft sells outcomes to users. For a book about the application layer, that gap is the uncomfortable headline: even inside the company best placed to monetise AI applications, the application layer is not yet where the acceleration is.

Map the company onto the four floors and the shape is easy to state. At the application layer: Microsoft 365 and Office, the Copilot family across them, GitHub, LinkedIn, Windows, and a games business. At the model layer: a roughly twenty-seven per cent stake in OpenAI with model access contracted to 2032, per CNBC's October 2025 reporting of the partnership's restructuring, alongside a growing family of in-house models. At the compute layer: Azure. At the silicon layer: the Maia accelerator programme and, for the overwhelming bulk of its fleet, purchased Nvidia and AMD parts. Four floors, one balance sheet. The rest of the chapter takes them in the order the money arrives — from the top.

## The moat that pays for everything

Every company in this book has one asset the others cannot buy. Microsoft's is the least glamorous in the industry: it already sells software to essentially every large organisation on earth, under multi-year enterprise agreements, through a sales and support machine assembled over four decades. The compute-layer chapter set out what that machine consists of — procurement relationships, certifications, identity integration, billing lines, references. Here the question is what it is worth at the application layer, where the customer actually pays for AI, and the answer comes in the design of the product itself.

Microsoft 365 Copilot was announced in July 2023 at thirty dollars per user per month, on top of an eligible Microsoft 365 licence, as reported from the company's partner conference in July 2023. Note what that pricing does. It does not ask the customer to adopt anything new. It attaches to a subscription that already exists, a deployment that has already happened, a budget line that is already approved. A start-up selling an AI assistant must win the enterprise's trust, pass its security review, integrate with its identity system and then persuade a committee to create a new supplier record. Microsoft sells the same capability as an amendment to an existing invoice. The marginal cost of that distribution is close to nil, and — a point made in the compute chapter that bears repeating from the application side — the asset producing it carries a book value of nil too, because forty years of relationship-building was expensed as salaries and marketing when it was incurred. The most valuable thing the company owns appears nowhere on its balance sheet.

The disclosed adoption record, assembled from the company's fiscal 2026 results calls as reported by Directions on Microsoft, No Jitter and CIO Dive across 2026, runs as follows.

| Date of disclosure | Microsoft 365 Copilot paid seats | Context disclosed alongside |
|---|---|---|
| January 2026 (Q2 FY26 call) | Fifteen million | Roughly four hundred and fifty million Microsoft 365 commercial subscribers |
| April 2026 (Q3 FY26 call) | Over twenty million | — |
| July 2026 (Q4 FY26 call) | Over thirty million | Customers with more than fifty thousand seats up more than sevenfold year on year; paid Microsoft 365 commercial seats up six per cent |

Read the table twice, because it supports two opposite stories and both are true.

The optimist's reading: seats doubled in six months, the latest quarter added ten million — the fastest since launch — and the sevenfold growth in fifty-thousand-seat customers says the product is moving from pilot to standard-issue inside the largest organisations, which is how enterprise software has always spread: slowly, then by the hundred thousand. At the announced thirty-dollar list price, thirty million seats would imply roughly ten point eight billion dollars of annualised revenue — but that is this author's arithmetic on the list price, not a disclosed figure, and enterprise agreements are discounted in ways Microsoft does not disclose, so treat it as an upper bound on a single product line, not a fact.

The sceptic's reading: one seat in fifteen, thirty months in, from the company with the least friction in the industry — and the underlying seat base growing at six per cent a year, which means the AI story cannot ride on new customers; it must come almost entirely from attach rate and price. The application layer's revenue question, for Microsoft, is not "who will buy software from us" — everyone already does — but "what share of an existing wage bill will a finance director sign over for assistance", and that question is being answered one renewal negotiation at a time, invisibly, in discounts the outside world never sees.

The same pattern, one layer more advanced, is visible at GitHub, which Microsoft has owned since 2018. GitHub Copilot — the AI pair-programmer, the first Copilot and still the proof of concept for the franchise — passed twenty million all-time users in mid-2025, as reported by TechCrunch in July 2025. Software developers were the first profession whose daily tool acquired a frontier model, and their adoption curve is the one every other Copilot is priced against. It is worth noticing that the franchise's flagship began as a coding tool: the application layer monetises first where the output is testable and the user can immediately see whether the machine was right.

What the moat does not do is equally important to state. Distribution wins the right to make the sale; it does not win the argument about value. A seat that is bought must be renewed, and a chief information officer who signed ten thousand seats in the enthusiasm of 2025 will, at renewal, ask the question this book keeps returning to: what did it change, and what would removing it cost? Microsoft's fortunes at the application layer rest on that renewal conversation going well tens of thousands of times, and no disclosure yet exists from which an outsider can judge how it is going. The honest position is that the attach numbers are real, dated and disclosed, and the retention economics are not yet visible from outside.

## The model layer: rented, part-owned, and now home-made

One floor down, Microsoft's position is unlike anyone else's, because it holds the layer three different ways at once: it rents intelligence, it part-owns the company it rents from, and it has begun manufacturing its own.

The rented-and-part-owned relationship is the OpenAI arrangement, whose full history — from the one billion dollar investment of July 2019, through the roughly ten billion reported by CNBC and Bloomberg in January 2023, to a total commitment generally reported at about thirteen billion — was traced in the compute-layer chapter. What matters at the application layer is the shape it had reached by mid-2026. Under the October 2025 restructuring, Microsoft held roughly twenty-seven per cent of OpenAI, then valued at around one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars, with access to OpenAI's models contracted through 2032, while OpenAI committed to purchase an incremental two hundred and fifty billion dollars of Azure services, per CNBC and Data Centre Dynamics in October 2025. Under the April 2026 rewrite, Microsoft stopped paying revenue share to OpenAI; OpenAI continues capped revenue-share payments to Microsoft through 2030 and became free to license its models to anyone, on any cloud, per CNBC, Bloomberg and Forbes reporting in April 2026. And after its one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round of March 2026, OpenAI carried a valuation of eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollars, per Forbes and industry reporting in 2026.

On paper this is one of the great investments of the era: a stake reported at one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars in October 2025 against roughly thirteen billion invested, plus contracted model access, plus a quarter-trillion-dollar customer commitment. But the application layer is where its cost becomes visible, because the thing Microsoft part-owns sells against the thing Microsoft sells. OpenAI's consumer and enterprise products compete for the same seats, in the same organisations, that Copilot is sold into — and since April 2026 it may serve them from any rival's cloud. Every seat OpenAI wins directly is a seat Copilot does not, inside a customer relationship Microsoft spent forty years building. The deepest moat in the industry is being fished, with permission, by the company whose models fill it.

Microsoft's response has been to make the intelligence inside its products a matter of sourcing rather than allegiance — to treat models the way a manufacturer treats components, dual-sourced and substitutable. Three moves define it, each dated.

First, its own models. In August 2025 Microsoft's AI division under Mustafa Suleyman released MAI-1-preview, the company's first foundation model trained end to end in-house, on roughly fifteen thousand Nvidia H100 chips, alongside a voice model — with the division's leadership stressing efficiency and cost rather than frontier ambition, per Semafor and Engadget in August 2025. By its Build conference in 2026 the company was showing a family of seven in-house models, including a thirty-five-billion-parameter reasoning model, MAI-Thinking-1, described by Suleyman as its first, per GeekWire's 2026 reporting. The stated register is telling: these are not announced as the best models in the world, but as good enough, cheap enough, and owned.

Second, the other laboratory. In November 2025 Microsoft announced an investment of up to five billion dollars in Anthropic — alongside Nvidia's up to ten billion — while Anthropic committed to purchase thirty billion dollars of Azure compute, in a transaction valuing Anthropic in the region of three hundred and fifty billion dollars, per CNBC in November 2025; the same reporting framed the deal explicitly as reducing dependence on OpenAI. Copilot, in consequence, is being re-plumbed to draw on OpenAI models, Anthropic models or Microsoft's own, workload by workload, whichever serves a given seat at the lowest cost per answer.

Third, the accounting consequence of part-ownership, which lands every quarter whether or not any strategy is executed. Under the equity method, Microsoft's share of OpenAI's losses flows through Microsoft's income statement: in October 2025 the company disclosed that its September-quarter net income of twenty-seven point seven billion dollars would have been three point one billion higher but for its share of OpenAI's losses — forty-one cents per diluted share, as reported by CNBC in October 2025. By August 2026 CNBC was reporting that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the earnings picture of the companies holding them. A reader of Microsoft's accounts is therefore also, involuntarily, a reader of OpenAI's burn rate — a quarterly telemetry feed on a private competitor, published inside the incumbent's own net income line.

The strategic logic of the whole structure is coherent, but it should be stated plainly, because it is stranger than the sanitised word "partnership" suggests. Microsoft is hedging against its most important supplier with that supplier's chief rival, while building its own substitutes, while booking a share of the supplier's losses, while collecting the supplier's committed cloud spending. Each arrangement is disclosed and dated. Together they describe two companies preparing, in full public view and at great mutual profit, for the end of needing each other.

## Silicon: the quiet floor

At the bottom of its stack Microsoft is a designer, not a manufacturer, and a recent one. It announced its first custom AI accelerator, Maia 100, with the Cobalt processor in November 2023, and unveiled Maia 200 on 26 January 2026: an inference-focused part built on TSMC's three-nanometre process with two hundred and sixteen gigabytes of high-bandwidth memory, already running production workloads including Microsoft 365 Copilot, per GeekWire and TrendForce in January 2026.

The honest way to size this position is modestly. Microsoft's own positioning for Maia is cost per unit of inference, not peak performance; the fleet remains overwhelmingly built on purchased Nvidia and AMD silicon; and no disclosure exists from which to estimate what share of Microsoft's workloads its own chips carry. What the silicon floor contributes to the whole-company position is not independence but negotiating posture. A buyer spending at the scale described below, able to move its highest-volume internal workload — Copilot inference — onto its own part, bargains differently with every supplier in the semiconductor part of this book. The chip does not need to win benchmarks to earn its keep; it needs to exist, credibly, at volume. That is what the January 2026 announcement, with a named process node and a production workload attached, was for.

## The bill

Now the largest number in the accounts, and the largest question. In April 2026, Microsoft told investors to expect about one hundred and ninety billion dollars of capital expenditure for 2026, citing among other pressures the rising price of memory, per CNBC in April 2026. The June 2026 quarter alone carried roughly forty-one billion dollars of capital spending, and the September 2026 quarter was guided to more than fifty billion, per the July 2026 results and CNBC's coverage. Set the plan against the results quoted earlier: one hundred and ninety billion of spending against one hundred and fifty-five point two billion of operating income. The company is committing more to physical plant in a year than its operations earn. And it is doing so in company: across 2026 the four largest cloud builders — Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta — were on course to spend approaching seven hundred billion dollars of capital expenditure between them, as reported by CNBC in February 2026.

The mechanism by which that spending reaches future income statements — capitalise now, depreciate over an assumed life, and mind the assumption — was worked through in the compute-layer chapter, along with Microsoft's own history on the assumption: the 2022 extension of server lives from four years to six, reported by The Register in August 2022 as adding roughly three point seven billion dollars to the following year's profit without a single machine running differently. There is no need to repeat the arithmetic here. What the whole-company chapter must add is what the spending means for the layers above it.

It means, first, that Microsoft's application-layer profits are no longer capital-light. The old Office franchise earned its margin on assets that amounted to little more than buildings and goodwill; the new one answers every prompt on hardware that ages like fruit. A Copilot seat's gross margin now depends on the cost per answer of a fleet whose economics are set two floors down, by Nvidia's pricing, by memory markets, and by depreciation schedules. The application layer's margin question and the compute layer's return question have become the same question, inside one company.

It means, second, that the revenue side of the return calculation leans heavily on two customers who are also the two best-funded private companies in the industry. OpenAI's incremental two hundred and fifty billion dollar Azure commitment, per the October 2025 reporting, and Anthropic's thirty billion, per CNBC in November 2025, are purchase obligations of a scale enterprise software has never seen — and they are promises made by loss-making companies funding themselves through venture rounds. The commitment and the eleven-figure quarterly losses flowing through Microsoft's equity-method line belong to the same counterparties. Contracted is not the same as collected; the remaining-performance-obligation disclosures in Microsoft's future filings are where those promises will either become revenue on schedule or quietly stretch.

And it means, third, that the market's tolerance is itself part of the record. When Microsoft raised its spending plans with the July 2026 results, citing demand, the shares rose eight per cent, per CNBC in July 2026. As at that date, shareholders were paying for the buildout willingly. That consent is a fact with a date on it, like every other figure here, and it is the one most likely to move without notice.

## Strategy, challenge, opportunity

Strip the noise and Microsoft's whole-company position reduces to three sentences, one for each word in this section's heading.

The strategy is to tax the transition rather than bet on any single winner of it. Microsoft does not need OpenAI to beat Anthropic, or its own models to beat either, or Maia to beat Nvidia. It needs organisations to adopt AI through channels it already owns — and it has arranged matters so that it collects at the seat if Copilot sells, at the meter if the labs sell, on the stake if OpenAI compounds, and at the toll if the whole market simply grows. Four floors, four ways to be paid; the company is long the transition itself. That is what the enterprise agreement machine, the twenty-seven per cent stake, the two model partnerships, the hundred-billion-dollar cloud and the in-house silicon add up to when read as one position.

The challenge is that every one of those collection points is under pressure from the same direction: the cost of staying in the game is set by other people. The capital expenditure plan is dictated by demand Microsoft does not control and hardware prices it does not set; the model roadmap inside its flagship product was, until recently, another company's; the equity-method line moves with a burn rate it cannot direct; and its single most important commercial relationship was renegotiated three times in eighteen months, each time loosening. For a company whose old franchise was defined by control — of the operating system, the file format, the licence terms — the new position is defined by dependencies, expensively hedged.

The opportunity is the one the ratio at the top of this chapter measures. Fourteen out of every fifteen commercial seats do not yet pay for Copilot. If the product earns its renewals — the part no outsider can yet verify — then the attach rate has four hundred and twenty million seats of headroom inside contracts that already exist, before a single new customer is signed. No other company at the application layer starts from that base. Whether the fraction closes is the cleanest single indicator of whether the application layer can ever justify what the layers beneath it are spending.

## What to watch, and what not to conclude

The disclosures a reader needs arrive quarterly, and five of them carry this chapter forward. The Copilot seat count against the four-hundred-and-fifty-million-seat base — attach rate, the application layer's scoreboard. The growth-rate gap between the cloud segment and the applications segment, thirty-two per cent against fourteen at the June 2026 quarter, which will show whether AI value keeps sinking down the stack even inside the company that owns every floor. The capital expenditure line against operating income, one hundred and ninety billion against one hundred and fifty-five point two in 2026's figures. The equity-method line, for as long as Microsoft holds its stake in a loss-making rival. And any change — in either direction — to assumed depreciable lives, where a single estimate revision moved reported profit by billions once already, in 2022's disclosure.

What the reader should not do is convert any of this into a view on the shares, and this book does not offer one; it is not investment advice, gives no price targets, and predicts nothing. Every figure above is a snapshot with a date attached, drawn from the company's announcements and the named reporting, and each will be superseded by the next results release.

The judgement that will outlast the snapshots is about position, not price. Microsoft entered the AI period as the incumbent with the most to lose and moved earlier, and at larger scale, than any incumbent in the industry's history: thirteen billion dollars into a research laboratory before the boom, a fleet built ahead of demand, a surcharge attached to the world's largest software estate. What it built is the only genuinely full-stack position in this book — and the accounts now show what a full-stack position costs to hold: record margins committed to record spending, a moat that must be defended seat by seat against a partner it funded, and a net income line that rises and falls with a competitor's losses. Owning every layer means owning every layer's risks. The next chapter turns to the other company that comes closest to the same claim, and manages the same contradiction by different means.
