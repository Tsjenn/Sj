# Where the Money Meets the User

On the third of the month a card is charged twenty US dollars, and a receipt arrives by email a few seconds later. One line, one amount, no detail. It is the least informative document in this entire industry, and this chapter is about everything it leaves out.

The opening chapter of this book followed the money down the stack at the scale of the industry: hundreds of billions of capital expenditure, trillion-dollar market values, national-grid quantities of electricity. This part starts at the other end of the telescope. The next nine chapters are about the application layer — the floor where the user actually pays — and the argument that runs through all of them is an argument about margin: who at the top of the stack can keep one, and why most cannot. To make that argument, the structure of the book has to stop being a filing system and become something you can watch operating on a single transaction.

So take the twenty dollars and walk it down the staircase, slowly this time. By the end you should be able to say who touched it, in what order, what each of them kept, and — the part that matters most and is least obvious — when. Because the four layers do not merely take different shares of the payment. They take them in different accounting periods, and the layer with the fattest margin was paid before the subscriber had ever heard of the product.

Twenty dollars a month is still the shape of the consumer price. As chapter one noted from the published pricing compared across vendors in 2026, the standard consumer tier at the major assistants has sat at twenty dollars a month, with premium tiers above it and team seats around thirty dollars per user. The price is a convention inherited from a decade of subscription software. Nothing below it in the stack was consulted when it was set. Everything below it determines whether it works.

## The toll collectors who are not on the stack

The first cut of the twenty dollars goes to companies that appear on no layer of the four.

Every card payment passes through processing infrastructure, and the infrastructure charges for passage. The standard published rate for online card payments in the United States — Stripe's widely used benchmark, per its published pricing as compiled in 2026 fee guides — is 2.9 per cent plus thirty cents per successful charge. Apply that tariff to a twenty-dollar subscription and the toll is eighty-eight cents. That is arithmetic on a public price list, not a disclosure by anyone; negotiated rates for large merchants are lower and are not published. Call it something under a dollar, taken before any layer of the stack has been paid at all.

That is the polite version. The impolite version is the app store. A subscription started inside an iPhone app must, in the standard case, run through Apple's payment system, and Apple's published standard commission on digital goods is 30 per cent — fifteen for developers under a million dollars of annual proceeds, and fifteen from the second year of any given subscription, per the terms of the App Store Small Business Program Apple announced in November 2020 and its published subscription terms since. On a twenty-dollar subscription, the standard first-year commission is six dollars. Thirty per cent of the retail price, for distribution, taken by a company that trained no model, built no data centre and fabricated no chip.

This is why assistant vendors work so hard to move subscribers to the web. It is also the first lesson of the application layer, delivered before we have even reached it: at the top of the stack, whoever controls the doorway can tax the room. The doorway is worth fighting over precisely because the twenty dollars has not yet met a single computational cost. Distribution takes its share off revenue; every layer below takes its share off what is left.

For the rest of this chapter, assume the subscriber signed up on the web and the payment toll was under a dollar. The stack now gets its turn.

## The application's share, and two accounting questions

The company that sold the subscription now holds roughly nineteen dollars. Two things about that money are worth more attention than they usually get, and both belong to this book's profession rather than to the technology.

The first is that the nineteen dollars is not yet revenue. It is cash, received in advance for a month of service not yet delivered, and it sits on the balance sheet as a liability — deferred revenue — that is released into the income statement as the month is served. For a steady-state subscription business this is a detail. For a fast-growing one it is not, because cash collected runs ahead of revenue recognised, which is one reason young subscription companies can burn less cash than their losses suggest, and one reason a reported "annualised run rate" — a monthly figure multiplied by twelve, as chapter one warned — tells you about the most recent month and nothing else.

The second question is stranger and matters more for reading this industry: when an application resells intelligence it buys from a model provider, whose revenue is the twenty dollars? Under the revenue standards that govern both IFRS and US GAAP, the test is control: a company that controls the service before delivering it to the customer books the full twenty dollars as revenue and the model fee as a cost — it is a principal. A company that merely arranges for someone else's service books only its own margin — it is an agent. The judgement is genuinely difficult for a thin product wrapped around someone else's model, and the difference is not cosmetic. The same business, the same users and the same cash flows can report twenty dollars of revenue per subscriber or six, depending on the answer. Every headline about an AI startup's revenue growth is downstream of that judgement, and the reader is rarely shown which answer the accounts embody. This book does not allege that any named company answers it wrongly. It says only: when you see a small application company reporting large revenue, the first question is not "is it true?" but "is it gross or net?"

Now to what the application actually keeps. Here the honest position is that for the AI-native application companies, almost nothing is disclosed: they are private, young and selective about numbers. So the mechanism has to be taught with labelled round numbers instead.

Take an illustrative application company, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real firm. It sells a specialist assistant at twenty dollars a month and has one hundred thousand subscribers: two million dollars of monthly revenue. Payment and platform tolls take two hundred thousand. Its model bill — the fee it pays a frontier lab for every token its subscribers consume — averages six dollars per subscriber: six hundred thousand a month. What remains is a gross margin of about 60 per cent, before a single engineer, marketer or lawyer has been paid.

Sixty per cent sounds comfortable. Look inside the average. Suppose the median subscriber's usage costs two dollars a month in model fees, while the heaviest twentieth of subscribers cost forty dollars each. The comfortable margin is a blend of very profitable light users and outright loss-making heavy ones — each heavy user consumes twice their subscription in model fees alone. The application's margin is not a property of its product. It is a bet on the usage distribution of its customers, and every model release that makes longer, richer output more attractive reprices the bet. Double the average usage with no price change and the illustrative company's gross margin falls from 60 per cent to 30. Nothing went wrong. Nobody left. The customers simply liked the product more.

This is the flat-rate problem, and it is the quiet engine of most of the odd behaviour at the top of the stack: usage caps on "unlimited" plans, throttling at peak times, cheaper models silently substituted for expensive ones on routine queries, premium tiers priced at ten times the standard one. Rate limits are margin management wearing a politeness costume. None of this is scandalous — a flat price for a metered cost always ends this way, as anyone who remembers all-you-can-eat mobile data will recognise — but it means the application layer's unit economics are permanently exposed to its own users' enthusiasm.

## The model provider's fee

The six dollars of model fees in the illustrative example now drop one floor, and here the numbers stop being illustrative, because API prices are published.

Model intelligence is sold by the token — a unit of text roughly three-quarters of a word — with separate prices for tokens in and tokens out. As at mid-2026, per the vendors' published price lists compiled by API pricing trackers, flagship models from the two leading labs were priced at about five dollars per million input tokens and twenty-five to thirty dollars per million output tokens; mid-tier models around three dollars in and fifteen out; small fast models at a dollar or less. Batch processing was offered at half price, and cached input at discounts of about 90 per cent. Those prices change often, and have moved in one direction: chapter one cited 2026 reporting that inference cost per query had fallen roughly 95 per cent since GPT-4 launched in early 2023.

Notice the ratio before moving on. Output tokens cost roughly five times input tokens. Generation is the expensive act; reading is comparatively cheap. Every product decision at the layer above — short answers by default, summaries instead of full drafts, "expand" buttons that make the user ask for length — is that ratio, surfaced as design.

For the model provider, the fee received is revenue, and what it keeps is the number this book has been circling since its first chapter. The reported figures — with all the caveats chapter five attaches to companies that publish no audited accounts — were given there: for Anthropic, compute costs of about seventy-one cents per dollar of revenue in the first quarter of 2026 with a projected fifty-six in the second and gross margin tracking around 44 per cent, per figures shared with investors and reported in 2026 by PitchBook and Morningstar; for OpenAI, gross margin reported at roughly 39 per cent in the first quarter of 2026. Unaudited, unfiled, indicative. But the shape is consistent: at the model layer, something like half or more of every dollar of revenue leaves immediately as compute.

One structural wrinkle matters here, because it collapses two layers of the diagram into one. The consumer subscription that opened this chapter is very often sold by the model provider itself — the frontier labs are their own largest applications. In that case there is no external model fee: the twenty dollars lands directly on the model layer, and the reported economics above are the economics of the subscription. The application layer as a separate business exists where someone other than a lab owns the customer — a software incumbent embedding intelligence in an existing product, or a startup building on rented models. Part of the margin fight this part describes is exactly here: the labs compete with their own customers for the top of the stack, and chapter fifty returns to what that does to the startups.

## The cloud's compute cost

Whether the model layer kept 44 cents or 39 of its dollar, the majority went down another floor, to whoever owns the machines. This is the layer where the twenty dollars stops being a payment and becomes physics: a slice of an accelerator's time, measured in seconds, plus the electricity to run it and the building it sits in.

Compute has a market price, and in 2026 it was unusually visible, because a rental market in GPU-hours had grown up alongside the giants. Per GPU rental market surveys published in August 2026 by Thunder Compute and IntuitionLabs, on-demand rates for an H100-class accelerator ran from about a dollar and a half per GPU-hour at the marketplace floor to over ten dollars at the top: the median across dedicated GPU clouds was about four dollars seventeen an hour, against roughly seven dollars eighty-nine for the same class of hardware on the three big hyperscale clouds. The same silicon, at nearly twice the price, depending on the door you buy it through — the premium buying integration, ecosystem, security accreditation and, not least, enterprise habit. Chapter twenty-eight, on the neoclouds, put the numbers of that gap in context; here it serves one purpose: compute is priced, liquid and comparable in a way nothing else in the stack is, which is exactly what you would expect of the layer closest to being a commodity.

The frontier labs, though, do not pay on-demand rates. They buy compute the way airlines buy fuel: years ahead, in negotiated multi-year commitments whose prices are not disclosed and which are tangled with the investment relationships mapped in Part 6 — the cloud is, in the two most important cases, also a shareholder of its customer. What a lab actually pays its cloud per hour is one of the genuinely important numbers in this industry that nobody outside the contracts can see. This book will not guess it. What can be said from disclosure is that the cloud layer, in aggregate, is not a charity: chapter one cited Google Cloud's second quarter of 2026 at twenty-four point eight billion dollars of revenue with an operating margin of 35.6 per cent, per reporting from The Motley Fool and The Globe and Mail in August 2026. Somewhere between the lab's compute bill and the cloud's operating margin, the pass-through is taking a healthy commission.

And what does the compute actually cost the cloud? Its cost of a GPU-hour is a stack of its own: depreciation on the accelerator and the building, power, cooling, networking, and people — almost all of it fixed the moment the data centre was committed. The marginal cost of serving one more query is close to the electricity; the average cost is dominated by capital recovery. Which brings the twenty dollars to the strangest part of its journey.

## The margin that was booked years earlier

Here is the fact this chapter exists to establish, because the rest of the part leans on it: by the time the subscriber's query runs, the most profitable participant in the whole transaction has already been paid, and the books on that profit are closed.

The accelerator answering the query was sold by its designer — say Nvidia, since per the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings it was the world's most valuable company at roughly five point three to five point four trillion dollars as at mid-August 2026 — at the moment the hardware was delivered to the cloud, in some earlier fiscal year. The margin on that sale is not a mystery: Nvidia's fiscal year ended 25 January 2026 produced a GAAP gross margin of 71.1 per cent, per the company's audited results announced in February 2026. The foundry's margin was earned earlier still, when the wafers shipped — TSMC reported a gross margin of 67.7 per cent in the second quarter of 2026, per its results. And the lithography supplier's margin was earned before that, when the machine was installed in the fab, possibly years before the chip it would eventually print was even designed.

The subscriber's twenty dollars never reaches those companies. It cannot; they were paid long ago, out of the cloud's capital expenditure, which was funded by the cloud's shareholders and its other businesses in anticipation of exactly this subscription. What the twenty dollars does is repay that anticipation, one month at a time. The mechanism that connects the two moments is depreciation: the cloud spreads the accelerator's purchase price — Nvidia's 71 per cent margin embedded within it — over the years it expects the machine to be useful, and charges each year's slice against the revenue of that year. The chip vendor's profit, recognised in one accounting period, is recovered from users across many later ones. A subscriber in 2026 is partly paying off a margin that was booked in 2024, on a machine printed on a tool bought in 2021.

That is why the assumed useful life of a server is, as chapter thirty-two argued at length, the single most consequential estimate in this industry's accounts, and why the changes chapter one catalogued — Microsoft extending server lives from four years to six from its 2023 fiscal year; Amazon going the other way, cutting certain server lives from six years to five from 1 January 2025, a change it disclosed on 7 February 2025 as expected to reduce that year's operating income by seven hundred million dollars — matter to a twenty-dollar subscription. The shorter the assumed life, the more of the chip vendor's old margin must be recovered from each month of service, and the higher the true cost of the compute under every query. The estimate is honest, disclosed and audited. It is also, functionally, the exchange rate between the bottom of the stack's past profits and the top of the stack's present costs.

So the picture of the payment is not a bucket brigade, with each layer passing a share of the same dollar downward in the same month. It is closer to a mortgage. The bottom layers were paid in full, up front, at high margins, with other people's capital. The layers above are working it off.

And for the single subscription itself, a reconciliation can only be illustrative, because the real contracts are private. So, explicitly: what follows is an illustrative split of one twenty-dollar month, using round numbers chosen for clarity, consistent in shape with the published tariffs and reported margins above but belonging to no real company. A dollar to payment rails — or six to an app store, if the subscriber signed up on a phone. Six dollars to the model layer in metered fees. Of that six, perhaps three and a half continue down to compute. Of that, well over a dollar is depreciation — the instalment on hardware margins recognised by the silicon layer years earlier. What remains at each floor pays that floor's payroll, research and shareholders, or fails to. The point of the exercise is not the split, which will be wrong in detail for any real firm. The point is the direction of error a reader should expect: the further down the stack, the harder the share and the older the profit.

## The fight this part is about

Stand back from the staircase and the shape of the application layer's predicament is visible, and it can be stated as three facts established above.

First, the price at the top was set by convention, not cost. Twenty dollars a month is what consumer software costs, because that is what it has cost. Nobody derived it from tokens.

Second, the costs below the top are metered, volatile and set by layers with more scarcity. The application pays per token, on price lists it does not control, for usage its own customers determine, to suppliers who are often also its competitors.

Third, the layer is porous at both ends. Above it, distribution platforms tax the doorway at up to 30 per cent, per Apple's published terms. Below it, the model providers sell the same intelligence to everyone — including, through their own consumer tiers, to the application's customers directly.

A business squeezed like that survives in one of a few ways, and the next nine chapters are organised around them. It can own distribution so thoroughly that the toll flows to it rather than from it — which is the story of Microsoft and Alphabet, whose assistants ride inside products with billions of installed users, and of Apple, which owns the doorway itself. It can bundle intelligence into software the customer already pays for, so the AI never has to justify its own price line — the incumbent software companies of chapter forty-nine. It can build workflow and data lock-in fast enough that switching stops being a matter of cancelling one direct debit — the hope of most of the startups in chapter fifty. Or it can be the model provider, vertically integrated into its own application, and fight for the customer relationship with the margin structure this chapter has just walked through.

What it cannot do is nothing. Chapter one made the argument in aggregate: this cycle's scarcity lives at the bottom of the stack, and margin has followed it there — roughly seventy cents of gross profit on the dollar at the silicon layer against forty-odd, unaudited, at the model layer, and an undisclosed figure at the top that no one is in a hurry to publish. This chapter's contribution is the mechanism, seen at the scale of one subscription: the bottom was paid first, at the highest margin, with capital the upper layers must now recover from users twenty dollars at a time, through prices set by habit, for usage they cannot fully control.

Whether anyone at the top can hold a durable margin against that current — and which of the very different companies now trying actually might — is the question the rest of this part answers, one set of accounts at a time. Start with the company that arranged to be paid at three layers at once.
