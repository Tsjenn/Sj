# The Application Startups

On 6 November 2023, OpenAI held its first developer conference and, in a keynote of about three quarters of an hour, announced the Assistants API, custom GPTs and a GPT Store. Each announcement was received as a gift to developers. Each was also, for a particular kind of young company, an obituary. Teams that had spent a year building document chunking, retrieval plumbing, conversation threading and a tidy interface on top of someone else's model watched the platform absorb all of it into a single API call and a menu item, priced at whatever the platform felt like charging.

The industry already had a word for this, borrowed from an older platform era: being sherlocked. What was new was the speed. In the desktop and mobile eras, a platform owner absorbed a third-party feature every year or two, usually at an annual release. In this cycle it happens at conference cadence, and the platform owner is also the supplier of the single most expensive input in the dependent company's cost of sales.

This chapter is about the companies at the top of the stack — the ones with no fabs, no clusters and no frontier training runs, whose entire business is the last mile between a model and a person who pays. Many of them grew faster than any software companies in history. A great many then stalled, sold themselves for parts, or discovered that their revenue growth and their gross margin were moving in opposite directions. Some did not stall at all. The interesting question is not which outcome was more common. It is what, in the accounts, distinguished the two.

## What a wrapper is, in accounting terms

The word wrapper is used as an insult, which makes it useless for analysis. Put it in the ledger instead.

An application company built on a model API has a cost of sales dominated by a variable payment to a supplier, incurred every time a customer uses the product. That is unlike classical enterprise software, where the marginal cost of one more user of an already-built product rounds to nothing, and where gross margins of eighty to ninety per cent were the norm rather than the aspiration. It is quite like a business that resells someone else's manufactured good with a service layer attached — a distributor, in other words, with better branding.

Three consequences follow directly, and they are consequences of arithmetic rather than of strategy.

First, gross margin is set partly by a supplier who is also a competitor. Second, usage growth raises cost of sales roughly in line with revenue, so scale does not thin the cost per customer the way seat-based software does. Third, the balance sheet is thin: there is no capitalised asset corresponding to the intelligence being sold, because the intelligence is rented by the token. A software company of the previous era at least capitalised some development cost and owned its code. A pure application layer built on an API owns an interface, a customer list and a set of prompts.

None of that is disqualifying. Distribution businesses can be excellent businesses. But it means the question an accountant asks of such a company is not "how fast is revenue growing" — in this cycle everything's revenue grew fast — but "what happens to the gross margin as you get bigger, and what would happen to the business if your supplier changed its price, its terms, or its product scope tomorrow".

The industry data on that first question is now reasonably good. ICONIQ's 2026 State of AI work, as summarised in industry coverage in January 2026, put the average gross margin on AI products at about fifty-two per cent in 2026, up from roughly forty-one per cent in 2024, against the eighty per cent that defined mature software-as-a-service. The same work put model inference at roughly twenty-three per cent of total revenue at scaling-stage business-to-business AI companies, a share that had risen rather than fallen as products matured. Bessemer Venture Partners' pricing work in February 2026 landed in the same place, framing AI gross margins at fifty to sixty per cent against eighty to ninety for traditional software. These are survey figures from private companies with inconsistent definitions, not audited numbers, and they will have moved by the time you read them. But the direction is consistent across sources: the software industry's defining margin structure does not automatically transfer to products that think.

Here is the shape of the difference, using illustrative round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company:

| Per 100 dollars of revenue | Classical seat-based software | Illustrative model-dependent application |
|---|---|---|
| Hosting and support | 15 | 12 |
| Model inference paid to a supplier | 0 | 30 |
| Gross profit | 85 | 58 |
| Sales and marketing | 40 | 40 |
| Research and development | 25 | 30 |
| General and administrative | 12 | 12 |
| Operating profit | 8 | (24) |

The illustration is not a forecast, and the cost lines are invented. Its only purpose is to show where the pressure sits. Move inference from thirty to fifteen and the illustrative company is close to breaking even; move it to forty-five and no amount of growth saves it. The single line item that decides the outcome is the one the company does not control.

## The critique, stated fairly

The case against the application layer, made properly rather than as a sneer, has four parts.

The first is input dependence, set out in the preceding section. When the largest component of cost of sales is bought from one of a handful of suppliers, pricing power runs upstream. In practice this cut both ways: published prices for a given level of capability fell hard. OpenAI's launch pricing for GPT-4 in March 2023 was thirty US dollars per million input tokens and sixty dollars per million output tokens; by 2026 the lists for models of comparable or better capability sat far lower, with fast tiers advertised at cents per million input tokens. Any token price quoted in a book is wrong within months, and the vendors publish their own. The point is structural: falling input prices flattered the gross margins of every application company simultaneously, which means they were not a competitive advantage for any of them. They also lowered the cost of a new entrant copying the product.

The second is feature adjacency, which the 2023 keynote demonstrated and which recurred throughout the period. If your product is a thin layer of user experience over a general capability, then the owner of the general capability can add your layer at any time, at close to zero marginal cost, to an audience you cannot reach. This is not misconduct; it is the ordinary behaviour of a platform, and every platform era has worked this way.

The third is distribution asymmetry, and it is the most brutal of the four. OpenAI said ChatGPT had reached about eight hundred million weekly active users in October 2025 and about nine hundred million by February 2026, per company statements reported at the time. No application startup has a top of funnel of that order. If a feature is genuinely general — summarise this, draft that, search the web, read this document — the company with nine hundred million weekly users will win it by default, because it does not need to acquire a single customer to launch.

The fourth is the absence of a switching cost. If a customer's data, configuration, history and process live inside your product, leaving is a project. If the customer merely typed into your text box, leaving is a decision made in an afternoon.

Jasper is the case usually cited, and it deserves to be described accurately rather than mocked. The company raised one hundred and twenty-five million US dollars in October 2022 at a one point five billion dollar valuation, led by Insight Partners, per reporting of the round at the time. It was a genuinely good product for marketing copy at a moment when the underlying capability was hard for ordinary users to reach. ChatGPT's public launch weeks later put a free, general version of that capability in front of everyone. Reporting during 2023 described the company making its first layoffs in July, less than nine months after the round, and cutting its internal valuation by about twenty per cent in September alongside the appointment of a new chief executive. The company did not disappear; it repositioned towards enterprise marketing teams. But the shape of the story — fast growth on a good interface, then a free general substitute, then a repricing — is the shape the sceptics predicted, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

Builder.ai illustrates a different failure that is often filed under the same heading and should not be. Administrators were appointed in May 2025, after a creditor seized thirty-seven million US dollars from the company's accounts, as Bloomberg reported on 20 May 2025. Outlets covering the collapse that month reported both a substantial gap between revenue the company had represented to investors and revenue subsequently identified, and work marketed as automated that was in fact performed by several hundred engineers. Reported peak valuations differ between outlets — figures between roughly one point two and one point five billion dollars appear in different accounts of the same funding history, which is itself a lesson about private valuations. Nothing beyond that public reporting is asserted here. The structural point is simply that a collapse caused by what was reported inside the company is not evidence about the viability of the application layer. Do not use it as such.

## Two different kinds of casualty

Separate the companies that were disrupted from those that were absorbed: their financial signatures differ completely.

Chegg was disrupted. It was not built on a model API at all; it was a homework-help subscription business with a large content library, and free chat assistants attacked its demand rather than its cost base. The numbers, unusually for this chapter, come from a public company's own reported results. In the first quarter of 2025 subscribers fell about thirty-one per cent year on year to three point two million and revenue fell about thirty per cent to one hundred and twenty-one million US dollars. In May 2025 the company cut two hundred and forty-eight roles, about twenty-two per cent of staff; in late October 2025 it announced a further reduction of three hundred and eighty-eight roles, around forty-five per cent of the remaining workforce, as covered by Forbes and others at the time. Fourth-quarter revenue for 2025 was seventy-two point seven million dollars, down forty-nine per cent year on year, per the company's results announcement of 9 February 2026. Retrospectives published as the company shrank put its share price peak at about one hundred and thirteen dollars in February 2021, a market capitalisation then of roughly fourteen and a half billion dollars; companiesmarketcap put the figure at under a hundred and ten million in July 2026, a snapshot that moves daily.

Two mechanisms did that damage, and only one of them is about chatbots. The direct one is substitution: a student who can ask a free assistant does not buy a subscription. The indirect one is search. Chegg's customer acquisition depended on organic search traffic, and generative answers at the top of search results reduced the number of people who ever arrived at the page. The company's own disclosures and coverage through 2025 pointed at both. The second mechanism is the more important warning, because it applies whether or not a company itself uses AI: the channel through which customers used to arrive can be re-plumbed by someone else.

Character.AI and Windsurf were absorbed rather than disrupted — a different fate, with different accounting, and those deal structures come later in this chapter.

## The counter-argument: three assets you can own

Now the other side, which the wrapper critique consistently underrates. The critique treats the model as the only asset worth having. That is a category error. Ask what a customer actually buys, and the model turns out to be a component, not the product.

Three assets can be genuinely owned by a company that trains no models at all.

### Workflow

Workflow is the sequence of steps, approvals, formats and integrations by which work actually gets done in a real organisation, together with the customer's willingness to reorganise around your product. A general assistant can draft a contract clause. It cannot, on its own, sit inside a law firm's matter structure, apply the firm's precedent bank, produce output in the format the partner expects, log time against the matter, and satisfy the firm's records obligations. Each of those is unglamorous, specific and hard to copy from a keynote stage.

The evidence that this is worth something is in what capital paid for it. Harvey, which sells to law firms and corporate legal departments, raised two hundred million US dollars at an eleven billion dollar valuation in March 2026, co-led by GIC and Sequoia, as reported by CNBC that month — a round CNBC framed explicitly as venture capital spreading its bets beyond the model companies. Sierra, which builds customer-service agents, reached a ten billion dollar valuation in a three hundred and fifty million dollar round in September 2025 led by Greenoaks, per CNBC and Bloomberg at the time, then raised about nine hundred and fifty million dollars at a valuation above fifteen billion in May 2026, led by Tiger Global and GV, per CNBC and TechCrunch coverage of that round. Sierra passed one hundred million dollars of annual recurring revenue in November 2025, seven quarters after launch, on the company's own account and press coverage of it.

Sierra is worth a further paragraph because of how it charges. Rather than selling seats, it has been described in press coverage and by its own executives as pricing on outcomes — a fee reported at around one and a half US dollars per customer issue the agent actually resolves, and nothing when the agent hands the case to a human. Read that as an accountant. Outcome pricing moves the revenue line from headcount to work completed, which means revenue scales with usage in the same way cost of sales does; the gross margin is set by how efficiently the system resolves an issue relative to what the tokens cost. It is harder to model and easier to justify to a procurement department, because the customer buys a result rather than access. It also faces the uncomfortable truth that a seat-based licence for an agent that replaces seats is a contradiction.

### Data and the regulated corpus

The second ownable asset is a body of data, or a right to operate on data, that the model provider cannot obtain by scraping the public internet.

Ambient clinical documentation is the clearest example. Abridge turns a recorded consultation between a clinician and a patient into a structured draft note, inside a hospital's records system, under the privacy and record-keeping rules that govern clinical data. It raised three hundred million US dollars at a five point three billion dollar valuation in June 2025, in a round led by Andreessen Horowitz with Khosla Ventures, as reported by Fierce Healthcare and TechCrunch that month — roughly double the valuation of a financing only four months earlier. What is being valued there is not transcription. It is the accumulated corpus of real encounters, the integrations into hospital systems, and the compliance posture that lets a health system say yes. A general model provider can match the transcription. It cannot instantly match the ten-year integration relationship with the hospital's records vendor.

OpenEvidence shows the same principle with an unusual revenue model. The company raised two hundred and fifty million US dollars in January 2026 at a twelve billion dollar valuation, co-led by Thrive Capital and DST Global, roughly doubling its valuation from a financing three months earlier, as reported by STAT and Fierce Healthcare in January 2026. It is free to verified clinicians, and it is funded, on the company's own description reported in that coverage, by pharmaceutical and medical advertising in the manner of a medical journal.

The accounting reading is the interesting one. An advertising-funded application company has a cost of sales dominated by inference and a revenue line that does not scale with per-user willingness to pay. That works only if the audience is valuable enough per impression to cover the cost of the thinking. In medicine, where advertisers have historically paid extraordinary rates to reach prescribers, it can. In most consumer categories it cannot, which is why "we will monetise with ads later" is a much weaker plan for an AI product than it was for a social network. Ads pay per impression; inference costs per token.

### Distribution

The third asset is distribution — an installed base, a habit, a place in someone's daily tools.

Cursor is the example that even sceptics concede. Anysphere, its developer, raised nine hundred million US dollars at a nine point nine billion dollar valuation in June 2025, with TechCrunch reporting at the time that the product had passed five hundred million dollars of annualised revenue. In November 2025 it raised two point three billion dollars at a twenty-nine point three billion dollar valuation, co-led by Accel and Coatue, per CNBC on 13 November 2025 — and, notably, with Nvidia and Google among the strategic investors. Then, on 16 June 2026, SpaceX signed an all-stock merger agreement for the whole of Anysphere at sixty billion dollars, described by Forbes that day as the largest acquisition of a venture-backed startup on record; the transaction completed on 14 August 2026, with the company folded into a new artificial intelligence division, per Seeking Alpha and Yahoo Finance coverage of the closing. Forbes put annualised revenue at about four billion dollars by early June 2026, against one billion a year earlier, while other outlets carried different figures for the same period. Every one of those revenue numbers is an annualised private figure reported by journalists, not an audited annual result, and the distinction matters more than the size.

Glean, in enterprise search, raised one hundred and fifty million dollars at a seven point two billion dollar valuation in June 2025, per CNBC on 10 June 2025. Its product indexes a company's own documents, messages and tickets, with permissions preserved. That index is the asset. It cannot be shipped in a keynote by a model provider, because it is made of the customer's own material and the customer's own access rules.

Notice what the Cursor investor list implies. A model supplier and a chip supplier both took equity in an application company that is one of the largest buyers of their output. If your suppliers are also your shareholders, the risk that they ship your feature is not eliminated, but it is complicated in your favour.

## Four questions to ask of any application company

The three assets just described suggest a test. It is not a scoring system and it does not produce an answer; it produces the right conversation. Apply it to any application business, public or private.

| Question | What a weak answer looks like | What a durable answer looks like |
|---|---|---|
| What happens if the model provider ships this feature next month? | We are faster and the interface is nicer | Their version cannot reach our customer's data, permissions or system of record |
| What happens if the underlying model is swapped for a different vendor's? | Everything breaks; we are tuned to one provider | Little changes; the model is a replaceable component behind our own evaluation harness |
| What happens if inference prices fall by ninety per cent? | Our gross margin improves and we are fine | Our margin improves, and so does every potential entrant's — our defence was never the cost |
| What does the customer lose by leaving on Friday? | A browser tab | Configuration, history, integrations, an index of their own data, a trained process and an audit trail |

The second row deserves emphasis, because it is where engineering choices become financial ones. A company that has built its own evaluation harness — a repeatable way of measuring whether a swapped model does the job as well on that company's specific tasks — has converted the model into a commodity input it can shop for. A company that has not is structurally a reseller of one supplier.

The third row is the one most often got backwards. Falling input costs are not a moat. They are a tide, and they lift the entrants as well as the incumbent.

## When the exit is the outcome

A distinctive feature of this cycle is a deal structure that mostly did not exist before: the large company hires the team and licenses the technology, without acquiring the company.

Microsoft agreed in March 2024 to pay about six hundred and fifty million US dollars to license Inflection AI's technology and hire most of its staff, including co-founder Mustafa Suleyman, who became chief executive of Microsoft AI, per Bloomberg's reporting of the deal terms at the time. Google agreed a roughly two point seven billion dollar arrangement with Character.AI in August 2024, hiring its co-founders and part of its research team and taking a non-exclusive licence to its technology, with the corporate entity left standing; reporting subsequently noted that the structure drew attention from the United States Department of Justice. In July 2025 Google agreed to pay about two point four billion dollars to license technology from the coding startup Windsurf and hire its chief executive, a co-founder and part of its research team, per Reuters and CNBC at the time; days later Cognition announced it was acquiring the remaining company — product, intellectual property, brand and staff — on undisclosed terms, per CNBC on 14 July 2025.

Set aside why these structures are used, which involves competition review and is not this chapter's subject. Look at what they price.

In each case the buyer paid a large sum and did not take the company. It took the people and a licence. That is a revealed judgment about where the value sat: in the researchers and, to a degree, in the technology — not in the customer relationships, the revenue or the brand. In an ordinary software acquisition the acquirer wants the recurring revenue and the customer base, because that is the asset. Here the asset walked out of the building on two legs.

There is also a plain accounting difference for the buyer. An acquisition of a company brings the target's assets, liabilities and any goodwill on to the balance sheet, and consolidates its results. A licence plus a hiring wave produces an intangible asset and compensation expense, no consolidated subsidiary, and no minority interest. The economics can be similar; the presentation is not. When you read that a large company "paid billions for" a startup in this period, check which of the two things actually happened, because the accounts of the acquirer will look quite different depending on the answer.

For founders and staff the difference is sharper still: some investors and employees were paid out while the residual company continued with a diminished team, on terms that varied considerably between deals. "Acquired by a large technology company" is no longer a single event with a single meaning.

Set against those deals, the Cursor transaction is the exception that defines the rule. SpaceX did not hire the team and license the technology. Its June 2026 agreement took the entire company — product, customers, revenue, brand and staff. When a buyer takes the whole thing, it is paying for something it cannot recruit. That is the closest available market test of this chapter's argument. Where the value at the application layer sat in researchers, the buyer took the researchers. Where it sat in a product developers already open every morning, the buyer took the company.

## Reading a private application company's numbers

Nearly every figure in this chapter that concerns a private company came from a journalist, a private-market data provider or the company itself. None of it is audited. That is not a reason to ignore it, but it is a reason to read it with a specific set of questions in mind.

**Annualised is not annual.** "Annual recurring revenue" of two hundred million dollars usually means the most recent month multiplied by twelve. For a company growing quickly, that is far larger than the revenue it will report for the year; for one that has just stopped growing, it is far larger than what it will collect next year. The two are not interchangeable, and press coverage frequently treats them as if they were.

**Is usage-based revenue recurring?** Consumption revenue can fall as fast as it rose when a customer finishes a pilot or optimises its prompts. Counting it in a recurring revenue figure without disclosing the mix flatters the durability of the business.

**How is gross margin defined?** Ask whether inference cost sits in cost of sales or has been parked in research and development on the argument that it is model improvement. Ask whether free-tier and trial inference is included, and whether the company benefits from supplier credits — capacity provided at a discount by an investor or partner — because credits subsidise the margin rather than belonging to the business, and they run out.

**Concentration.** How much revenue comes from the largest handful of customers, and how much from other venture-funded companies? An application company selling mostly to other startups in the same cycle has a revenue base correlated with a single funding environment.

**Net revenue retention.** In the previous software era this was the single most informative number: what a cohort of customers spends this year against last, including expansion and churn. It remains the best available answer to "did they stay", and it is the number least often quoted in coverage of AI application companies, which should tell you something.

**Who is the customer's champion?** A tool bought by an individual with a credit card, on enthusiasm, has different retention from a tool procured by a department with a business case and an implementation project. The first grows faster. The second stays.

None of this is investment advice, and this book gives none. It cannot tell you what any of these companies is worth. It can tell you that the difference between the ones that lasted and the ones that did not was legible, at the time, in the answers to those six questions.

## What separates the survivors

Draw the strands together.

The companies that grew quickly and then struggled generally shared a profile. They sold a general capability in a specific costume. Their cost of sales was dominated by a supplier who could also be a competitor. Their customers had bought a habit rather than a system, and could stop in an afternoon. Their acquisition depended on a channel — search, social, a platform's app directory — that someone else could re-plumb without warning. And their differentiation was the part of the product that improves for everyone each time the underlying models improve, which is to say it was not differentiation at all.

The companies that lasted are not distinguished by having better models, because in most cases they have the same models. They are distinguished by owning something the model does not contain: a process a customer has reorganised around, a body of data the public internet does not hold, an index of the customer's own material, a compliance posture that took years to earn, a pricing structure tied to work completed rather than seats occupied. Those assets are boring. That is precisely why they survive a keynote.

Two honest caveats close the chapter. First, several of the durable-looking companies described here are young, privately valued at multiples of revenue that assume years of compounding growth, and untested through a downturn in funding conditions. That a company has an asset is not proof the price paid for it was reasonable, and this book will not tell you whether it was. Second, everything here is a snapshot. Valuations and revenue run rates carry the month they were reported for exactly that reason, and the sensible reader checks the current figure rather than trusting a printed one.

What is unlikely to change is the underlying arithmetic. At the application layer the product is assembled from a bought input, and the margin is whatever the customer's switching cost lets you keep. That is a hard business, and an old one. It is also the only layer of the four where the customer is a person deciding to pay — which makes it, for all its fragility, the layer on which every number in the rest of this book eventually depends.
