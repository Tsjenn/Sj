# Every Number in This Book Has a Date

On 27 January 2025, Nvidia lost about 589 billion US dollars of market value in a single trading session — the largest one-day loss of value by any company in market history, triggered by the release of a Chinese laboratory's model that raised questions about how much computing power the industry really required (CNBC and Bloomberg, 27 January 2025). Nine months later, on 29 October 2025, the same company became the first in history to close a trading day worth more than five trillion dollars (CNBC and Forbes, 29 October 2025).

Between those two dates, no fab moved. No machine was invented or uninvented. The number of employees barely changed. What changed was the price at which a small proportion of the shares happened to trade, and the confidence of the people setting it.

That is the problem this chapter is about. This industry re-prices faster than anyone can write about it, and a book is a physical object that stops changing the day it is printed. Most books solve this by quietly hoping the reader will not check. This one solves it by telling you, up front, exactly what kind of number you are being handed, when it was true, and where to go and find today's version yourself.

## The convention, stated once

Every figure in this book carries two things: the date it refers to, and the name of whoever published it. Not the date it was written, not "recently", not "as of this writing" — the actual date the figure describes.

So the book says: Nvidia was the world's most valuable company at roughly 5.3 to 5.4 trillion US dollars as at mid-August 2026 (companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings), with Apple at roughly 4.41 trillion, Alphabet roughly 4.20 trillion and Microsoft roughly 3.66 trillion over the same period and from the same sources. It does not say Nvidia "is worth five trillion dollars". The first version can be checked and, if wrong, corrected. The second version is a claim about a present that no longer exists by the time you read it.

Where a figure is unusually perishable, the book says so in the sentence itself. Where a figure is not disclosed, the book says it is not disclosed rather than reaching for an estimate. Where a number is invented to teach a mechanism, the book labels it as invented before it uses it, and never attaches it to a real company.

That is the whole convention. The rest of this chapter explains why it is necessary, what the different sorts of number actually are, and what you should do when — not if — a figure here goes stale.

## Four kinds of number, four kinds of time

The word "figure" hides at least four different objects, each true over a different span of time. Confusing them is the single most common error in commentary about this industry, and it is not a small error. It is the difference between "the company earned this" and "somebody paid this".

### A price is a moment

A share price is the price of the last trade. It is true for an instant. Market capitalisation is that instant multiplied by every share in issue, which means a single trade of a hundred shares can revalue a company by billions of dollars.

The scale of the movement is not theoretical. Nvidia added about 277 billion dollars of market value on 22 February 2024 — a record at the time — and then broke its own record with a gain of about 330 billion dollars on 31 July 2024 (Al Jazeera, August 2024, reporting on both sessions). It lost about 589 billion in the January 2025 session already mentioned. Each of those swings is larger than the entire market value of most companies in the world, and each occurred inside one working day.

The lesson is not that markets are irrational. It is that a market capitalisation is a claim about the future, priced continuously by people who keep changing their minds. It is not money the company holds, and it is not a measurement of anything that happened. Treat it as the most perishable number in the book, because it is.

### A quarter is a period

A revenue figure is different in kind. It is not a moment; it covers a stretch of time that has already ended, and it is the sum of thousands of transactions inside that stretch.

Nvidia reported revenue of 68.1 billion dollars for the quarter ended 25 January 2026, up 73 per cent on the same quarter a year earlier, and 215.9 billion dollars for the full fiscal year then ended, up 65 per cent (Nvidia results announcement, 25 February 2026). Those numbers do not move. They are not going to be different next week. They describe a period that is closed.

That stability is exactly why period figures are more useful for understanding a business than price figures, and it is why this book leans on them. It is also why the date matters in a different way: a revenue figure tells you about a window that has already shut, and by the time you read it the company is somewhere else entirely.

### A filing is a snapshot

A balance sheet is a photograph taken at one instant — the last day of the period — of things that were moving before and after the shutter opened. Inventory, cash, receivables and debt are all captured on that one date. A company can have a comfortable balance sheet on 31 December and an uncomfortable one on 15 January without anybody doing anything unusual.

Filings also arrive late, by design. In the United States, a large accelerated filer has sixty days after its financial year end to file an annual report on Form 10-K, forty days after a quarter end to file a Form 10-Q, and four business days to file a Form 8-K reporting a material event (SEC filing-deadline requirements; see any of the standard 2026 filing calendars published by US law firms). Nvidia's fourth quarter ended on 25 January 2026 and the results were announced on 25 February 2026 — a month between the end of the period and the public seeing the number, and that is fast.

So a filing is a snapshot, taken on a date already in the past, published on a later date, and read by you on a later date still. Three different dates, only one of which is usually quoted.

### A book is printed once

Which brings us to the awkward one. A book is fixed at the moment it goes to print, and then it sits on a shelf being read for years.

There is no way to fix this. There is only a choice about how to handle it honestly. The dishonest handling is to write numbers without dates so that the text reads as though it is describing the present, whenever the present happens to be. The honest handling is to date everything, so that a reader in three years' time can see instantly which parts of the book are history and which parts are still live — and can go and check the live parts.

| Kind of number | The time it belongs to | How fast it goes stale | Where to check the current version |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share price, market capitalisation | An instant | Seconds | Any market data service; the company's investor relations page |
| Revenue, profit, capex for a period | A closed period | Never — it is history | The company's own results release and filing |
| Balance-sheet items | One date | The moment the period ends | The latest annual or quarterly filing |
| Guidance and forecasts | A statement made on a date | Until the company changes it | The most recent results release |
| Private company valuation | The date of the last funding round | Until the next round, which may be years | Press reporting; the company's own announcement |
| Market share, industry size | An estimate for a period | Annually, and disputed meanwhile | The analyst house that published it, by name |

## The same asset, two profits

There is a deeper reason to distrust undated figures, and it belongs to the accountant rather than the market watcher. Even the audited numbers are not fixed facts. They rest on assumptions, and assumptions change.

The clearest example in this industry is the assumed useful life of a server. A data centre full of equipment is capitalised on the balance sheet and then charged against profit over the number of years the company expects to use it. Choose a longer life and the annual charge is smaller and reported profit is larger. Choose a shorter one and profit falls. Not a dollar of cash changes either way.

Amazon has disclosed both directions of that change within two years, in its own annual reports:

| Change effective | Useful life of servers | Disclosed effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 January 2024 | Increased from five years to six | Reduced depreciation and amortisation expense by 3.2 billion dollars and increased net income by 2.5 billion dollars for the year ended 31 December 2024 |
| 1 January 2025 | Reduced from six years back to five, for a subset of servers and networking equipment | Decreased 2025 operating income by roughly 0.7 billion dollars in respect of assets already held at 31 December 2024; total effect for the year an increase in depreciation and amortisation of 1.4 billion dollars and a reduction in net income of 1.0 billion dollars, mainly in the cloud segment |

Both sets of figures are Amazon's own, disclosed in its annual reports on Form 10-K, and the reason given for the 2025 reversal was the pace of technological change in artificial intelligence and machine learning.

Read those two rows together and the point becomes hard to miss. The same warehouses, the same racks, the same electricity — and profit moved by billions in one direction and then billions back, because the estimate of how long the equipment would remain useful was revised. Chapter four takes this apart properly, because it is the single most important piece of accounting in the cloud business. Here it serves a narrower purpose: to show that even a figure with an auditor's signature on it is a figure as at a date, prepared on assumptions held at that date.

If a company can restate the profitability of its own past by changing one estimate, no book should present a profit number as a permanent property of a business.

## A year is not always a year

A further trap, and one that catches careful readers as often as careless ones: the year in a company's results is not necessarily the year on your wall.

Nvidia runs a fiscal year of fifty-two or fifty-three weeks ending on the last Sunday in January. Its fiscal 2026 ended on 25 January 2026, which means "fiscal 2026" is, in ordinary calendar terms, almost entirely 2025. The 215.9 billion dollars of revenue reported for fiscal 2026 (Nvidia results announcement, 25 February 2026) was earned in the calendar months from late January 2025 to late January 2026.

So when one source says a company grew sixty-five per cent "in 2026" and another says the industry grew by some quite different figure "in 2025", they may be describing overlapping periods, non-overlapping periods, or the same period under two names. Comparing them without checking the year-ends is comparing nothing at all.

This book always states the period end where a fiscal year is involved. When you check a figure elsewhere, do the same. The question "which twelve months is this?" is not pedantry; it is the first question.

## A currency is a date too

Translation adds another timestamp that most reporting drops.

TSMC reports in New Taiwan dollars. Its July 2026 consolidated revenue was NT$467.58 billion, up 44.7 per cent on July 2025, announced on 10 August 2026 under the Taiwanese disclosure rules that require listed companies to publish monthly revenue by around the tenth of the following month (TSMC monthly revenue report, 10 August 2026). The same figure was reported in the trade press as roughly 14.5 billion US dollars (Digitimes, 10 August 2026).

The second figure is the first figure multiplied by an exchange rate. Which exchange rate — the rate on the last day of the month, the average rate for the month, the rate on the day the article was written — is usually not stated, and the three are not the same. A dollar figure for a non-dollar company therefore carries two dates: the period of the revenue, and the moment of the translation. When comparing a Taiwanese foundry with an American designer and a Dutch equipment maker, part of any apparent difference in growth is currency movement rather than business performance.

The monthly disclosure rule is also, incidentally, a gift to anyone trying to follow this industry. Most of the world's large companies report four times a year. Taiwan's listed manufacturers report revenue twelve times a year, which makes the Taiwanese foundry and its neighbours among the most closely observable companies in the supply chain. Where a book can point you at a source that updates monthly rather than annually, it should, and this one does.

## The number with the longest shelf life and the least support

Private valuations are the worst case: the figure moves least often and means least.

Anthropic closed a 65 billion dollar Series H at a 965 billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly making it the most valuable private company in the world (CNBC, May 2026). OpenAI closed a 122 billion dollar round in March 2026 at an 852 billion dollar valuation, with Amazon reported as investing 50 billion dollars in that round (reporting collated by valueaddvc and Forbes during 2026).

Those are real, reported, dated figures and this book uses them. But understand what they are. A post-money valuation is the price of one class of shares, sold on one day, on terms that are not published, multiplied by every share as though all shares were the same. They are frequently not the same: later investors often hold preferences that pay them out first, and sometimes protections that adjust their stake if the next round is priced lower. A public company's market capitalisation is at least a price that anyone can obtain by trading. A private valuation is a price that a specific set of investors agreed once, in a negotiation you cannot read.

And it does not move. It sits at the round price until the next round, which may be a year or two away, while the business underneath it changes completely. A public market re-prices a company thousands of times a day, sometimes violently and often wrongly, but it re-prices. A private valuation is a photograph that stays on the wall.

There is one further complication, and it is a live one. Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic have been reported as distorting the earnings picture of the companies that hold them (CNBC, August 2026) — because a gain on a stake in a private company that has just raised at a higher price can flow through a public company's accounts as profit, without any cash arriving. Chapter five sets out how to read these companies properly. The rule this book applies throughout is simple: report what was disclosed, name who disclosed it, give the date, and never present it as equivalent to an audited filing.

## An illustrative case: the run-rate

Some numbers are not stale so much as manufactured out of a single moment. The most common in this industry is the annualised revenue run-rate, and it is worth taking apart with invented numbers.

Take an illustrative model laboratory, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. In June it bills subscription and interface customers 40 million dollars. Multiply by twelve and the company has a "480 million dollar annual run-rate". By September the monthly figure is 60 million and the run-rate is "720 million". Nobody has lied. But consider what has been asserted:

| What was actually measured | What the run-rate implies |
|---|---|
| One month of billings | Twelve months of revenue |
| Revenue billed, not necessarily earned | Revenue recognisable over the contract |
| Revenue before refunds, credits and churn | Revenue that will all be collected |
| A month that may include a one-off enterprise contract | A repeating monthly base |
| No cost of serving it | Nothing about profit at all |

In a business whose costs of serving each customer are substantial — and inference is a genuine cost, not a rounding error — a run-rate says nothing whatever about whether the company makes money. It is a growth signal dressed as a financial statement, and it is the number most often quoted about the model layer, precisely because those companies publish nothing that would let you check it.

This book will quote run-rates where a named source has reported one, with the date and the source, and will say plainly that they are run-rates. It will not annualise a number itself, and it will not compare an unaudited run-rate with an audited revenue figure as though the two were the same species. Again: illustrative figures above, invented for teaching, belonging to no company.

## The most reliable numbers are the oldest ones

There is a pleasant irony in all of this. The figures least likely to embarrass a book are the ones furthest in the past, because a completed transaction is finished and cannot be revised by sentiment.

SoftBank's Vision Fund built a stake of roughly 4.9 per cent in Nvidia during 2017 and sold it in January and February 2019 for about 3.3 billion dollars, a profit of roughly 2.6 billion (CNBC, February 2019). In November 2025 SoftBank sold its entire remaining holding in the company for 5.83 billion US dollars, to fund investment in OpenAI and the Stargate data-centre programme (CNBC, November 2025). Neither figure will ever change. They are historical prices paid in historical transactions, reported at the time by a named organisation, and they will read exactly the same in a decade.

They also carry more information than a current market capitalisation does, because they show a decision being made under uncertainty by people who did not know what came next. The 2019 sale looks a particular way with hindsight; at the time it was a large realised gain on a successful holding, which is generally considered good practice. That is what a dated historical figure gives you and a live one cannot: a decision separated from its outcome.

One correction belongs here, because it travels widely and is wrong. Nvidia's early backers, when the company was founded in 1993, were Sequoia Capital and Sutter Hill Ventures — not SoftBank, which acquired Arm in 2016 and returned it to public markets in 2023. Popular accounts confuse the two. A figure with a date attached is easy to check; an origin story repeated without one is how errors become common knowledge.

## What does not go stale

If the numbers move this much, why write the book at all?

Because the numbers are the least durable part of what there is to know, and the industry's structure is the most durable part. Consider what has not changed while the market capitalisations were doubling and halving:

- One company still makes the machines that write the finest patterns onto silicon, and there is no second source. ASML crossed 500 billion US dollars in market value for the first time in January 2026 (Reuters and Yahoo Finance) — that figure will have moved. The absence of a competitor is a fact about physics, optics and two decades of accumulated process knowledge, and it will not move by Friday.
- A leading-edge fab still has to be committed years before the revenue arrives. TSMC guided 2026 capital expenditure to 52 to 56 billion US dollars, above analyst expectations (company results, January 2026). The specific number will change with every guidance update. The structural fact — that capacity three years out is being decided now, on a forecast that may be wrong — is permanent.
- Software written for one company's parallel computing platform still has to be rewritten to run well elsewhere. That switching cost accumulated over roughly a decade and is not undone by a quarter.
- Memory is still a cyclical, capacity-constrained business where supply is booked far ahead. The 2026 high-bandwidth memory market was estimated at about 54.6 billion US dollars, up roughly 58 per cent year on year, with capacity reported sold out through 2026 across major suppliers and SK Hynix holding the largest share (industry analysis, 2026). The size estimate will be revised. The cycle will not stop being a cycle.

Structure, economics, incentives and history are the durable material. Why a chokepoint earns more than a market share; why a foundry must run its fabs full; why depreciation assumptions decide reported cloud margins; why a company that both supplies and invests in the same customer deserves a closer reading; why capital keeps arriving at the bottom of the stack. Those explanations were true in 2019 and will be true in 2032, and they are what the rest of this book is for.

The figures are there to make the explanations concrete. They are illustration, not foundation. A reader who finishes this book knowing that Nvidia was worth roughly 5.3 trillion dollars in August 2026 has learned a piece of trivia with a short life. A reader who finishes it able to open a filing and work out for themselves what the gross margin implies about pricing power has learned something that keeps working.

| The volatile layer | The durable layer |
|---|---|
| Market capitalisation and rankings | Why a chokepoint is worth more than a share of a market |
| Quarterly revenue and growth rates | How a foundry's cost per wafer falls as yield climbs |
| Capex guidance for a given year | Why capacity must be committed years before demand is known |
| A private company's latest round | Why a post-money valuation is not a measure of worth |
| This year's memory prices | Why memory is cyclical and capacity is booked ahead |
| Who leads on a benchmark this month | Why software switching costs outlast hardware advantages |

## What to do when a figure here is out of date

Assume, from the first page, that it will be. Here is the procedure.

**Establish which kind of number it is.** Price, period, snapshot, guidance, estimate or private round. The kind determines how quickly it decays and where its replacement lives.

**Go to the primary source, not to a summary of it.** For a US-listed company, the annual report on Form 10-K and the quarterly report on Form 10-Q are filed publicly with the Securities and Exchange Commission and are searchable in its public filing database; foreign issuers file a Form 20-F or furnish results on Form 6-K. For a Taiwanese-listed manufacturer, the monthly revenue announcement and the quarterly results are on the company's own investor relations pages and in its regulatory filings. These documents are free, and they are where the numbers that everyone else quotes actually come from.

**Check the period end, not the year label.** A "2026" annual report may cover a period ending in January, March, June, September or December.

**Note the currency and, if it has been translated, the fact that a rate was applied.** If the source does not say which rate, treat the translated figure as approximate.

**For anything not filed — private valuations, market sizes, market shares, headcounts — find the name of the organisation that published it and the date it published.** An industry estimate without a named house behind it is a rumour with a decimal point. Two houses will often give materially different numbers for the same market, because they define the market differently. That is not dishonesty; it is why the definition matters more than the total.

**Where two sources disagree, prefer the one closest to the company's own disclosure, and say that they disagree.** This book does that where it happens.

None of this requires a subscription, a terminal or a professional qualification. It requires knowing which document to open, which is a skill this book's early chapters exist to teach.

## Two things this book will not do

It will not predict a price. There are no share price targets in these pages, no recommendations to buy or sell anything, and no forecasts of what any company will be worth. This book is not investment advice, and the reason is consistent with the rest of this chapter: a prediction is a figure with a date in the future and no source at all. If a number cannot be checked, it does not belong here, and a number about next year cannot be checked by anyone.

It will not close the gap between what is disclosed and what would be interesting. Some of the most important numbers in this industry are simply not published. How much of a given accelerator maker's revenue comes from its four largest customers is disclosed in outline and not in detail. What a hyperscaler actually pays per chip is not disclosed. What it costs to serve one request from a frontier model is not disclosed by anyone. Where that is the case, this book says so and stops, rather than filling the space with a plausible figure. A book that guesses in the gaps is more comfortable to read and worth considerably less.

## The rule, restated

Every number here is a measurement taken at a moment, published by somebody, and already ageing when it reached the page. Dating it and naming its source costs a few words per sentence and buys the only thing that matters: you can check it, and you can tell when it has expired.

That is not a limitation of this book. It is a description of the subject. An industry that can add or remove half a trillion dollars of market value in an afternoon, and that revises its own reported profits when it changes its mind about how long a server lasts, cannot be captured in a fixed number. It can be explained. The explanations are durable, the numbers are not, and this book keeps the two clearly apart so that when the numbers go — and they will — the explanation still stands.
