# Apple: The Quiet One

On 30 July 2026, Apple reported its strongest June quarter on record: revenue of one hundred and nine point four billion US dollars, up sixteen per cent year on year, net profit of twenty-nine point eight billion, and a company gross margin of fifty point one per cent — which included roughly two percentage points of benefit from tariff refunds — with services revenue of thirty point seven four billion, itself a June-quarter record, per the company's third-quarter fiscal 2026 results as covered by MacRumors, TechPowerUp and earnings coverage that day. In the same twelve months, four of Apple's peers were between them committing something in the order of seven hundred billion dollars to capital expenditure.

Apple was not among them. That is the chapter.

For most of the past decade Apple was the most valuable company in the world, and for most of the past three years it has been the least discussed company in the industry this book is about. Its market capitalisation stood at roughly four point four one trillion US dollars in mid-August 2026, per companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings — second in the world, behind Nvidia's roughly five point three to five point four trillion over the same period from the same class of sources, and both figures move daily. A company of that size cannot be described as struggling. But it can be described as absent, and for two years the market treated absence as a defect. As at 17 September 2025, Apple was down about five per cent for the year to date while Alphabet, Nvidia and Meta had returned between twenty-eight and thirty-three per cent and Microsoft more than twenty, per FinancialContent's MarketMinute coverage that day. Apple was the laggard in a cohort defined by AI enthusiasm, and the reason given was always the same: it had nothing to show.

This chapter argues that the absence is a position, not an oversight — and then argues that the position carries a real and under-examined risk, which surfaced in 2026 in a form that can be read directly off two announcements.

## The company that did not build a data centre

Begin with the number that separates Apple from every other company in this part of the book.

For fiscal 2026, the four largest infrastructure buyers guided to capital expenditure of roughly seven hundred and twenty-five billion US dollars between them, up from about four hundred and ten billion the prior year — Amazon around two hundred billion, Microsoft near one hundred and ninety, Alphabet in a one hundred and seventy-five to two hundred and five billion range, Meta at one hundred and twenty-five to one hundred and forty-five — per the 2026 compilations published by valueaddvc and CNBC's February 2026 coverage of the same guidance. Fast Company's 2026 ranking of big-technology capital spending observed that Apple's full-year capital expenditure amounted to about two point two per cent of the hyperscaler total.

Apple's own figure is small enough to be easy to miss. Macrotrends' compilation of Apple's fiscal 2025 cash flow statement puts payments for acquisition of property, plant and equipment at about twelve point seven billion US dollars for the year, against research and development expense of thirty-four point five five billion, up ten per cent from thirty-one point three seven billion the year before. Read those two lines together and the shape of the company appears: Apple spends nearly three times as much on engineers as on buildings and machines.

That is not a recent economy. It is what Apple has always been. Apple does not own the factories that assemble its products; contract manufacturers do. It does not own the fabs that make its chips; TSMC does. It does not rent out compute; it has no cloud business to speak of, and the storage service it does sell runs partly on infrastructure it buys from others. Apple's historic genius has been to design the two ends of a product — the silicon and the experience — and to let other people's balance sheets carry the middle.

| What the capital buys | Data centres, accelerators, power | Product development, tooling, retail, some servers |
| Who bears the depreciation | The company | Largely the supply chain |
| Where inference is paid for | Cost of revenue, per token served | Largely the customer's own device |
| What the customer buys | Capacity or a subscription | A device, then a services attach |

The right way to read the difference is not that Apple is being careful with money. It is that Apple has designed a business in which the expensive part of artificial intelligence — running the model, over and over, for hundreds of millions of people — is paid for by hardware the customer has already bought and already owns. Every other company in this part of the book must buy the machine, power it, cool it, depreciate it, and then hope the revenue arrives. Apple sells the machine.

## Where the money actually comes from

Before assessing the strategy, establish the accounts, because they explain what Apple is actually defending.

For fiscal year 2025, ended 27 September 2025, Apple recorded sales of four hundred and sixteen point two billion US dollars and net income of one hundred and twelve billion, against three hundred and ninety-one billion and ninety-three point seven billion in fiscal 2024 — both all-time company records, per MacRumors' coverage of the fourth-quarter results on 30 October 2025, which put the September quarter alone at one hundred and two point five billion of revenue and twenty-seven point five billion of profit. Services revenue passed one hundred billion dollars for the first time in a fiscal year, at one hundred and nine point one six billion, and coverage of the same results put the services gross margin at seventy-five point four per cent against a products margin of thirty-six point eight per cent.

Those two margin figures are the most important numbers in the chapter, so sit with them.

Apple's hardware business earns a little over a third on each dollar of sales. Its services business — the App Store, iCloud, Apple Music, advertising, payment fees, and the search placement money discussed below — earns three-quarters. Services is roughly a quarter of revenue and a far larger share of profit. And services revenue is a function of one thing: how many Apple devices are in active use, and how deeply their owners are inside the ecosystem. Apple said it had passed two point five billion active devices as at January 2026, up from more than two point three five billion a year earlier, per the company's first-quarter results as reported by AppleInsider and 9to5Mac on 29 January 2026.

So the structure is this. Apple sells a device at a good margin. The device creates an installed base. The installed base generates services revenue at an exceptional margin. Anything that increases the number of devices, the price of devices, or the intensity of ecosystem use, increases the profit twice over. Anything that weakens the tie between the person and the device weakens both halves at once.

Hold that sentence. The whole risk section of this chapter is contained in it.

## Arriving late on purpose

Apple has a long-standing pattern that is easy to describe and hard to imitate: it is rarely first, frequently late, and occasionally decisive.

It did not invent the portable music player, the smartphone, the tablet, the smartwatch or the wireless earphone. In each case a category existed, was served badly, and was then entered by Apple with a product that integrated hardware, silicon and software tightly enough that the earlier products looked like prototypes. The pattern has a cost — Apple regularly cedes several years of a new market and absorbs the reputational damage of being called complacent — and a benefit, which is that when it does arrive it arrives with distribution, a supply chain and a business model already attached.

In this cycle the lateness has been unusually visible and unusually painful.

Apple announced Apple Intelligence at its developer conference in June 2024 and shipped the first features that autumn. Among the features demonstrated was a substantially more capable Siri, able to act inside applications and draw on personal context. It was promoted in advertising around the iPhone 16 launch in September 2024. On 7 March 2025 Apple said the personalised Siri features would take longer than expected and would arrive "in the coming year", as reported by CNBC that day. In June 2025 the timeline was described as spring 2026, per MacRumors. By December 2025, CNBC was writing that Apple had punted on AI for the year and that the following year would be critical.

There is no charitable reading in which that was the plan. A company does not advertise a feature and then withdraw it for eighteen months by design. What can be said fairly is that the delay was a failure of execution inside a strategy that was nonetheless coherent, and the strategy is worth stating in its own terms rather than in its critics'.

The strategy has three parts. First, most of what an ordinary person wants from an assistant on a phone does not require a frontier model. Second, the parts that do require a frontier model are better bought than built, because the frontier is being commoditised by competition that Apple is not paying for. Third, the thing Apple must control is the interface, the silicon and the privacy guarantee — because those, not the model weights, are what make the device worth its price.

That is a defensible thesis. Whether it survives contact with 2026 is the second half of this chapter.

## The on-device argument

The technical expression of the thesis is a small model that runs on the phone.

At its 2025 developer conference Apple opened its on-device foundation model to third-party developers through a Foundation Models framework. The model is described in Apple's own machine-learning research publications as having about three billion parameters, quantised to two bits per parameter, with architectural work — key-value cache sharing, quantisation-aware training — aimed squarely at making it run inside the memory and power budget of a handset. Apple's published description is candid about the limits: the on-device model is built for summarisation, extraction, classification and similar work, and is not designed for broad world knowledge or advanced reasoning.

Set the accounting consequence beside the technical one, because that is where this book earns its keep.

When a model runs in a data centre, every query has a marginal cost, and that cost lands in cost of revenue. Chapter one traced the shape of it: for the frontier labs, reported figures put compute at more than half of each revenue dollar. Gross margin at the model layer is therefore a direct function of inference efficiency, and every additional user makes the cost line grow.

When a model runs on the customer's phone, the marginal cost to the seller is zero. The silicon was paid for by the customer at purchase, at a thirty-six point eight per cent gross margin to Apple. The electricity is the customer's. The depreciation is the customer's — indeed, the faster the customer's device becomes inadequate, the sooner Apple sells another one. Nothing lands in cost of revenue at all.

Take an illustrative company, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real business. Suppose it serves one hundred million people, each making twenty assistant requests a day, and suppose each request served from a data centre costs one tenth of a US cent in compute. That is two million dollars a day, or roughly seven hundred and thirty million dollars a year, of pure cost of revenue that grows with every new user and every new feature that makes the assistant more useful. Move the same workload onto devices the customers already own and that entire line disappears from the income statement — replaced by a one-off design and validation cost, and by the requirement that the hardware be good enough to carry it. The invented figures above are chosen to be arithmetically simple; the point is not their size but their behaviour. One cost scales with usage. The other does not.

This is the strongest form of Apple's argument, and it is genuinely strong. It also explains why the privacy claim and the economic claim are the same claim wearing different clothes. Apple says data stays on the device because that is what customers should want; it is also true that data staying on the device is what keeps the cost line flat.

Where on-device is not enough, Apple built Private Cloud Compute — announced in 2024, a server tier running on custom hardware built around Apple silicon, with the Secure Enclave and secure boot brought into the data centre, running a stripped-down operating system, engineered so that servers are stateless and their behaviour is cryptographically verifiable, per Apple's own security research publications. Requests go up, are answered, and nothing is retained. It is a serious piece of engineering and it was, for two years, the clearest evidence that Apple intended to own its intelligence stack from transistor to interface.

## The silicon capability

Apple's claim to that stack rests on the one thing nobody disputes: it is among the best chip designers in the world, and has been since it began designing its own processors.

Every current Apple product line runs on silicon Apple designed — the A-series in phones, the M-series in computers, with dedicated neural accelerators alongside the CPU and GPU. The M5, introduced in 2025, is built on TSMC's third-generation three-nanometre process and pairs a sixteen-core Neural Engine with a GPU whose cores carry their own neural accelerators, per Apple's product materials and semiconductor trade coverage in 2025 and 2026. Trade reporting in 2026 has Apple securing more than half of TSMC's initial two-nanometre output for its forthcoming A20 and M6 generations, with mass production expected to commence in 2026.

That last fact deserves emphasis in a book organised on layers. TSMC is the chokepoint the whole industry runs through, and the customer with first claim on its newest capacity is not the company selling accelerators. It is the company selling phones. Apple has been TSMC's largest customer for years, and its willingness to pay for and absorb an entire new node's early output is the reason TSMC can afford to build it. Nvidia's chips are the ones the market talks about; Apple's volumes are the ones that finance the leading edge.

Apple has also been buying its way further down the stack in public. In August 2025 it raised its stated United States investment commitment to six hundred billion dollars over four years and announced an American Manufacturing Program with ten named partners including TSMC, Broadcom, Texas Instruments, GlobalFoundries, Amkor, Applied Materials, Samsung and Corning, per the company's announcement of 6 August 2025 and contemporaneous coverage; four further partners were added in March 2026, per CNBC. Note what that money is and is not. It is a commitment to a domestic supply chain — glass, wafers, packaging, chips — not a commitment to build data centres. Apple's largest publicly stated capital promise of the cycle is a promise about manufacturing, not about compute.

So Apple entered 2026 with the deepest silicon capability of any application-layer company, first call on the world's most advanced manufacturing, an installed base of two and a half billion devices, and a plausible technical architecture for delivering intelligence without buying accelerators by the hundred thousand.

And then it paid Google.

## The billion-dollar admission

In November 2025, 9to5Mac reported that Apple was nearing an agreement to pay Google roughly one billion US dollars a year for a custom Gemini model to power Siri. On 12 January 2026 CNBC reported the choice as made: Google's Gemini would run the AI-powered Siri. At its developer conference on 8 June 2026, Apple confirmed it publicly — a rebuilt Siri and the wider Apple Intelligence experience running on a custom Gemini model reported at about one point two trillion parameters, against roughly one hundred and fifty billion for the model previously behind Apple Intelligence, under a multi-year arrangement reported at around one billion dollars a year. Reporting on the selection process, including 9to5Mac's, said Google won a comparison against Anthropic, and that price rather than model quality decided it.

Then, on the same day, a second announcement that received a fraction of the attention and matters more. Apple said it would run new Apple Intelligence workloads on Google Cloud, extending Private Cloud Compute's privacy guarantees to third-party data centres for the first time. The published description of the expanded architecture is explicit that this tier does not run on Apple silicon: it uses Nvidia GPUs in confidential-computing mode, Intel processors with trusted-domain extensions, and a Google security chip, per Apple's security research publication and coverage of it on 8 June 2026.

Read the two announcements together and the position is unambiguous. As at mid-2026, the intelligence in Apple's assistant is designed by Google, and a growing share of it runs on hardware Apple neither designed nor owns, in buildings Apple neither built nor operates.

It is important to be precise about what this is and is not. It is not evidence of failure — buying a capability at a good price is a legitimate decision, and one billion dollars a year against one hundred and twelve billion of annual net income is, in cash terms, trivially affordable. It is not unusual either; Apple has bought maps, modems, search and voice recognition from outside before, sometimes for years. And the on-device tier remains Apple's own, as does the interface, as does the silicon in the customer's hand.

But it is a change in kind, and the accountant's instinct is to ask what has been converted into what. Apple has converted a capability it might have owned into an operating expense it now rents, from a company that competes with it at the application layer, sells the cloud capacity underneath it, and is simultaneously its largest single source of high-margin services revenue. That last relationship is the one to look at next.

## The risk: distribution for someone else's intelligence

The brief for this chapter calls this the genuine risk, and it is worth stating carefully, because it is easy to state hysterically and thereby get it wrong.

Apple's business rests on the device being the place where computing happens. The device is the interface, the identity, the payment method, the photo library, the store, the point at which a customer is captured and monetised. Everything downstream — the seventy-five per cent services margin, the two and a half billion active devices, the App Store commission — depends on the phone being the layer that matters.

An assistant that is genuinely good does something specific to that arrangement: it moves the interface up a level. If the thing a person addresses is a conversational model, and the model can search, book, buy, summarise and act, then the applications underneath become interchangeable plumbing, and so, eventually, does the hardware that hosts them. The device becomes a screen and a microphone attached to somebody else's intelligence. That is what "distribution channel" means in this context. It does not mean Apple stops selling phones. It means the profit pool migrates from the layer Apple controls to the layer Apple rents.

There is already a precise, audited-adjacent illustration of what it costs Apple to be a distribution channel — and, awkwardly, of how lucrative it can be in the short run. Court documents in the United States search antitrust case showed Google paying Apple about twenty billion dollars in 2022 to be the default search engine in Safari, as reported in 2024 by Bloomberg and widely covered. On 2 September 2025, the remedies ruling permitted Google to continue paying for placement so long as the arrangements are not exclusive, and Apple shares rose more than three per cent on the decision, per CNBC that day.

Consider what that episode reveals. Roughly twenty billion dollars a year of Apple's very highest-margin revenue — a payment with essentially no cost of goods attached — comes from renting out the front door of the device to a company that owns the service behind it. For two decades that was an outstanding trade for Apple: it collected the toll and Google collected the search business. It also means Apple has already demonstrated, at scale, its willingness to be paid for distribution rather than to own the underlying service. The Gemini arrangement runs the same trade in the opposite direction, and for a much smaller sum in the other column.

|---|---|
| The device and its silicon | Apple |
| The interface and operating system | Apple |
| The small on-device model | Apple |
| The privacy architecture | Apple, extended by contract to partners |
| The frontier model behind Siri | Google, under a reported one-billion-dollar-a-year arrangement |
| The data centres for new Apple Intelligence workloads | Google, on Nvidia hardware |
| Default search in Safari | Google, paying Apple |

Look down the right-hand column and the concentration is striking. One counterparty appears four times, twice as Apple's supplier and twice as Apple's payer. This book does not allege anything improper in that; there is nothing improper in it, and both companies have every commercial reason to keep the arrangements running. But concentration is a risk whether or not anyone behaves badly, and any auditor looking at a customer or supplier appearing four times in a critical dependency map would raise it. If the search payments were ever curtailed by a court, and the model arrangement repriced, and the cloud dependency deepened, they would not be three separate events. They would be one counterparty's position changing.

The second risk is subtler and does not involve Google at all. Apple's argument for on-device inference is strongest when models are small enough to fit and good enough to satisfy. If the useful frontier keeps moving faster than the memory and power budget of a handset, then the share of requests that must go to a data centre rises every year, and Apple's structural cost advantage erodes from the top. It does not vanish — the routine requests stay local — but the interesting ones leave, and the interesting ones are the ones customers judge the product by. Apple's capital-light position is not a permanent property of its business model. It is a bet on where the useful frontier settles.

## What to watch in the accounts

Because Apple discloses no artificial-intelligence segment — nobody does — the honest approach is to name the lines where the answer will eventually appear, and then check them each quarter.

Watch the capital expenditure line first. Apple's roughly twelve point seven billion dollars in fiscal 2025 is the signature of a company that intends to keep inference off its own balance sheet. If that figure begins climbing towards its peers', the strategy has changed, whatever the language on the call says. Cash speaks before management does.

Watch services revenue and its growth rate. Services was one hundred and nine point one six billion in fiscal 2025 and thirty point seven four billion in the June 2026 quarter, up twelve point one per cent. It is the profit engine and the exposure at once: it contains the search payment, the App Store commission and the subscription businesses, each of which faces its own regulatory or competitive pressure. Growth here is the clearest single measure of whether the ecosystem is tightening or loosening.

Watch the products gross margin, at thirty-six point eight per cent in fiscal 2025. On-device intelligence requires more silicon, more memory and more power management in every unit. If Apple is absorbing that cost rather than pricing it, the margin tells you before anything else does.

Watch research and development against capital expenditure. Thirty-four point five five billion against twelve point seven billion in fiscal 2025 is the ratio of a design company. Its direction of travel describes the strategy more honestly than any keynote.

And watch the disclosures around the Google relationship. The payments in both directions are contractual and mostly invisible in the published accounts; what surfaces is litigation, regulatory findings and the occasional court exhibit. The reader who wants the current numbers should go to the company's quarterly results and the most recent court filings rather than to any book, including this one. Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot with a date attached, and several of them will have moved by the time it is read.

## Challenge and opportunity

The challenge, stated plainly: Apple has the largest and most valuable customer relationship in consumer technology, and has chosen to fill the most important new part of that relationship with a competitor's product. Its execution on its own assistant slipped by roughly two years between the June 2024 demonstration and the June 2026 rebuild. Its capital-light position depends on an assumption about model size that it does not control. Its highest-margin revenue line is concentrated in services, and within services on a payment from the same counterparty that now supplies its intelligence. And it enters this period under new leadership: Tim Cook becomes executive chairman and John Ternus becomes chief executive with effect from 1 September 2026, announced in April 2026 — a hardware engineer taking over a company whose central question has become a software one.

The opportunity is the mirror image and it is large. Two and a half billion active devices is a distribution position no model laboratory can buy, and Apple can put a new capability in front of that base in a single software update at zero marginal distribution cost. Its silicon organisation is world-class and has first call on the most advanced manufacturing capacity in existence. Its cost structure means intelligence delivered on-device drops almost entirely to margin rather than being consumed by inference. And if frontier models do commoditise — if the difference between the best and the fourth-best becomes invisible in ordinary use — then the company that owns the device, the interface and the customer relationship, and rents the model from whoever is cheapest that year, will look extremely well positioned. Renting from a rival is a weakness only while the rival's product is scarce.

In the frame this book uses, Apple is the clearest test of the central argument. Value has been moving down the stack, away from applications and towards compute and silicon. Apple sits at the top of the stack and pays down it — to TSMC, to Google, to the memory makers — and yet retains a higher margin than almost anyone it pays, because what it sells is not intelligence but the object through which intelligence reaches a person. Either that object remains the scarce thing, in which case the quiet strategy will look like discipline. Or the intelligence becomes the scarce thing, in which case the most valuable company of the last decade will have spent this one collecting a toll on somebody else's road.

Nothing here is a view on the shares, and this book does not offer one. The verdict worth having is the habit: open the results, put capital expenditure beside research and development, put services growth beside products margin, and ask who is paying whom for what. Apple's accounts have always answered that question more honestly than its keynotes. They will answer this one too.
