# The Semiconductor Cycle

In December 2025, the World Semiconductor Trade Statistics organisation — the industry's own statistical body, whose numbers come from the companies themselves — forecast that the global semiconductor market would grow by more than twenty-five per cent in 2026, to about nine hundred and seventy-five billion US dollars. Six months later, in a revised forecast reported by Digitimes and the semiconductor trade press in June 2026, it put the same year at roughly one and a half trillion dollars: growth of about ninety per cent, with memory alone forecast to rise around two hundred and fifty per cent to more than eight hundred billion dollars, and logic up thirty-seven per cent.

Same industry. Same year. Same forecasters, working from the same members' data. Half a trillion dollars of difference, six months apart.

There is nothing improper about that. It is what happens when an industry's output is decided years in advance and its demand is decided in the current quarter. But hold on to the size of the revision, because it is the cleanest available evidence for the argument of this chapter: the people best placed on earth to know what this industry will sell next year, with privileged access to the order books, were out by roughly the whole of a recent year's revenue — and they were out in six months, in the direction of understatement. In other years they have been wrong by comparable margins in the other direction.

That is not incompetence. It is the structure. This chapter explains what the structure is, why it produces the same loop no matter who is running the companies, why the new capacity is always late, and why an industry that has understood its own cycle perfectly for fifty years has never once managed to step out of it. The chapter's purpose is defensive. A reader who finishes it should be harder to sell both the story that this time the cycle has been abolished and the story that a collapse is due on a timetable.

## The record

Start with the shape of the thing, because the shape is not in dispute.

Worldwide semiconductor sales rose from one hundred and thirty-nine billion US dollars in 2001 to six hundred and thirty and a half billion in 2024, a compound growth rate of about six point eight per cent a year, per the Semiconductor Industry Association's 2025 factbook. In 2025 the figure was seven hundred and ninety-one point seven billion, up twenty-five point six per cent, per the association's annual release. Over a quarter of a century, this has been one of the great growth industries of the world economy.

Now the same period, read as a path rather than a trend.

| Year | What happened to global semiconductor sales | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 2000 | Record year, sales of about 204 billion dollars | SIA, reported by EE Times |
| 2001 | Fell about 32 per cent to 139 billion — the sharpest annual decline in the industry's history to that date | SIA, reported by EE Times, 2002 |
| 2008 | Fell 2.8 per cent | SIA, 2009 |
| 2009 | Fell about 9 per cent, from 248.6 billion to 226.3 billion | SIA, 2010 |
| 2019 | Fell 12.1 per cent to 412.1 billion | SIA, February 2020 |
| 2023 | Fell 8.2 per cent to 526.8 billion, from 574.1 billion in 2022 | SIA, February 2024 |
| 2025 | Rose 25.6 per cent to 791.7 billion | SIA, 2026 |
| 2026 | Forecast at about 1.51 trillion, up about 90 per cent | WSTS forecast reported June 2026 |

Both columns of that story are true simultaneously, and almost every argument about this industry consists of one side quoting one column at the other. Sales have compounded upward for decades. Sales have also fallen by nearly a third in a single year, and have fallen by around a tenth twice in the last seven years. A business whose revenue can do that is not made safe by a good trend line, and a business whose revenue can do that is not made worthless by a bad year.

Note also what the down years have in common, which is that they follow good ones. 2001 followed a record 2000. 2019 followed a boom. 2023 followed 2021 and 2022, two years in which the world's principal complaint about semiconductors was that it could not get any. The industry does not fall because something goes wrong. It falls because something went right, and everyone responded to it in the same way at the same time.

## Three facts that assemble the machine

The memory chapter set out this loop in the narrowest and cruellest version of it, where the product is a standardised commodity. The cycle is not confined to memory. It is milder in logic, milder still in analogue and mature-node parts sold on long product lifetimes, and it has never been absent from any of them. Three facts produce it, and each of the three is individually sensible.

**Capacity is lumpy, expensive and slow.** You cannot add ten per cent more leading-edge wafer capacity because demand rose ten per cent this quarter. You add a fab, or a phase of a fab, and it is a multi-billion-dollar construction project with a multi-year timetable. The decision is discrete, large and irreversible in practice: nobody has ever half-built a cleanroom and stopped.

**Once capacity exists, its costs barely move.** Depreciation on the building and the equipment, the process engineers, the cleanroom running continuously, the abatement and the water treatment — these are incurred whether the fab runs at ninety-five per cent utilisation or sixty. The incremental cash cost of one more wafer is small relative to the cost of standing ready to make it. That is why utilisation, not price, is the number a fab operator watches, and why gross margin in this industry swings so violently: a modest fall in volume lands almost entirely on profit.

**No individual producer can protect the price by restraint.** If you slow your fab to firm up prices, you carry the fixed costs anyway, you hand share to whoever did not slow theirs, and the price moves very little because your restraint is a fraction of world supply. This is the point at which outsiders reach for the word irrational and accountants reach for the contribution statement. Running a depreciated asset at a reported loss, so long as revenue exceeds cash costs, is the profit-maximising choice. Every producer performs the same arithmetic and reaches the same answer, so nobody withdraws supply, and the glut persists until demand grows into it.

Put those together and you have a machine that runs itself. In a shortage, price rises and every producer's return on an incremental fab looks excellent. Each board approves capacity, correctly, on the information in front of it. Because the lead time is years, all of that capacity arrives at roughly the same moment, and it arrives into whatever demand conditions happen to exist then rather than the ones that justified it. Supply overshoots, price falls, and the only line item management can cut quickly is the one that determines supply two years out. So capital expenditure is slashed at the bottom, which does nothing for today's oversupply and everything to guarantee the next shortage.

It is important to be precise about what this does and does not accuse anyone of. There is no stage in that loop at which a competent executive behaves stupidly. A chief executive who declines to build in a boom watches a rival take the share and is replaced. A chief executive who keeps spending through a bust with no balance sheet to support it destroys the company. The behaviour that produces the cycle is the behaviour that keeps each individual firm alive. That is the definition of a structural problem rather than a managerial one, and it is the reason fifty years of pattern recognition has not dissolved the pattern.

## Why the capacity is always late

The lag is the load-bearing element, so it is worth taking apart properly. What actually happens between the moment a shortage is recognised and the moment additional wafers reach a customer?

The sequence is roughly this. Demand is observed to exceed supply, but the observation is not instant — it reaches the manufacturer through order books and lead times, both of which lag. A capital plan is prepared and approved. Long-lead equipment is ordered, and the most important tools have their own queue: ASML's order backlog stood at a record of about thirty-nine billion euros at the end of 2025, per trade coverage of the company's results, with extreme-ultraviolet systems the majority of it, and delivery times for those systems reported in months rather than weeks. Meanwhile the building must be found or built: site, power, water, permits, construction, cleanroom fit-out. Then the tools are installed and qualified, then the process is qualified, then yields must be brought up to something economic, then the customer must qualify the part for its own product, which for automotive and industrial customers is itself a matter of many months.

A real example fixes the scale. TSMC began construction of its first Arizona fab in April 2021 and began production there on its N4 process in the fourth quarter of 2024, per the company's own announcements and its updates on the site — a span of about three and a half years from breaking ground to production, on a project with essentially unlimited money, a customer queue and government attention behind it. Add the year or so of deliberation before the ground was broken and the round trip from decision to output is comfortably four years.

Take an illustrative capacity decision, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company, to see how that timetable interacts with a forecast:

| Stage | Elapsed time | What the decision-maker knows |
|---|---|---|
| Shortage observed, plan approved | Month 0 | Order book is full, prices firm, customers begging |
| Long-lead tools ordered | Month 3 | Same information, now committed in cash |
| Building complete, tools installed | Month 30 | Demand picture has changed once already |
| Process qualified, yields economic | Month 40 | Committed capital is sunk |
| Customer-qualified volume shipping | Month 48 | Whatever the world looks like in year four |

Every figure in the plan that justified the spend was a fact about month zero. Every dollar of revenue it earns is a fact about month forty-eight. Nobody in the chain has done anything wrong; the information simply does not survive the four years in transit.

There is an accounting consequence of that timetable which matters more than it is usually given credit for. The fab bought at the top of the boom does not merely arrive into a weaker market; it brings its depreciation with it. Capacity commissioned in month forty-eight starts charging a full year's depreciation against profit from month forty-eight, on an asset priced at boom-era construction and equipment costs, and it keeps charging it for the whole of the downturn regardless of how many wafers it makes. That is why the margin fall in this industry is so much steeper than the revenue fall: the denominator of unit cost shrinks while the numerator is fixed by a decision taken four years earlier. It is also why the accounts of a downturn look worse than the cash position — the depreciation is a sunk cost being recognised late, not money leaving the building — and why a reader should always put operating cash flow next to the operating loss before concluding that a chip maker is in danger.

The same lag runs in reverse, and this is the half that gets forgotten in a downturn. In October 2022, SK Hynix said it would cut its 2023 investment by more than half in response to a severe drop in memory demand, as Reuters reported at the time; its capital expenditure duly fell by more than half in 2023. TSMC, which had already trimmed its 2022 capital expenditure to about thirty-six billion dollars in October 2022 citing short-term uncertainty, per Focus Taiwan, spent less again in 2023 — TrendForce reported in October of that year that the figure would come in below thirty billion dollars — and its 2024 capital expenditure was about twenty-nine point eight billion. Micron cut hard through the same period.

Those decisions were correct. They were also the direct cause of the shortage that followed. By 2025 TSMC's capital expenditure had risen to about forty point nine billion dollars, and the company guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion, above analyst expectations, in its January 2026 results. In August 2026, CNBC reported SK Hynix committing thirty-eight billion dollars to new memory plants. The capacity that would have relieved 2026 needed to be ordered in 2023, in the middle of an eight per cent industry decline, by managers whose shareholders were then asking why they were still spending. The 2026 shortage was manufactured in 2023, by rational people, doing the right thing.

That is the sentence to carry out of this chapter. The cycle is not a story about greed at the top and panic at the bottom. It is a story about a control system with a four-year delay in the feedback loop. Engineers know what such a system does without being told anything about the psychology of its operators: it oscillates.

## The bullwhip: why the demand signal is worse than the demand

There is a second amplifier sitting on top of the first, and it explains why the swings in what chip makers sell are so much larger than the swings in what the world actually consumes.

Between the fab and the end user sit distributors, module makers, contract manufacturers and the customers' own stockrooms, each holding inventory. In normal conditions each holds a few weeks. When lead times extend, every one of them rationally increases safety stock — and, if allocation is being handed out in proportion to what you ask for, rationally asks for more than it needs. Orders placed in several places at once, with the intention of cancelling the losers, are the industry's oldest open secret. The manufacturer sees a surge of orders that is part real demand and part insurance, and cannot easily tell the proportions, because the paperwork looks identical.

The 2020 to 2023 episode is the textbook case and it is fully documented. Average chip delivery times reached twenty-seven point one weeks in May 2022, a record, per Susquehanna Financial Group's tracking — more than half a year between order and delivery. The shortage was severe enough to stop factories in other industries: AlixPartners forecast in September 2021 that semiconductor-related shortages would cost the global automotive industry two hundred and ten billion dollars of revenue and seven point seven million units of production that year, roughly double its own May forecast of one hundred and ten billion.

Then the demand signal reversed faster than the physical demand did. From mid-2022, Digitimes and other trade outlets reported major customers scaling back orders at TSMC. Nothing in the world had stopped needing chips; the buffers had simply filled, and a buffer that stops growing removes its entire contribution to orders in one step. The industry recorded an eight point two per cent revenue decline in 2023.

The accounting consequences were exactly what the mechanism predicts. Micron's revenue for its fiscal 2023 fell forty-nine per cent to fifteen and a half billion dollars from thirty point eight billion, gross margin went from forty-five point two per cent in fiscal 2022 to minus nine point one per cent — selling product for less than it cost to make — and the company reported a net loss of five point eight billion dollars for the year, per its results and as recapped by The Motley Fool in August 2026. That is a business with the same fabs, the same people and the same technology as the year before.

For a reader of accounts there is a specific lesson in the bullwhip, and it is worth stating as a rule. Your customer's inventory is your future revenue, already recognised. When the channel is restocking, a supplier's revenue is running ahead of consumption and is borrowing from the following year. When the channel is destocking, the supplier's revenue understates consumption. Neither is visible in the supplier's own income statement. It is visible in its customers' balance sheets, in distributor inventory disclosures where they exist, and in the gap between unit shipments and unit consumption where an industry publishes both.

## Four times the industry believed it had escaped

The cycle has been declared over more than once, and never by fools. Each argument was serious, each described something that was genuinely true, and each turned out to describe a change in the cycle's shape rather than its existence.

**The separation of design from manufacturing.** The rise of the fabless model and the pure-play foundry through the 1990s produced a reasonable argument: if most chip companies no longer own fabs, most chip companies are no longer exposed to fab economics, and the capital risk is spread across a wider industry that can absorb it. The first half of that was true — a fabless designer's cost base really is more flexible. The second half was not. The risk was not spread; it was concentrated into a small number of foundries and memory makers whose spending decisions now set world supply for everybody. And the fabless model did not protect anyone from the demand side: 2001, the worst single-year decline in the industry's history, arrived after the model had taken hold.

**The diversification of end markets.** Through the middle of the last decade the argument was that semiconductors had escaped their dependence on any one product. Chips were going into phones, cars, data centres, industrial equipment and household objects; no single end market could sink the industry, so the aggregate should be steadier than any part. Analysts made this case widely in 2017 and 2018, as contemporary coverage records. Then 2019 arrived and global sales fell twelve point one per cent. Diversification of end markets does not help when the common factor is not an end market at all but the inventory cycle and the capacity cycle, both of which move together across every end market at once.

**Consolidation.** After the cull described in chapter twenty, the argument was that a three-firm memory industry could not behave like a twenty-three-firm one, because each survivor is large enough to see the whole market and has more to lose from a price war than to gain. That argument had real force, and the years after 2013 were, by the industry's standards, unusually profitable. It did not prevent the 2019 collapse or the 2023 one. Fewer players changed the amplitude and the recovery time; it did not remove the four-year lag, and the lag is the mechanism.

**Contracts, prepayments and sold-out capacity.** The most recent version, made confidently in 2021 and 2022, was the strongest of the four: demand is no longer a forecast, it is a contract. Customers had signed long-term agreements, made prepayments, paid capacity reservation fees and accepted non-cancellable terms, and governments were subsidising construction on top. A January 2022 piece in The Motley Fool asked, in as many words, whether the semiconductor industry's boom-and-bust cycle was finished — a fair question to ask at that moment, roughly a year before an eight point two per cent annual decline. Contracts turned out to be a claim on a customer's willingness and ability to take delivery, not a law of physics. They slow the adjustment; they do not cancel it, because the adjustment happens through the inventory the customer has already accepted.

Set out together, the four arguments share a family resemblance worth naming. Each identified a real structural change and then assumed the change addressed the binding constraint. None of them touched the binding constraint, which is that supply is decided years before the demand it must meet, and no contract, merger or market diversification shortens that.

## Where in the loop are we? What the accounts say

The useful question is never whether the cycle exists. It is where in the loop the numbers currently sit. Almost all of the evidence is public, and most of it is in filings rather than commentary.

| Indicator | Late in a boom it looks like | Late in a bust it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Capital expenditure against depreciation | Capex far above depreciation, guidance revised upward mid-year | Capex cut below depreciation, projects deferred |
| Utilisation commentary | Sold out, allocation, customers prepaying for slots | Underloading charges, idle capacity, shortened working |
| Inventory days, supplier and customer | Rising at customers while suppliers stay lean | Rising at suppliers, write-downs to net realisable value |
| Purchase commitments and prepayments | Growing faster than the order book | Cancellation and renegotiation disclosures |
| Gross margin | Rising on both price and volume together | Falling on both, with negative margins possible |
| Lead times and book-to-bill | Extending; bookings comfortably above billings | Collapsing; bookings below billings |
| Language of guidance | Confidence about multi-year demand | Talk of digestion, normalisation, a temporary pause |

Chapter twenty put the first line of that table in a single sentence, and it is the one to memorise: capital expenditure far above depreciation is an industry building its next glut, and capital expenditure cut below depreciation is an industry starving its way towards the next shortage. Neither is a criticism. Both are simply where the loop currently stands.

Two of those rows deserve a caution. Inventory is the earliest genuine warning, but it is only informative when read on both sides of the transaction: a supplier with lean inventory and a customer with six months of stock is a boom about to end, and neither party's accounts say so on their own. And purchase commitments — the amounts a company has contracted to buy from its suppliers, disclosed in the notes rather than on the face of the statements — are the closest thing to a look at management's real forecast, because they cost money to be wrong about. When commitments grow faster than the order book, someone has taken a view.

The one indicator that deserves the least weight is the one that gets the most airtime, which is the confidence expressed by executives about multi-year demand. That confidence is genuine, it is required of the job, and it has been expressed with equal sincerity at every previous peak and every previous trough.

## Neither euphoria nor doom

It remains to say, as fairly as possible, what is genuinely different in the current expansion and what is not, because both errors available to the reader are expensive.

What is different is real. Demand is concentrated in a handful of extremely well-capitalised buyers rather than a fragmented channel: on CNBC's February 2026 reporting, the four largest of them were on course to spend approaching seven hundred billion dollars of capital expenditure between them in 2026, most of it funded from operating cash flow rather than borrowing. Much of the leading-edge and high-bandwidth memory capacity is contracted well in advance, which flattens the ordering behaviour that produced the 2021 bullwhip. Memory, the most cyclical product in the industry's history, has partly changed character, for the reasons chapter twenty-one sets out. And supply is constrained by things money cannot compress quickly: a single supplier of the most advanced lithography, a single dominant leading-edge foundry, and an equipment queue that is full.

What is not different is also real, and it is the same list as always. The lag between decision and output has not shortened; if anything the largest projects have got longer. Fixed costs still dominate, so utilisation still decides margin. Forecasting is still what the opening of this chapter showed it to be — an activity in which the best-informed body in the industry revised a single year by half a trillion dollars in six months, and could as easily have revised it the other way.

And there is one feature of the current expansion that is new and is a fragility rather than a comfort. End demand now rests on the capital budgets of a small number of companies. A fragmented market of a billion consumers changes its mind slowly and in aggregate; five capital budgets can be revised in five board meetings, in the same quarter, for the same reason. Concentration cuts both ways, and the chapters that follow examine what the bear case makes of that.

None of which supports a prediction, and this book does not make them. There is no date at which the cycle turns, no reliable relationship between the size of a boom and the size of the bust that follows, and nothing in this chapter that constitutes advice to buy or sell anything. The record shows only that the loop has completed every time it has been given long enough, and that each previous argument for its abolition was serious, specific, partly correct and insufficient.

The defensible position is therefore modest, and it is a position about evidence rather than about the future. Anyone claiming the cycle has ended is claiming that a four-year lag between capital decisions and output no longer produces oscillation, and they owe you the mechanism by which that has changed — not a list of reasons demand is strong, which is what every previous claim consisted of. Anyone claiming a collapse is imminent owes you the same standard in reverse: which specific capacity, arriving when, meeting which demand that will not be there. Both claims are testable against the capital expenditure lines, the inventory notes and the purchase commitments of about a dozen companies, all of which are published, most of them quarterly.

The industry's own history is the best guide it has. Read that way, the question is never whether the cycle turns. It is who has committed what, and on what terms, by the time it does.
