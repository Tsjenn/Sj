# The Power Problem

In July 2025, the operator of the largest electricity market in the United States published the results of its annual capacity auction. The price cleared at 329.17 US dollars per megawatt-day, the maximum the regulator allowed, for the delivery year running from June 2026 to May 2027. Two auctions earlier, the same product had cleared at 28.92 dollars in most of the region. The following auction, held in December 2025, hit a new cap of 333.44 dollars — a record for the third auction running, as reported by Utility Dive and Canary Media.

A capacity payment is not a payment for electricity. It is a payment for the promise of electricity: a generator's commitment to be available if called. The price of that promise rising more than tenfold in two years is the clearest single number in the industry's accounts of what this chapter is about. The market monitor for that grid, Monitoring Analytics, attributed sixty-three per cent of the price increase in the 2025/26 auction to data centre demand, worth roughly 9.3 billion dollars, and estimated that data centre load accounted for about 6.5 billion of the 16.4 billion dollars in costs from the December 2025 auction, per Utility Dive's reporting of the monitor's analysis in 2025 and 2026.

For most of this book the scarce thing has been silicon: a place in the foundry queue, an allocation of accelerators, a share of the memory that feeds them. This chapter is about the constraint that has been overtaking it. A company can now get chips faster than it can get the electricity to run them. The queue that matters is no longer at the fab. It is at the substation.

## Why a data centre is a power problem first

Start with the machine itself, because the problem begins there.

A conventional server rack — the kind that filled cloud data centres for two decades — draws on the order of ten to twelve kilowatts. Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 rack, the flagship AI system of 2024 and 2025, is planned at roughly 120 kilowatts, with each of its seventy-two graphics processors drawing on the order of 1,200 watts, per vendor specifications and trade reporting from 2024 onwards. That is an order of magnitude of density in a single product generation, and it cannot be cooled with air. Direct liquid cooling — coolant piped to plates sitting on the chips themselves — stops being an exotic option and becomes a requirement.

Density changes the building. A hall designed for twelve-kilowatt racks cannot simply have denser racks rolled in; the electrical distribution, the cooling plant and the structural loading are all wrong. This is one reason the industry builds new campuses rather than upgrading old ones, and it is why the capital expenditure figures in chapter one — the largest four buyers approaching seven hundred billion dollars of combined capex in 2026, as reported by CNBC in February 2026 — are dominated not just by chips but by the buildings, switchgear, transformers and cooling plant around them.

And density changes the arithmetic of everything upstream. Electricity is generated somewhere, stepped up to transmission voltage, carried across a network, stepped down at a substation, distributed through the campus, converted and conditioned several more times, and finally delivered to the chip — with the cooling system drawing its own share alongside. Every stage exists as physical equipment with a rating, a lead time and an owner. A data centre operator controls only the last few stages. The rest belongs to utilities and grid operators, moves at their pace, and is shared with every other user of the network. The industry's standard efficiency measure, power usage effectiveness, captures only the last portion of that chain — how much of the power entering the building reaches computing equipment rather than cooling and conversion losses. It says nothing about whether the power can be had at all.

The scale of the aggregate demand is genuinely contested, and the honest position is to quote the ranges. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's December 2024 report for the US Department of Energy estimated that data centre load growth had tripled over the prior decade and projected it would double or triple again by 2028, reaching somewhere between 6.7 and 12 per cent of total US electricity consumption. The International Energy Agency's 2024 electricity report projected global data centre consumption, including cryptocurrency mining, could exceed one thousand terawatt-hours in 2026; its later Energy and AI analysis put global data centre demand at around nine hundred and forty-five terawatt-hours by 2030. As noted in chapter one, those figures have different scopes and vintages and do not reconcile. A spread from 6.7 to 12 per cent is not a forecast; it is an admission that nobody knows how much of the announced construction will be built, or how efficient the machines inside will turn out to be. Read every projection in this space with that admission in mind.

## The queue at the substation

Anyone can order a transformer. What cannot be ordered is a connection to the transmission grid, because a connection is not a product. It is a study.

When a large generator — or, increasingly, a large load — asks to connect to the high-voltage network, the grid operator must work out what that connection does to everything else: whether lines overload, whether voltage holds, whether the system survives the loss of its largest element with the newcomer attached. If the answer requires new lines or substations, someone must pay for them, and deciding who pays is a regulatory process of its own. The request goes into a queue, the studies proceed in batches, and the queue has become one of the defining bottlenecks of the American energy system.

The numbers are documented annually by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. Its Queued Up report, 2025 edition, counted nearly 11,600 active projects seeking transmission interconnection at the end of 2024 — about 1,570 gigawatts of generating capacity and a further 1,030 gigawatts of storage, roughly 2,600 gigawatts in total, which is more than double the entire installed generating capacity of the United States. The same report found that the median time from interconnection request to commercial operation had doubled, from under two years for projects built between 2000 and 2007 to over four years for those built between 2018 and 2024. Total queued capacity actually fell about twelve per cent in 2024, the first significant decline in years — driven partly by reform and partly by a historic wave of withdrawals, because a queue that slow fills up with speculative applications that die waiting.

Two features of this queue matter for the compute layer.

The first is that it is a queue of both supply and demand. The generation that would serve new data centres is stuck in the same process as the data centres themselves. A campus can be permitted, financed and built in two to three years; the transmission upgrades it depends on can take longer than that, and the new generation behind them longer still. The physical asset with the shortest construction time waits on the asset with the longest.

The second is that the queue cannot be jumped with money. Foundry capacity, as earlier chapters showed, is rationed by price and relationship: pay more, commit longer, and allocation moves. An interconnection study is rationed by engineering sequence and regulatory process. A hyperscaler with two hundred billion dollars of annual capital expenditure waits behind a solar developer with a filing fee, because the study of one affects the study of the other. There are lawful ways around the queue — co-locating with existing generation, building your own — and the industry is pursuing all of them, as this chapter will come to. But the default path runs through a process that no balance sheet can accelerate.

## The contract becomes the asset

Follow the consequence. If connected megawatts are the scarce input, then a signed, dated, bankable right to megawatts becomes a strategic asset — something with the character of the foundry allocations of chapter thirteen or the HBM supply agreements of the memory chapters. The industry's instrument for this is the power purchase agreement, and it is worth reading as an accountant reads it.

A PPA is a long-term contract — commonly ten to twenty-five years — under which a buyer commits to purchase power, usually at a fixed or indexed price, from a specific generator. For the generator and its lenders, the contract converts a merchant asset with volatile revenue into something close to an annuity; new plants get financed against PPAs, which is precisely why a creditworthy buyer signing a long one can cause generation to exist that otherwise would not. For the buyer, the contract is three things at once: a hedge on a cost that has begun rising, a claim on capacity in a queue-constrained system, and — where the power is carbon-free — the substance behind public emissions commitments.

What the accounts show of all this is less than you might expect. A PPA is typically an executory contract: no asset or liability appears on the balance sheet when it is signed, beyond cases where it qualifies as a lease or embeds a derivative that must be marked to market. A twenty-year commitment to buy the output of a nuclear plant can therefore sit almost invisibly in the financial statements, surfacing mainly in the notes — in contractual-obligation and purchase-commitment disclosures — and in the operating expense line as the power is actually consumed. When you want to know how exposed a cloud operator is to power prices, the income statement will not tell you. The commitments note is where to look, and the disclosure is often aggregated to the point of being uninformative. That opacity is worth remembering whenever a company's energy strategy is described to you in a press release rather than a filing.

The clearest evidence that these contracts have become strategic is what buyers have been willing to sign for. Consider what happened to two reactors on the Susquehanna and Susquehanna-adjacent grid. In June 2025, Talen Energy announced an expanded agreement to supply Amazon with 1,920 megawatts from its Susquehanna nuclear plant in Pennsylvania through 2042, with options to extend — an arrangement that began as a co-located, behind-the-meter supply to a data centre campus next to the plant and was restructured to deliver through the grid, front of the meter, from spring 2026, per Talen's announcement and Utility Dive's reporting. Nearly two gigawatts is a meaningful fraction of one company's regional compute ambitions committed under a single contract running seventeen years.

The behind-the-meter detail deserves a pause, because it shows the economics and the politics in one frame. Plugging a data centre directly into a power station bypasses the transmission queue and the transmission charges — which is exactly why regulators scrutinised it. Power delivered behind the meter does not pay the network costs that other users share, and a gigawatt of nuclear output withdrawn from the market is a gigawatt other consumers no longer bid for. The restructuring of the Talen arrangement to front-of-the-meter delivery is the system pushing back: the compute layer can contract for power, but it is being made to do so inside the market, visible to the rules, rather than beside it.

And the cost of the crowding is already measurable, which returns us to the capacity auction this chapter opened with. Capacity prices are paid by all consumers in the market through their retail bills. When the market monitor attributes the bulk of a record price to data centre load — sixty-three per cent of the increase in one auction, forty per cent of total costs in another, per Monitoring Analytics as reported by Utility Dive — it is quantifying a transfer: the cost of the compute build-out appearing on household electricity bills years before any of the promised productivity appears anywhere else. This book takes no position on whether that trade is worth making. It notes only that the number exists, is large, and is exactly the kind of number that turns an engineering constraint into a political one.

## Nuclear returns

The nuclear industry spent two decades as the sector nobody would finance: enormous upfront cost, decade-long construction, and merchant power prices that made existing plants marginal. Several American reactors closed in the 2010s for want of a buyer willing to pay a premium for their defining characteristics — carbon-free output, around the clock, from a machine that runs for sixty years.

The compute layer turned out to be that buyer. What a data centre wants is precisely what a reactor sells: firm power, every hour, for decades, with no emissions to spoil the owner's climate accounting. Within roughly a year, every one of the four largest cloud and platform companies signed a nuclear arrangement of a different shape, and the set of them is best seen side by side.

| Amazon | Talen Energy — Susquehanna, Pennsylvania | 1,920 MW at full quantity, through 2042 | Expanded June 2025 | Talen announcement; Utility Dive |
| Meta | Constellation — Clinton Clean Energy Center, Illinois | 1.1 GW, 20 years from June 2027, including a 30 MW uprate | June 2025 | CNBC, Utility Dive |
| Alphabet | Kairos Power — small modular reactors, multiple sites | About 500 MW pipeline; first unit targeted by 2030 | October 2024 | World Nuclear News and trade press |

The Microsoft deal is the emblematic one. Three Mile Island Unit 1 — the reactor next to the one that failed in 1979, and itself shut down in 2019 because it could not cover its costs in the market — is being restarted on the strength of a single customer's twenty-year commitment. Constellation put the restart investment at about 1.6 billion dollars, later secured a one-billion-dollar loan from the US Department of Energy for the project, and by late 2025 was guiding to a return to service in 2027, about a year ahead of the original 2028 schedule, per reporting by Utility Dive, NucNet and World Nuclear News across 2024 and 2025. A plant that closed because electricity was too cheap is reopening because one buyer's demand made it dear again. It is hard to state the repricing of power more plainly than that.

The honest caveats belong in the same paragraph as the enthusiasm. First, restarts and uprates reallocate or modestly extend existing capacity; only new construction adds supply at scale, and large new reactors in the West have a recent record of decade-long schedules and multiplied budgets. Second, the small modular reactors behind the Alphabet agreement — and behind Amazon's roughly five-hundred-million-dollar anchor investment in the developer X-energy, reported in October 2024 — are a technology with no completed commercial Western deployment at the time of writing. The 2030 date in the Kairos agreement is a target in a press release, not a plant. A June 2026 Carnegie Endowment analysis of hyperscaler nuclear commitments made the same point at length: the announced pipeline is far ahead of what the industry has demonstrated it can build on schedule. None of this means the deals are unwise. It means the reader should file every SMR megawatt under announced, not built — the same discipline chapter twenty-nine applied to the megaprojects.

## Gas returns

Nuclear supplies the decades. It does not supply the next three years, and the next three years are when the compute is being built. The technology that fills that gap is the gas turbine, and the market for gas turbines tells you what happens when the power problem meets a manufacturing base sized for a smaller era.

GE Vernova, the largest Western maker of heavy-duty gas turbines, reported a gas equipment backlog that reached 116 gigawatts by the second quarter of 2026, against annualised production running at roughly twenty gigawatts — around five years of committed output — with delivery slots described as effectively sold out through 2030 and only about ten gigawatts of cumulative availability left across 2029 and 2030, per company statements reported by Turbomachinery Magazine, Utility Dive and Power Engineering in 2025 and 2026. The company has said it plans to lift output to around thirty gigawatts a year by 2030. Notably, its own reporting attributed only about a fifth of the contracted backlog directly to data centre customers, the rest being utilities and power producers — some of whom are, of course, building for the same data centre load one step removed.

The structure of this shortage should look familiar, because it is the accelerator shortage of 2023 transposed down two layers. A demand shock met a supply chain that takes years to expand; the order book stretched past the planning horizon; a delivery slot became a tradable advantage; and buyers responded by integrating backwards. The industry that spent 2023 and 2024 learning to queue for chips spent 2025 and 2026 learning to queue for turbines.

The clearest case of integrating backwards is the first Stargate campus at Abilene, Texas — the megaproject of chapter twenty-nine seen from its electrical room. The site's demand at full build-out has been reported at around 1.2 gigawatts. Rather than wait for the grid to supply all of it, the developers filed permits for on-site gas generation — an initial 360 megawatts of turbines, per public filings reported in 2025 — and in 2026 the equipment maker Parker Hannifin announced a supply agreement covering twenty-nine GE Vernova aeroderivative turbines for the campus, a combined 1.015 gigawatts, alongside reported plans for on-site solar and battery storage, per Data Center Dynamics. Read that as an income-statement decision: the developer is accepting the higher running cost and the fuel-price exposure of self-generated power in exchange for certainty of schedule. On-site gas is rarely the cheapest electricity. It is the electricity you can have on the date the chips arrive, and in this period the cost of an idle accelerator fleet — depreciating, as chapter thirty-two will quantify, whether or not it runs — dominates the premium on the fuel.

A word of accounting caution about the backlog figures that now decorate the power equipment sector's results. A backlog is an order book, not revenue; orders can be cancelled, deferred and renegotiated, and the disclosure of what deposit sits behind an order is usually thin. The semiconductor chapters made this point about foundry prepayments and it holds here unchanged: a company reporting a backlog "bigger than some countries' GDP", as the financial press put it of GE Vernova's 176-billion-dollar total backlog in August 2026 per the Motley Fool, is reporting demand as observed at a peak of enthusiasm. The 2001 telecommunications equipment order books looked similar shortly before they did not.

## Power decides the map

Put the pieces together — a four-year interconnection queue, sold-out turbines, nuclear commitments that deliver next decade — and the consequence follows: the location of compute is increasingly decided by where power can actually be had. Site selection in the cloud industry used to be led by fibre routes, land, tax treatment and latency to users. Training clusters broke most of those constraints — a training run does not care about latency to customers — and left the electrical one standing alone.

Ireland is the cautionary case study, and it matters because Dublin was one of Europe's great data centre hubs. Data centres consumed about twenty-one per cent of Ireland's metered electricity in 2023 and roughly twenty-two per cent in 2024, up from five per cent in 2015, per the national statistics office's figures as widely reported. In 2022 the state grid operator, EirGrid, stopped accepting new data centre connection applications in the Dublin region — a de facto moratorium reported as running until 2028, per Data Center Dynamics. The freeze was only lifted in early 2026, when the energy regulator reopened large-load connections under conditions reported to include majority-renewable supply within a set period and full on-site backup, per Energy Connects and KPMG's 2026 policy analysis, even as the grid operator continued to warn of peak-demand shortfalls through 2028. A jurisdiction that had spent two decades courting the industry spent four years refusing it at the socket. Capacity that would have landed in Dublin went elsewhere in Europe and beyond — not because anywhere else was better, but because elsewhere could say yes.

The same selection pressure explains the American map. Texas offers the combination the industry now optimises for: a single-state grid with a fast interconnection process, abundant gas and renewable generation, land, and a regulatory culture that permits on-site generation — which is why the largest announced campuses of the megaproject era, Abilene among them, cluster there. The general rule is worth stating because it inverts two decades of practice: the industry used to bring data centres to the demand; it now brings them to the supply. Where the supply is a stranded asset — a nuclear plant without a premium buyer, a gas field without a pipeline customer, a wind-rich grid without transmission out — the compute layer has become the buyer of last resort, and sometimes of first resort.

For a reader auditing any company's expansion claims, this yields a practical test. An announcement that names a site, a megawatt figure, and a signed supply arrangement with a counterparty describes a project. An announcement that names a gigawatt figure and a region describes an ambition that has not yet met a substation. The difference between the two is usually several years and occasionally the difference between happening and not.

## Reading the power problem in the accounts

Finish where this book always finishes: what of all this is visible in the numbers, and where.

For the compute layer's public companies, look in four places. Capital expenditure disclosures, first, though they rarely separate the building from the chips inside it; where a company discusses "infrastructure" capex against "technical equipment", the split matters because the building depreciates over decades and the chips over a handful of years — the subject of the next chapter. Second, the purchase-commitment and contractual-obligation notes, where long-term power contracts surface if they surface at all; a rising multi-year commitments figure with no corresponding balance-sheet item is often the footprint of PPAs. Third, the operating expense discussion, where energy costs sit inside cost of revenue, almost never separately quantified; a cloud operator's gross margin is partly a bet on power prices, and the bet is disclosed nowhere. Fourth, for the utilities and equipment makers on the other side of these contracts — the Constellations, Talens and GE Vernovas — the disclosures are far richer, because for them the data centre is the customer concentration risk, and concentration must be disclosed. Some of the clearest published information about the compute layer's power arrangements appears in the filings of its suppliers rather than its members. The accountant's habit of reading a company through its counterparties earns its keep here.

To see why power deserves this attention in the unit economics, take an illustrative campus, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Give it one hundred megawatts of continuous total draw and a power price of eighty dollars per megawatt-hour, all-in. One hundred megawatts for the year's roughly 8,760 hours is about 876,000 megawatt-hours, which at eighty dollars is roughly seventy million dollars of electricity a year. Against that, suppose the chips inside cost four billion dollars and depreciate over five years: eight hundred million dollars a year. On these invented numbers the electricity is real money — seventy million dollars annually, decades of it contracted — but it is a tenth of the depreciation charge sitting above it. That ordering is the quiet answer to a common confusion: power is the binding constraint on whether and where compute gets built, but depreciation is the dominant cost of running it. Scarcity and expense are different properties. The grid decides if the campus exists; the accelerated march of silicon decides what it earns.

Both halves of that sentence carry dates, and both will move. The capacity auctions reprice annually; the turbine backlog is a snapshot of mid-2026; every nuclear restart date quoted here is a schedule, and schedules in that industry have a direction of travel. The reader who wants the current state of the power problem should do what this book keeps recommending: go to the grid operators' published queue statistics, the capacity auction results, and the commitments notes of the companies on both sides of the meter. The press release will tell you about the ambition. The interconnection queue will tell you about the date.

The deeper point belongs to the book's spine. The argument so far has been that value moves down the stack — from applications to models to compute to silicon — towards whichever layer holds the input that cannot be quickly reproduced. This chapter found a layer below the bottom one. Under the silicon sits the grid: generation, wires and transformers, owned by none of the companies in this book, expanding on a timetable set by engineering studies and regulators rather than by capital. The industry that compressed the doubling time of computing into eighteen-month rhythms is now waiting on infrastructure that moves in decades. Whether value keeps moving down — into the utilities, the turbine makers and the owners of connected megawatts — is a question the next few years of contracts will answer. The auctions, at least, have already voted.
