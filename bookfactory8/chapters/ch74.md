# Who Could Still Enter

Two facts, set side by side, describe the whole industry.

A leading-edge two-nanometre fab module, with capacity of roughly twenty thousand wafer starts a month, costs somewhere between twenty-five and thirty-five billion US dollars to build and equip, per Tom's Hardware reporting in July 2026 on TSMC's additional one hundred billion dollar Arizona commitment. In the same month, an artificial-intelligence coding tool built by a company under four years old was reported at roughly four billion dollars of annualised revenue, and was being acquired for sixty billion dollars in stock, per Forbes on 16 June 2026.

One of those businesses is nearly impossible to start. The other was started by a handful of people with a laptop each. Both sit in the same supply chain, and money flows from the second to the first.

This chapter asks a narrow question: given the stack as it stands, who could still get in, at which floor, and what exactly would they need? It is a question about barriers to entry, and barriers to entry are not a matter of opinion. They can be counted.

## What a barrier to entry actually is

In ordinary usage a barrier to entry means "it is hard". In accounting terms it has three separable components, and each can be measured for each layer of the stack.

The first is **minimum viable capital**: the smallest cheque that buys a competitive position rather than a hobby. Not the cost of trying, but the cost of mattering.

The second is **time to first revenue**: how long the capital sits idle before a customer pays anything. This is the component that kills entrants, because capital that must wait five years for revenue is a different instrument from capital that waits five months. It needs a different sort of owner.

The third, and least discussed, is **recoverability**: what the assets are worth if the attempt fails. An entrant who can sell the wreckage for eighty cents in the dollar is taking a different risk from one whose assets are worth scrap. Accountants have a word for this — asset specificity — and it decides whether a failed entry is an embarrassment or an extinction.

Put those three together and the ordering of the stack falls out.

| Layer | Minimum viable capital | Time to first revenue | What a failed attempt is worth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Semiconductor (leading-edge logic) | Tens of billions | Five to ten years | A building and depreciating tools for a node nobody wants |
| Compute | Billions, mostly borrowed | Two to four years | Real estate, power rights and second-hand accelerators |
| Model | Hundreds of millions | Months, at low revenue | A research team, which walks out of the door |
| Application | Low single-digit millions | Weeks | Almost nothing, and almost nothing was spent |

Read the last column carefully, because it contains the argument of this chapter. The layers where failure destroys the most capital are the layers where the survivors earn the most margin. That is not a coincidence. It is the same fact stated twice.

## The semiconductor layer: capital, physics and a queue

Start at the bottom, where entry is hardest and the reasons are most concrete.

The capital number is the one already given: twenty-five to thirty-five billion US dollars for a leading-edge module, per Tom's Hardware in July 2026. That is not the cost of entry. It is the cost of one module for a company that already knows how to run one.

The incumbent's spending sets the pace an entrant must match. TSMC guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion US dollars in its January 2026 results, above analyst expectations; by July 2026 the figure being reported was as much as sixty-four billion, alongside the additional one hundred billion dollar Arizona commitment for at least four more fabs at two nanometres and below, per Tom's Hardware and Yahoo Finance. Cumulative planned United States spending was reported at two hundred and sixty-five billion dollars. Note the direction of the revision rather than the level: guidance in this business has moved up, not down, and an entrant is aiming at a target that is accelerating.

Then there is the equipment queue, which no amount of money shortens by much. ASML shipped forty-eight extreme-ultraviolet systems in 2025 and planned to ship more than sixty in 2026, per TechPowerUp's coverage of the company's guidance; the first production-grade High-NA EXE:5200B shipped in the fourth quarter of 2025, with Intel first in line for its 14A node, and ASML has scheduled ten High-NA scanners for delivery in 2027. High-NA tools were reported at about three hundred and eighty million US dollars when announced, with 2026 coverage putting the most advanced above four hundred million euros — trade-press figures, not list prices, since ASML sells to a handful of customers on negotiated terms.

Sixty machines a year, globally, allocated years in advance among perhaps five buyers. An entrant needs more than the money. It needs a place in a queue that the existing customers have every reason to keep full.

### What entry at this layer has actually looked like

Two live attempts show the conditions required, and neither is a startup in any normal sense.

**Rapidus**, in Japan, opened a pilot line in April 2025 and targets mass production of two-nanometre logic in fiscal 2027. In February 2026 it completed a funding round of two hundred and sixty-seven point six billion yen, about one point seven billion US dollars, of which one hundred billion yen came from the government's Information-technology Promotion Agency and the remainder from thirty-two companies including Canon, Fujitsu, NTT, SoftBank, the Development Bank of Japan and Sony, per the company's own announcement and reporting by Tom's Hardware and The Register in February and March 2026. Japanese state support for the venture has been reported at more than two point three five trillion yen in total. The company said it was in active discussions with more than sixty potential customers.

Read that as an accountant would. A one point seven billion dollar equity round is a rounding error against a twenty-five to thirty-five billion dollar module cost. What makes the attempt possible is not the round; it is the state commitment behind it, and the willingness of thirty-two industrial companies to fund a supplier they would like to exist. This is the first condition of entry at the bottom of the stack: **capital that does not require a return on the ordinary schedule.**

**Intel Foundry** is the other case, and its management has been unusually explicit about the second condition. Intel's leadership has said that 14A capacity will only be built against firm commitments from external customers, with those customers expected to make supplier decisions from the second half of 2026 into the first half of 2027, per Forbes and trade coverage through 2026. Intel is not currently producing meaningful external volume on 18A. The company has the fabs, the process people, the balance sheet and the High-NA tools ahead of anyone else — and it is still waiting on the one thing it cannot buy.

That thing is an anchor customer: **a buyer willing to move a real product to an unproven line.** Nobody does that for a discount. They do it for capacity they cannot otherwise get, or for a second source they are willing to pay for.

**Samsung** shows how long the third condition takes. Its foundry share was reported at seven point seven per cent in the first quarter of 2025 against TSMC's sixty-seven point six per cent, per 2026 trade compilations, with utilisation above eighty per cent in the first quarter of 2026, a one-year high. It signed a sixteen point five billion dollar multi-year agreement with Tesla in July 2025 for chips on its two-nanometre process, and Tesla's AI5 taped out at Samsung for production at the Taylor plant in Texas, per Electrek and Tom's Hardware in 2026. Samsung has been in leading-edge foundry for years, with capital and customers, and is still fighting for share. The third condition is **yield**, and yield is earned rather than bought.

Then there is the case that ignores the queue entirely. Chinese manufacturers have been producing seven-nanometre and reportedly five-nanometre-class parts using deep-ultraviolet lithography rather than the restricted EUV tools, and domestic EUV development using laser-induced discharge plasma has been reported as undergoing trials with mass manufacturing targeted for 2026, per DigiTimes reporting from March 2025 onward. Treat all of it carefully: none of these figures come from audited disclosure or from the companies themselves, yields at advanced nodes without EUV are reported as poor by the same trade press, and this book does not have a verified figure for them. What can be said without a number is structural. A state that decides an industry must exist domestically can substitute time and money for tools, at a cost in unit economics that a commercial entrant could never carry.

### The entrant who does not build a fab

There is a second kind of semiconductor entrant, and it is the one that has actually succeeded recently: the designer who buys manufacturing from someone else.

**Cerebras** went public on 14 May 2026, priced at one hundred and eighty-five dollars a share, raising five point five five billion dollars and closing its first day at a fully diluted valuation of about fifty-six billion, per Forbes in May 2026 — reported as the largest United States technology listing since 2020. Its revenue went from twenty-four point six million dollars in 2022 to five hundred and ten million in 2025, with one hundred and ninety-three point four million in the first quarter of 2026, roughly double the same quarter a year earlier.

**Groq** took the other exit. On 24 December 2025 Nvidia agreed to acquire the startup's assets for about twenty billion dollars in what CNBC reported as its largest deal on record, structured as a non-exclusive licensing agreement with Groq's chief executive and senior leaders joining Nvidia while the company continued independently. For scale, Nvidia's previous largest acquisition was Mellanox, at close to seven billion dollars in 2019.

Both proved that a genuinely different architecture can be designed, fabricated and sold by an entrant. Neither proved it can be sold at scale against the incumbent's software, supply and installed base — one listed, one was absorbed. The design entrant clears a barrier measured in hundreds of millions rather than tens of billions, then meets a second barrier, at the point of distribution, which is not made of capital at all.

## The compute layer: capital, power and a credit rating

One floor up, the physics relaxes and the arithmetic takes over. Anyone can buy accelerators. The barrier is that the machines must be paid for years before the revenue arrives, and paid for mostly with borrowed money.

The public neoclouds show the shape precisely, because unlike almost everything else in this industry they file.

**CoreWeave** reported second-quarter 2026 revenue of two point six billion US dollars, more than double the prior year, with four point seven billion in first-half sales and full-year guidance of twelve point four to thirteen point two billion, per the company's results and CNBC and The Register in August 2026. The same disclosures put total indebtedness at thirty-five point six billion dollars as at 30 June 2026, quarterly net interest expense up one hundred and forty per cent to six hundred and forty million, capital expenditure guidance of thirty-five to thirty-nine billion for the year, deferred revenue of nine point seven billion and remaining performance obligations of one hundred and three point seven billion, against a target of more than one point eight five gigawatts of active power by year end.

**Nebius** guided 2026 revenue to three to three point four billion dollars, supported by an annualised run-rate target of seven to nine billion and more than three gigawatts of contracted power, per 2026 reporting; its run rate was reported at three billion by mid-2026, up from one point nine billion at the end of March. Its agreement with Microsoft was reported as generating as much as seventeen point four billion dollars through 2031, including roughly seven billion of upfront payments.

Read those two sets of figures together and the real barrier at this layer becomes visible, and it is not the money.

It is the contract. A one hundred and three point seven billion dollar remaining performance obligation, or a seventeen point four billion dollar agreement with a company of Microsoft's credit standing, is what makes thirty-five billion dollars of debt financeable in the first place. Lenders are not underwriting the entrant's business plan. They are underwriting the counterparty's promise to pay, secured on hardware.

Take an illustrative build, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. A one hundred megawatt site costs, say, four billion to fill with hardware and fit out. Equity of one billion, debt of three billion at eight per cent, is two hundred and forty million of annual interest before a single machine is depreciated. Now assume the hardware is written off over five years: eight hundred million a year of depreciation. Before power, staff, land or profit, the site must produce over a billion dollars of annual revenue simply to stand still. No sensible lender advances that money against hope. They advance it against a signed, dated obligation from someone who will still exist in year five.

So the condition for entry at the compute layer is **an offtake contract signed before the concrete is poured**, and the corollary is uncomfortable: the entrant's business is largely a function of the creditworthiness of the handful of companies capable of signing one. Independence at this layer is thinner than it appears. Where the counterparty is also an investor in, or supplier to, the entrant, the structure deserves the careful reading given to cross-holdings elsewhere in this book. That is a note about how to read the accounts, not an allegation about anyone.

### The constraint that money does not solve

There is one input at this layer that behaves like physics rather than finance, and it has become the binding one.

Securing grid power for a large new site was reported in 2026 as typically taking twenty-four to seventy-two months, with connections in constrained regions quoted at five to seven years, and waits of four to seven years reported in Northern Virginia, Phoenix and Dallas, per 2026 industry analysis of interconnection queues. ERCOT was tracking a large-load interconnection queue of roughly four hundred and ten gigawatts as at April 2026, with data centres accounting for about eighty-seven per cent of it. National generation and storage queues were reported at more than two thousand six hundred gigawatts.

Those are queue figures rather than commitments, and a queue is a wish list — most of what sits in it will never be built. That is the point. An entrant with capital and no queue position is four to seven years from a customer in the markets where customers are, which is the same lead time as a fab.

This creates a new class of potential entrant that has nothing to do with computing. Whoever already holds interconnection rights, generation, or a site with power — a utility, an industrial landowner, a decommissioned plant, a sovereign fund with its own grid — holds the scarce thing. Several have noticed.

**Humain**, owned by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund and launched in May 2025, broke ground on data centres in Riyadh and Dammam with first facilities expected live in the second quarter of 2026, targeting one point nine gigawatts installed by 2030 and six gigawatts by 2034, with an initial fund commitment reported at about forty billion dollars, per CNBC in August 2025 and Data Center Dynamics in 2026. It has been reported as purchasing eighteen thousand Nvidia GB300 chips, subject to United States government approval, and as signing a ten billion dollar infrastructure agreement with AMD. Those are announcements, and the distinction between announced, contracted and delivered matters more here than almost anywhere.

The condition for a sovereign entrant is therefore **power, land, patient capital and an export licence** — three of which are domestic decisions and one of which is not.

## The model layer: capital and talent, and both are falling

The middle of the stack is where the barrier has changed fastest, and where the direction of change is genuinely contested.

At the frontier, the barrier is rising. Epoch AI's research on training costs finds that the amortised hardware and energy cost of the final training run for frontier models has grown at roughly two point four times a year since 2016, and projects that the largest models will cost over a billion dollars by 2027. On that trajectory, the number of organisations able to fund a frontier pre-training run falls every year rather than rising.

Below the frontier, the barrier has collapsed. DeepSeek reported that its V3 model required two point seven eight eight million H800 GPU-hours, which it costed at about five point six million US dollars — a figure that drew immediate and well-founded scepticism, reported by CNBC in January 2025. The scepticism was not that the number was false but that it measured something narrow: SemiAnalysis's analysis put the company's total server capital expenditure at approximately one point six billion dollars with a further nine hundred and forty-four million associated with operating those clusters, and noted that the five point six million covered the final pre-training run alone, excluding research, failed runs, staff and infrastructure.

Both numbers are true, and the gap between them is the single most useful lesson in this chapter. The cost of *reproducing* a known capability is now a small fraction of the cost of *discovering* it. An entrant that is content to arrive second, using published architectures and open weights, faces a barrier one or two orders of magnitude below the one facing the company that goes first.

That is why the model layer's barrier is described as falling even while frontier training costs rise. Those are two different businesses wearing the same name.

### What the recent entrants actually needed

The labs founded since 2024 show which conditions still bind.

**Safe Superintelligence**, founded in June 2024, has been reported at a thirty-two billion dollar valuation with no commercial product and a public commitment to keeping it that way until its stated mission is complete, per 2026 company profiles; a further five billion dollar investment by Nvidia was reported in July 2026 by trade press. Treat both figures as reported rather than audited: this is a private company that files nothing, and a valuation of this kind is the arithmetic of a negotiated round, not a market price.

**Mistral** raised one point seven billion euros in September 2025 at a post-money valuation reported by CNBC at about thirteen point eight billion dollars, led by ASML with an investment of one point three billion euros for roughly eleven per cent on a fully diluted basis, alongside existing investors including Nvidia; a debt financing of about eight hundred and thirty million dollars from a consortium of seven banks was reported in March 2026.

Look at who is writing those cheques. A lithography company took the lead position in a European model lab; a chip designer invested in a research company with no product. Capital entering the model layer increasingly comes from the layers below it, which have the cash and a strategic interest in there being more than two customers.

The conditions for a model-layer entrant are therefore fairly specific: **capital that tolerates three years without revenue; a compute contract at a price the entrant could not negotiate alone; a research group of a few dozen people; and a reason to exist that is not "another frontier model".** The reasons that have worked so far are sovereignty, a specific vertical, an open-weight strategy, or a research thesis that the incumbents have chosen not to pursue.

The accounting test for whether this layer is becoming a business at all is the one this book keeps returning to: compute as a share of revenue. Reported figures for one of the private labs moved from about seventy-one cents in the revenue dollar in the first quarter of 2026 to a projected fifty-six cents in the second, with gross margin tracking around forty-four per cent; the other was reported at about thirty-nine per cent gross margin in the first quarter of 2026. Both are unaudited and reached the press through interested parties. If that ratio keeps falling, an entrant is joining a business. If it stalls, an entrant is joining a pass-through.

## The application layer: low, and getting lower

At the top, the barrier is a laptop and a payments account. This is not a figure of speech, and the consequences run in both directions.

The evidence for how low it is comes from one company. Cursor, built by Anysphere, was reported at one hundred million dollars of annualised recurring revenue in January 2025, five hundred million by June 2025, above one billion by late 2025, above two billion by February 2026 and roughly four billion by June 2026, per 2026 compilations of the company's disclosed milestones. It closed a two point three billion dollar Series D at a twenty-nine point three billion dollar valuation in November 2025. On 16 June 2026 SpaceX announced an all-stock acquisition of the company at sixty billion dollars, described by Forbes as the largest acquisition of a venture-backed startup on record, with roughly two point six billion of the annualised revenue reported as enterprise business.

Four years, from nothing to that. No fab, no interconnection queue, no pre-training run. There is no comparable path at any other layer of the stack.

Now read the same fact from the other side. If a company can reach four billion dollars of annualised revenue in four years, so can the next one. Nothing in that story is protected by physics, by a supplier queue, or by a five-year lead time. The application layer's inputs are available to every competitor on the same terms: the same models, priced from a published rate card, running on the same clouds. Its price point is conventional rather than derived — twenty US dollars a month for a consumer tier, inherited from a decade of subscription software. Its customers can switch by cancelling one direct debit and starting another.

And its cost of goods sold is set by a supplier that also competes with it. A model provider that raises token prices compresses the application's gross margin directly; a model provider that ships a first-party feature removes the application's product. This is the position that the accounts describe as customer concentration when read from below, and as supplier concentration when read from above. It is the same relationship.

Set the layers' gross margins next to each other and the pattern is hard to miss: Nvidia at seventy-one point one per cent GAAP for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026 and TSMC at sixty-seven point seven per cent in the second quarter of 2026, both audited company results, against roughly forty-four and thirty-nine per cent reported and unaudited at the two largest model companies in 2026. The application layer sits above all of them, buying from the forty-per-cent business, at prices convention has fixed.

So the layer with the lowest barrier is the layer where margin is hardest to defend, and the two facts are the same fact. A barrier to entry is also a barrier to exit. Sunk, specific, illiquid assets are what keep competitors out; a business whose assets can walk out of the building at five o'clock has nothing keeping anyone out either.

That does not mean nobody can defend anything up here. It means the defence has to come from somewhere other than the technology. Four things have historically worked: proprietary data that cannot be re-created; embedment in a workflow that is expensive to change; distribution the entrant already owns; and a regulated position that takes years to obtain. Every one of those is a reason a customer stays that has nothing to do with the model being better.

## Who could still enter, and on what condition

| Potential entrant | Layer | The condition it must meet | What to watch in the record |
|---|---|---|---|
| State-backed foundry (Rapidus and successors) | Semiconductor | Capital with no ordinary return schedule, plus an anchor customer | Whether announced customer discussions become named, dated commitments |
| Intel Foundry as a merchant fab | Semiconductor | External commitments before 14A capacity is built | Supplier decisions reported from H2 2026 into H1 2027 |
| Domestic Chinese leading edge | Semiconductor | Substituting time and money for restricted tools | Verified yield or output data from any independent source |
| Architecture specialists (inference silicon) | Semiconductor | Distribution, not design | Revenue growth against gross margin; whether they list or are absorbed |
| Neoclouds | Compute | Signed offtake from an investment-grade counterparty | Remaining performance obligations against total debt and interest cover |
| Power owners: utilities, industrial landowners | Compute | Converting interconnection rights into a rentable asset | Queue positions and energisation dates, not announcements |
| Sovereign programmes | Compute | Power, patient capital and an export licence | Announced versus contracted versus delivered capacity |
| Fast-follower model labs | Model | Open weights plus a compute contract they could not negotiate alone | Compute as a share of revenue; whether anyone publishes audited accounts |
| Frontier research labs | Model | Three years of funding with no revenue and a distinct thesis | Who is funding them, and from which layer |
| Application companies | Application | Data, workflow, distribution or regulation — not model quality | Whether any of them raises price and keeps its customers |

Two entries in that table are worth stating plainly rather than in a cell.

The first is that the most plausible new entrant at the compute layer may not be a technology company at all. If power is the binding constraint — and the queue figures suggest it is, in the markets that matter — then the party holding generation and interconnection rights holds the scarce input, and technology companies become its customers rather than the other way round. That is a reversal the accounts would show quickly, in the form of long-term power purchase agreements moving from a footnote to a principal risk.

The second is that entry at the semiconductor layer almost certainly does not mean a new TSMC. It means a credible second source at a specific node, which is a far smaller claim and a far more consequential one. Concentration risk in this industry is not that there is one good foundry. It is that there is one for the parts that matter most. A second source at two nanometres would change the pricing of everything above it, without anyone's market share having to move very much.

## What would change the ranking

The ordering set out in this chapter is a description of 2026, not a law. Three things would reorder it, and each is observable.

If frontier training costs keep compounding at the rate Epoch AI has measured, while capability at the fast-follower tier keeps converging, then the model layer splits permanently into two businesses with different barriers — a research business fundable only by the largest balance sheets, and a deployment business with almost no barrier at all. Watch the gap between what the frontier costs and what reproducing it costs.

If power rather than silicon becomes the constraint that binds first, the compute layer's barrier stops being financial and becomes political, since interconnection is granted by regulators rather than bought. Watch utility filings and queue data, which are public and free, rather than company announcements, which are marketing.

And if an entrant at the bottom of the stack ever lands a named anchor customer at the leading edge, the concentration that produces the margins in the first place begins to loosen. Watch for a commitment with a customer's name and a date on it. Anything short of that is an intention.

Every figure here is a snapshot with a date attached, and several will have moved before it is read. The current versions sit where they always have: Form 10-K and 10-Q filings for listed United States companies, Form 20-F and 6-K for foreign private issuers including TSMC and ASML, investor relations pages, and the interconnection queue data published by grid operators. Private companies file nothing, which is why the figures for them here are attributed to whoever reported them rather than stated as fact.

Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything, and nothing here predicts what any of these companies will be worth. The useful question is not whether someone could enter a given layer. Plainly they could; people are doing it at every floor as this is written. The question is whether an entrant could still be earning a return in year eight, after the capital is sunk, the competitors have arrived, and the thing that made the entry possible has become available to everybody.

At the bottom of the stack, that question is answered by physics and a queue. At the top, it is answered every month, by customers who can leave.
