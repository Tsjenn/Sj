# Nvidia II: The Decade Nobody Noticed

On 8 November 2006 Nvidia launched the GeForce 8800 GTX, built on a chip called G80. The reviews that day were about games. The 8800 was the first graphics part with a unified shader architecture — a single pool of programmable cores that could be aimed at vertices, pixels or geometry as the workload demanded, instead of separate fixed blocks for each — and the first to support DirectX 10, as the contemporaneous November 2006 reviews at PC Perspective and HardwareZone recorded. It was a fast card. That was the story.

The other thing that arrived with that generation got a paragraph, if it got anything. Nvidia had been building, since 2004, a way for an ordinary programmer to write ordinary programs for a graphics chip. The effort was led by Ian Buck, who had joined the company in 2004 after doctoral work at Stanford on stream computing and the Brook language, and who has described the goal in interviews since as taking the graphics processor beyond graphics and making it programmable for general-purpose work. The result was called CUDA. Nvidia showed it alongside the G80 in 2006 and made the first public toolkit available in February 2007; the company itself dates the platform's beginning to 2006, and marked twenty years of it at its March 2026 developer conference.

Nothing about that looked like the most valuable decision of the period. It looked like a graphics company paying to become a slower graphics company. This chapter is about why it was not, and it is a software chapter in a book about hardware, because the moat this industry now argues about was not cut in silicon.

## What CUDA actually is

Strip the acronym away and there are two facts.

The first is about the machine. A central processor is built to do one thing after another, very fast, with a great deal of circuitry devoted to guessing what it will be asked to do next. A graphics processor is built the other way. It has many simple arithmetic units and very little cleverness, because the job it was designed for — deciding the colour of two million pixels, where the calculation for each pixel is the same and none of them depends on the others — rewards width rather than speed. A graphics processor is not a faster computer. It is a wider one, and it is only useful when the work divides.

The second fact is about access. Until CUDA, using that width for anything other than graphics meant lying to the machine. A researcher who wanted to multiply large matrices had to express the matrices as textures, express the multiplication as a shading operation, and read the answer back out as an image. This was done, seriously and successfully, and it was called general-purpose computing on graphics processors. It also required a person who understood both the mathematics and the graphics pipeline, and there were not many of those.

CUDA removed the disguise. In plain terms it is three things bundled together: a small set of extensions to the C programming language that let a programmer mark a function as one to be run in parallel on the device; a compiler that turns that into instructions the chip understands; and a runtime and driver that move the data across and manage the thousands of threads. A competent programmer who had never opened a graphics textbook could now write something that ran on the wide machine. That is the whole of it.

It is worth being clear about what CUDA is not, because the word gets used loosely. It is not a chip. It is not an industry standard agreed by a committee. It is not a product with a price; the toolkit is a free download, and Nvidia does not report revenue from it as a separate line. This last point matters more than it sounds. A thing that is given away has no revenue line, and a thing with no revenue line is very hard for an outsider — or a board — to value.

## The line item nobody wanted to pay

Free to the user is not free to the maker, and this is where an accountant's reading starts to differ from a technology writer's.

Making the G80 generation genuinely programmable cost silicon area. General-purpose code needs a proper memory hierarchy, predictable addressing, and eventually error correction and full double-precision arithmetic — none of which a game needs, and all of which take transistors that could otherwise have gone into frame rates. The die gets bigger. A bigger die yields fewer good chips per wafer. The cost per chip rises. And the product carrying that cost was a consumer graphics card, sold into a market that compares parts on gaming benchmarks and price, and that would not pay a penny more for a feature it could not see.

Jensen Huang has put the effect bluntly. Describing the period in a long interview with Lex Fridman released in 2025, he said the problem was that CUDA increased the cost of the graphics processor — a consumer product — so much that it completely consumed all of the company's gross profit dollars. In the same conversation he recalled the market value of the company falling from roughly six to eight billion dollars to something near one and a half billion. Treat that second set of figures as what they are: a chief executive's recollection of a share price move nearly two decades earlier, given in an interview, not a figure taken from an audited statement. The direction is not in dispute. The precision is not the point.

Now the accounting, which is the part that explains why nobody outside the company could see what was being built.

Research and development spending is expensed as it is incurred. Under United States accounting rules, research and development costs go through the income statement in the period they are spent, with only narrow exceptions for certain software costs; under international standards, development costs may be capitalised, but only where a company can demonstrate technical feasibility, an intention and ability to complete, and — critically — the way in which the asset will generate probable future economic benefits. A programming platform being built for customers who do not yet exist fails that last test comfortably.

So the money goes out, the profit goes down, and no asset appears anywhere. The balance sheet of a company spending heavily on a decade-long software platform looks exactly like the balance sheet of a company spending heavily on nothing at all. There is no line called "moat under construction". There is only a smaller operating profit and, if the company is unlucky, a shareholder asking why.

What the accounts did show, in the years the platform was maturing, is a research budget that stayed large against a revenue base that was not growing.

This was a company of roughly four billion dollars in annual sales carrying a research budget more appropriate to a much larger one, in years when sales went sideways.

### An illustrative case, belonging to no real company

Take two illustrative companies, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to nobody real. Both earn one hundred million a year in revenue and both spend twenty million a year for ten years building a platform.

The first is required to expense it. Every year its reported profit is twenty million lower. After ten years its balance sheet shows nothing for the effort, and its cumulative reported profits are two hundred million lighter.

The second, by the accident of what it is building and which rules apply, capitalises the spend. Every year an asset grows on its balance sheet, amortised over some assumed life. After ten years there is a visible asset, and analysts can argue about whether it is worth its carrying value.

The two companies did exactly the same thing. Only one of them looks like it did. This is not an argument that expensing is wrong — it is the conservative and usually correct treatment, precisely because most such projects fail. It is an argument that the accounts are not designed to tell you which of the failures is going to work, and that the reader who wants to spot the next one will not find it in the assets. It will be in the operating expense line, looking like waste.

## The years in between

Between the launch of CUDA and the moment it mattered, roughly six years passed in which the investment produced very little that could be pointed at.

They were not quiet years for other reasons. In July 2008 Nvidia announced it would take a charge of between one hundred and fifty and two hundred million dollars against cost of revenue for warranty, repair, return and replacement costs arising from a weak die and packaging material set in certain earlier notebook parts; the charge recorded in the quarter ended 27 July 2008 was 196.0 million dollars. Tom's Hardware, following the matter through subsequent filings, reported the cumulative net charges over the material defect reaching 475.9 million dollars. The episode is remembered in the industry by a nickname; for the purpose of this book it is a reminder that the company spending on an unproven platform was also absorbing a nine-figure quality failure, into a global financial crisis, while its main market was contracting.

Through the same stretch the platform kept getting more expensive to build, and the features being added were increasingly not for gamers. Nvidia announced the Fermi architecture in November 2009, as HPCwire reported at the time: error correction protecting memory from registers through to DRAM, double-precision floating point compliant with IEEE 754-2008, proper caches, and support for a broader set of languages. Volume deployment of the Tesla parts built on it was planned from May 2010. Every item on that list is a feature that scientific and financial customers ask for and a gaming customer will never notice. The company was paying, in silicon area, to be taken seriously by a set of buyers it did not yet have.

A standard arrived in this window and did not change the outcome. The Khronos Group released the OpenCL 1.0 specification on 9 December 2008, proposed originally by Apple and ratified with AMD, ARM, Broadcom, Intel and others alongside — an open, royalty-free way to program parallel processors across vendors. On paper this should have ended any single company's advantage. It did not, and the reason is instructive: a specification is not a toolchain. Programmers do not adopt the thing that is most open. They adopt the thing that compiles, has documentation, has a profiler that tells them why their code is slow, and has an answer on a forum when it does not work. Nvidia had spent the intervening two years building all of that, and kept building it.

By late 2012 the scientific side had visible wins. The TOP500 list published on 12 November 2012 put Titan, the Cray XK7 at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, in first place at 17.59 petaflops, built with 18,688 Nvidia Tesla K20X accelerators. That is a real achievement and it is also, commercially, a small market. National laboratories buy a handful of machines a decade.

What the shareholders saw was different. The Motley Fool, writing in February 2014 and looking back, described a company that as recently as mid-2012 faced a falling share price alongside a rising cash pile, with cash accounting for more than forty per cent of its entire market value, and a share count that had risen from under five hundred million in 2004 to more than six hundred million by 2012. In November 2012 the company extended a dormant repurchase programme and started paying a dividend. That is what a board does when it has run out of ways to explain itself.

It is worth saying plainly here, once, what this book will not do with any of that. It offers no view on whether the shares were cheap then or are cheap now, no price target, and no recommendation. The historical share price move is evidence about how the investment was perceived at the time. It is not advice, and it is not a prediction.

## 2012: the tooling was already there

In October 2012 a system called AlexNet won the ImageNet image classification competition. Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton reported a top-five error rate of 15.3 per cent against a previous best of 26.2 per cent — a margin large enough that the result could not be argued away as tuning. Their paper, published in the 2012 conference proceedings, describes a network of roughly sixty million parameters trained for five to six days on two GeForce GTX 580 cards, each with three gigabytes of memory.

Read that sentence as an accountant reads a purchase order. Two consumer graphics cards. Not a supercomputer, not a national facility, not a grant-funded cluster. The hardware that produced the most consequential result in machine learning for a generation was available at retail.

Set beside it the alternative approach of the same year. In June 2012 Google's research team reported a large neural network built across sixteen thousand processors that had, among other things, taught itself to recognise cats from unlabelled video, a result covered at the time by NPR and widely elsewhere. Shortly afterwards Bryan Catanzaro of Nvidia's research group worked with Andrew Ng's group at Stanford on the same class of problem using graphics processors; the work, presented in 2013, showed that a handful of graphics-based servers could stand in for a thousand conventional ones, and Nvidia's own account of the collaboration puts twelve of its processors at the deep-learning throughput of two thousand central processors. Comparisons of that kind depend heavily on what is being compared and should be read as an order of magnitude, not a measurement. The order of magnitude was the story.

Here is the part that is usually told backwards. The researchers who turned to graphics processors in 2012 did not evaluate the options and select Nvidia. There was no evaluation to run. The work was numerically simple and enormously parallel — the same multiply-and-add, performed across very large arrays, over and over — which is precisely the shape of work the wide machine was built for. And there was exactly one way to write that code without a graphics degree, because one company had spent six years building it and giving it away, and had put it in the hands of every university department that owned a gaming card.

The moment did not create the platform. It found it, sitting there, finished enough to use.

## Libraries accumulate

What happened next is the mechanism that turns an advantage into a moat, and it is worth watching closely because it is not obvious.

Once researchers were writing in CUDA, Nvidia stopped competing on the language and started competing on what came with it. Rather than let every laboratory write its own matrix routines, the company shipped tuned ones. The decisive step for machine learning came on 7 September 2014, when Nvidia announced cuDNN, a library of primitives for building and training neural networks, hand-tuned for its processors; The Register and InfoQ both covered it that September, and it was integrated into the neural network toolkits of the day, Caffe among them.

The consequence is a change in what "switching" means, and it is the single most important idea in this chapter.

Before cuDNN, a researcher who wanted to move to another vendor had to rewrite their own program. Annoying, but finite — a few weeks, perhaps.

After cuDNN, the researcher was not writing against the hardware at all. They were writing against a framework, which was written against the library, which was tuned against the chip. To move, somebody had to rewrite the framework, and match not only its behaviour but its speed, because a framework that gives the same answer three times slower is not a substitute. The switching cost had moved one level down and grown by an order of magnitude, and it had moved off the desk of the person who wanted to switch and onto the desk of somebody who had no particular reason to help.

Then it compounded. Each library that worked made the next default choice easier. Each published result that could be reproduced only on one stack made the stack the safe option for the next paper. Graduate students learned it because their supervisors' code was in it, and took it with them into industry. None of this required a single unfair act; it required only time, and time is the one input a competitor cannot buy.

An important corollary follows. The asset is not the source code. Nvidia's competitors could read the specifications, and the industry did not lack for clever engineers. The asset is the accumulated stock of tuned routines, the habits of hundreds of thousands of programmers, and the enormous published corpus of work that must remain reproducible. None of those three things can be reverse-engineered, and none of them appear on any balance sheet.

## Where the switching cost shows up in the accounts

It does not appear as an asset. That is the whole difficulty, and the whole opportunity for a reader who knows where else to look.

A switching cost shows up in two places. The first is gross margin, because a customer with nowhere to go negotiates differently from one with a live alternative. The second is the absence of a discount — visible not in what a company charges, but in what it does not have to give away to win business it might otherwise lose.

The turn is visible in the reported numbers, and it is fast. Nvidia's GAAP gross margin for fiscal 2016 was 56.1 per cent, with a non-GAAP figure of 56.8 per cent, per the company's results for that year. For the fourth quarter of fiscal 2017 it reported a record quarterly GAAP gross margin of 60.0 per cent. Revenue over the same span went from 5.01 billion dollars in fiscal 2016 to a record 6.91 billion in fiscal 2017, up thirty-eight per cent, per the results announcement of February 2017. Within that, the datacentre business — Tesla, GRID and the first DGX-1 systems — grew about one hundred and forty-five per cent across the year, with a record fourth quarter of 296 million dollars, up two hundred and five per cent on the same quarter a year earlier. The full-year datacentre total for fiscal 2017 was not verified for this chapter and so is not given.

Two cautions before anyone reads too much into a margin.

A gross margin can rise for reasons that have nothing to do with pricing power. Product mix changes; a company selling proportionally more of a high-margin line will show a higher blended margin without raising a single price. Manufacturing yields improve, which lowers cost without touching revenue. Component prices fall. Any of these will move the number. To tell pricing power from mix, you have to read the segment disclosure alongside it and ask whether the margin improved within each segment or only in the average — a distinction the headline figure will never tell you.

And a margin of sixty per cent in a chapter about the mid-2010s should be held next to the figures already given in this book for the mid-2020s, because the gap between the two is the size of what happened next, and it belongs to a later chapter rather than this one.

There is a cleaner test than any margin, and it is a counterfactual. If the advantage were only in the device, you would expect a rival part with comparable specifications and a lower price to take share, because that is what happens in every other component market. If instead rival parts benchmark respectably, price aggressively and still struggle to displace the incumbent in the workloads that matter, then whatever is holding customers in place is not in the device. That is the observation to make. It is a good deal more reliable than any single financial ratio, and it can be made from the outside by anyone who reads the specifications and then reads the market shares.

## Why silicon alone could never have done this

A chip advantage has a known half-life. A competitor with a comparable process and a competent design team can close a generation's gap in a few years, and every design win has to be won again at the next socket. The industry has watched this happen repeatedly, in graphics and in every other category of component.

A software installed base does not work that way, for three reasons.

It is cumulative rather than periodic. Each year's libraries do not replace last year's; they sit on top of them. The stock grows and never resets at a product transition.

It is held by people who are not the buyer. The person who would have to do the rewriting is a framework maintainer, a research group, a compiler team — none of whom take instructions from the procurement department that would like a cheaper part.

And it is reinforced by the requirement that published results be reproducible. In a field where a paper's claim must be checkable, the toolchain the paper used has a gravitational pull that no purchase order can counteract.

The strategy was not secret and the answer was not hidden. AMD announced a new version of ROCm, its open-source platform for graphics computing, on 14 November 2016, and the Khronos standard had existed since 2008. The obstacle was never that nobody knew what to build. It was that the thing being built takes about ten years and shows up in the accounts as expense the entire time.

The honest counterweight, which this book owes the reader, is that none of this is permanent. Three things could erode it, and each is worth watching rather than assuming.

Frameworks are themselves an abstraction, and an abstraction that hides the hardware from the programmer can eventually hide which hardware. The more machine learning code is written against a framework rather than against a device, the less the device's native language matters. That is a slow process, and it works against the incumbent.

Buyers of sufficient scale can afford to do the porting themselves. A company purchasing accelerators by the hundred thousand has an arithmetic reason to fund a software team that would be absurd for a university department, and the same company has an incentive to design its own silicon. Later chapters deal with the custom-silicon programmes in detail.

And inference is not training. A moat built around the tooling for research and training is weakest at the point where a trained model is simply being run, many times, as cheaply as possible — a workload where the software surface is narrower and the buyer's only question is cost per unit of output.

None of these is a prediction. They are the places where, if the position weakens, it will weaken first.

## What to watch, and where

For a reader who wants to follow this rather than take it on trust, the evidence is public and mostly free.

| Signal | Where to find it | What a change would mean |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin, by segment rather than blended | Quarterly results and the segment note | Sustained pressure within a segment suggests customers have found an alternative |
| Research and development as a share of revenue | Income statement, several years side by side | Tells you what a company is buying that it cannot yet sell |
| Which framework a major laboratory's published code targets | Public code repositories accompanying papers | The most direct measure of where the default sits |
| Whether a large buyer announces its own silicon and its own software stack | Company announcements and capital expenditure guidance | Silicon without a software stack is a bargaining posture; both together is a commitment |
| Developer population claims | Company conference statements, with the definition attached | Useful only if the definition is stable between statements |

That last row deserves its caveat spelled out, because the numbers get repeated carelessly. Nvidia's chief executive said in June 2024, at Computex in Taipei, that the company had five million CUDA developers, as reported by MarketScreener; the company's material for its March 2026 developer conference, marking twenty years of the platform, referred to six million developers. Separately, Nvidia's own August 2025 statement of reaching two million registered developers noted that the first million took thirteen years and the second took under two, and put average monthly CUDA downloads at around 438,000. "Registered developers", "CUDA developers" and "downloads" are three different measures, counted by the company itself, unaudited, and not defined identically. They are indicative of direction and useless as precision. Any figure in this section is a snapshot from the date given and will have moved; the current versions live in the company's quarterly results and conference materials.

## The decade, in one line

The chapter closes where an accountant would want it to close: with the arithmetic of what was actually decided.

A company with roughly four to five billion dollars of annual revenue chose, for about a decade, to spend a substantial part of its research budget on a free product that made its chips more expensive to manufacture, in service of customers who did not exist, and it kept doing so through a nine-figure warranty charge, a financial crisis, a flat share price and the point at which its own cash exceeded forty per cent of its market value. Every year of that decade, the spending reduced reported profit and created no asset any auditor could recognise.

The same company reported revenue of 215.9 billion US dollars for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026, of which 197.3 billion was datacentre, per its results announcement of February 2026, and was the most valuable listed company in the world at roughly 5.3 to 5.4 trillion dollars in mid-August 2026, per the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings. Those figures are snapshots and will have moved by the time this is read.

The temptation is to call the earlier decision visionary and move on. Resist it, because the useful lesson is duller and more transferable. The decision was not verifiable at the time by any of the instruments an investor or a board has available. There was no line item to check, no asset to value, no revenue to extrapolate, and no benchmark against which to measure progress. The only evidence that it was working was that people who had no reason to be there kept turning up to use a free tool.

That is not an argument for tolerating every unprofitable software project a hardware company wishes to run; most of them are exactly what they look like. It is an argument for knowing the limits of the accounts. They will tell you, precisely and reliably, what a company has spent. They will not tell you what it has bought.
