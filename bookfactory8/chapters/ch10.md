# ASML: One Machine, No Substitute

On 15 January 2026 a company in Taiwan said it intended to spend more money than anyone expected, and a company in the Netherlands became worth half a trillion dollars.

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company raised its planned 2026 capital spending to between fifty-two and fifty-six billion US dollars, against market expectations of about forty-six billion according to analysts polled by Visible Alpha. ASML shares rose 5.4 per cent by 1106 GMT that day, taking the gain for January alone to 24 per cent, and the company's market value crossed five hundred billion US dollars for the first time, as reported by Reuters on 15 January 2026.

ASML announced nothing. It shipped nothing new, invented nothing, and signed no contract that the market learned about. Its customer changed one line in a capital budget, and half a trillion dollars of value was assigned to the supplier.

That is the cleanest available demonstration of what this chapter is about. ASML sits one step further down the stack than the foundry, and its demand is entirely derived. Nobody wants a lithography system. People want capacity, and capacity requires the system, and there is exactly one place to get the system that prints the finest features. When the buyer of capacity raises its budget, the arithmetic runs downhill automatically.

By June 2026 the same mechanism had carried further. ASML became Europe's most valuable listed company in history, at a market value reported at about six hundred and seventy-four billion US dollars, after a rise of roughly 60 per cent in the year to date driven by demand for exposure to data centre construction (Bloomberg, 3 June 2026, and Tom's Hardware, June 2026). As at August 2026 companiesmarketcap put the figure at roughly six hundred and seventy-three billion dollars. All of those numbers move daily and several of them will have moved by the time you read this. Look them up rather than trusting the page. Nothing in this chapter is a view on whether the shares are worth what they cost, and nothing in this book is investment advice.

The durable fact is the one underneath the price, and it does not move by Friday: there is no second source.

## A joint venture nobody wanted

ASML was established as ASM Lithography on 1 April 1984, a joint venture between Philips and ASM International, with a mandate to commercialise a wafer stepper — the PAS 2000 — that had been developed inside a Philips laboratory. It started with thirty-one employees working out of a temporary wooden shed beside that laboratory in Eindhoven, and settled in Veldhoven, where its headquarters remain (ASML's own published account of its founding, 2024, and industry histories of the company).

It is worth pausing on how unpromising that was. The venture existed because a large electronics company had built a machine it did not want to be in the business of selling, and had found a partner willing to take on the problem. Contemporary accounts describe the early years as close to a write-off: no commercial traction, and for a while not even a proper building. There is no version of this story in which a Dutch industrial start-up was obviously going to end up owning the narrowest passage in the world economy.

What it had instead was a very long time horizon and a supply chain it kept buying pieces of. In 2001 ASML acquired Silicon Valley Group, the last American lithography manufacturer, for about 1.6 billion US dollars, which gave it the United States research and manufacturing base it still operates at Wilton, Connecticut (as recorded in histories of the EUV programme, notably Brian Potter's account for Construction Physics). That is the pattern to hold on to. Almost every important thing ASML did over the following two decades was a decision to own, fund or lock in a supplier rather than to hope one would exist.

A company that assembles machines from parts it does not control has customers. A company that has quietly acquired or financed every irreplaceable part of its own supply chain has something else.

## The bet: twenty years, three national laboratories, and the customers' own money

Optical lithography works by shining light through a patterned mask onto a wafer coated in a light-sensitive film. The finer the feature you want to print, the shorter the wavelength you need. For a long generation the industry printed with deep ultraviolet light at 193 nanometres and got clever about the rest — more exposures, more masks, more tricks, each one adding cost and lowering yield.

The alternative was to move the wavelength itself, down to 13.5 nanometres: extreme ultraviolet, EUV. That is not a modest improvement on 193 nanometres. It is a different kind of physics. Light of that wavelength is absorbed by essentially everything, including air and including glass. You cannot build a lens for it. The whole optical path has to be mirrors, and the whole machine has to run in a vacuum, and the mirrors have to be built out of dozens of alternating layers of material engineered so that a small fraction of the light bounces rather than being swallowed.

In 1997 a lithography task force convened by SEMATECH, the American semiconductor research consortium, ranked EUV last of four candidate technologies, behind X-ray lithography, electron-beam and ion projection. Intel funded it anyway. It formed a consortium, EUV LLC, which contracted with the United States Department of Energy to run a research programme of roughly 250 million dollars at the Lawrence Livermore, Sandia and Lawrence Berkeley national laboratories from 1997. Motorola, AMD, IBM and Micron joined; Intel remained the largest and most influential member. ASML joined the consortium in 1999, and the work produced a first full-field EUV prototype in 2001 (Construction Physics, and contemporaneous industry press).

Then it took another two decades.

This is the part of the story that gets compressed in the retelling, and the compression is what makes ASML look lucky rather than stubborn. The gap between "we have demonstrated that this can print a pattern" and "we can sell a machine that will print several thousand wafers a week, every week, in a customer's factory, at a yield that pays for itself" was, in EUV's case, roughly twenty years of engineering. Most of it was the light source. A prototype that produces a few watts of EUV light is a laboratory result. A production tool needs hundreds of watts, delivered continuously, without contaminating the optics.

By 2012 the cost of finishing the job had grown beyond what ASML could fund out of its own revenue. What happened next is the single most instructive transaction in the company's history, and it is a financing structure rather than an invention.

ASML's three largest customers bought it. Intel, TSMC and Samsung together took an aggregate 23 per cent minority equity stake for 3.85 billion euros in cash, and separately committed 1.38 billion euros to fund ASML's research into next-generation lithography over five years. Samsung's portion, announced on 27 August 2012 and completing the programme, was 503 million euros of equity and 276 million euros of research funding. The shares issued were non-voting except in exceptional circumstances (ASML's announcements of the Customer Co-Investment Program, 2012).

Read that as an accountant. Three companies that compete ferociously with each other agreed to finance a supplier's research on the same terms, took equity they could not vote, and did it because each of them faced the same alternative: no EUV, and therefore a hard stop on the roadmap that their own businesses were built on. The programme is often described as a vote of confidence. It is better understood as three customers concluding that the tool had to exist and that no one else was going to pay for it.

Two further transactions closed the chain.

Three billion euros of equity and cash, spread across a decade, bought a supplier's control of the two hardest parts of its own machine and the funding to finish it. Whether that was expensive is not really a question that can be asked in isolation. There was no second bid.

There is a general lesson here that recurs throughout this book. When a customer funds a supplier's research, an accounting question follows immediately: is that money revenue, a reduction of research expense, or a liability carried until the work is delivered? The three answers produce three different reported margins from identical cash. Wherever you find customer-funded development in this industry — and you will find it repeatedly in the compute and model layers — find the note that says how it was treated before you compare the margin to anyone else's.

## What the machine is

The physical description matters here, because the argument of this chapter is that the absence of a competitor is a technical fact rather than a commercial one, and that claim can only be assessed if you know roughly what has to be built.

EUV light is made by hitting metal. In ASML's system, tin droplets are fired into a vacuum chamber at a rate of fifty thousand per second and struck by two consecutive pulses from a high-power carbon-dioxide laser supplied by TRUMPF, a German industrial laser company. At thirty kilowatts, that laser is described by its maker as the most powerful industrial laser in the world (TRUMPF, EUV drive laser materials; ZEISS SMT's published explanation of EUV lithography). The first pulse flattens the droplet; the second turns it into a plasma hot enough to radiate at 13.5 nanometres. This happens continuously, for years, inside a machine that must not be contaminated by its own tin.

The light is then collected and steered by an all-mirror optical system built by Carl Zeiss SMT in Oberkochen, in Baden-Württemberg, operating in vacuum, using multilayer-coated mirrors. Zeiss has published the standard description of the tolerance involved: if one of these mirrors were scaled up to the size of Germany, the largest bump on it would be about a tenth of a millimetre. The vapour deposition technique behind the multilayer coating alone took more than twenty years of research to develop, and ZEISS SMT marked thirty years of EUV optics work in 2025.

Source power is the number that decided whether any of it was commercially usable, because power determines how many wafers an hour the tool can expose, and throughput determines cost per wafer. ASML's source reached 250 watts in 2017, enough for 125 wafers per hour, and the current benchmark cited in industry coverage is around 600 watts (industry analysis of EUV light sources, 2025 and 2026 reporting).

Then there is the next generation. High numerical aperture EUV — High-NA — uses larger, differently shaped optics to print finer features in a single exposure. ASML's first commercial High-NA system is the TWINSCAN EXE:5000; the second-generation EXE:5200B improves output, overlay accuracy and the light source. Intel took delivery of the first system in 2024 and installed it at its Hillsboro, Oregon campus, and in July 2026 became the first manufacturer to ship high-volume logic products made using High-NA, in its Core Ultra Series 3 processors on the 18A process (Quartz and SiliconANGLE, July 2026).

The prices are worth stating carefully, because they are trade-press figures rather than list prices, and ASML sells to a handful of customers on negotiated terms. High-NA systems were reported at about 380 million US dollars around announcement, against roughly 180 million dollars for a standard EUV machine on the same reporting, and about 170 million euros for a low numerical-aperture system on other trade coverage (Tom's Hardware and TrendForce, 2024), with 2026 coverage putting the most advanced tools above 400 million euros.

A single machine, then, costs about what a mid-sized office tower costs, is delivered in pieces, takes months to install and qualify, and will be serviced by its maker for the rest of its working life.

## Why there is no second source

Start with the market shares, which are unusually easy to state. ASML supplies 100 per cent of the world's EUV lithography systems, because no other company makes one. Across all lithography categories including older deep-ultraviolet and i-line tools, industry estimates put its share closer to 90 per cent (Motley Fool industry estimates, cited in 2026 coverage).

Nikon and Canon, which between them dominated lithography in earlier decades, abandoned EUV development in the late 2010s and now compete at older wavelengths. Canon markets nanoimprint lithography systems that claim resolution equivalent to advanced nodes at lower power and cost, but adoption is early and concentrated in memory and specialty optical applications (industry analysis of the lithography market, 2026). Neither company is absent from the leading edge because it lacked ambition. Both were serious optical manufacturers with decades of experience and large balance sheets.

So the interesting question is not why ASML won. It is why nobody can now arrive.

A rival would need, simultaneously, four things that cannot be bought and cannot be developed quickly. It would need a light source that turns tens of thousands of tin droplets a second into plasma without destroying the optics, which is the problem that took ASML from a prototype in 2001 to production two decades later, and which it eventually solved by buying the supplier outright in 2013. It would need optics, and the firm that makes them is part-owned by the incumbent under an agreement dating from 2016. It would need the accumulated process knowledge — not the design of the machine, which can in principle be understood, but the thousands of undocumented adjustments that make a particular tool hold its specification through a particular customer's process, knowledge that exists only in the heads of people who have done it and in the service records of machines that have run.

And it would need export licences, which are granted by governments and are discussed in the next section.

The financial dimension of the barrier is the one most often missed. Building a rival is not principally an engineering budget problem; it is a customer problem. The three companies with both the money and the motive to fund a competing EUV programme are Intel, TSMC and Samsung, and in 2012 all three chose to fund this one instead, taking equity in the incumbent as they did so. A new entrant would have to persuade those same customers to finance a second twenty-year programme, at a moment when the first one is finally working and their capacity plans depend on it. Chapter nine explained why a fab must run full; a fab equipped with an unproven lithography tool does not run at all.

That is what "no substitute" means in this book. Not that ASML is unbeatable in principle, but that the specific combination of an unsolved physics problem, a single optics supplier, two decades of undocumented process learning, and government control over who may buy the result, cannot be assembled by anyone in less time than the current technology cycle lasts.

## What it looks like in the accounts

Now read the numbers, because the strategic position eventually has to show up as a line item or it is a story rather than an analysis.

ASML reported total net sales of 32.7 billion euros and net income of 9.6 billion euros for 2025, with a gross margin of 52.8 per cent, in its fourth-quarter and full-year results published on 28 January 2026. EUV system sales rose 39 per cent to 11.6 billion euros, on 48 EUV systems recognised in the year against 44 in 2024. Revenue for the year was up about 16 per cent (Counterpoint Research's analysis of the 2025 results, 2026).

Three things in that table deserve attention beyond their size.

The first is the size of the guidance revision. In January 2026 the company guided 2026 sales to between 36 and 40 billion euros at a gross margin of 51 to 53 per cent. By 15 July it was guiding 43 to 45 billion euros at 54 to 56 per cent. That is not a rounding adjustment; it is a materially different year, revised upward twice within six months. Guidance in this industry has been drifting upward rather than down, which is a fact about the current cycle and not a promise about the next one. Chapter sixty-three deals with what happens when it drifts the other way.

The second is that margin moved with volume, which is what you would expect in a business whose costs are dominated by research, engineering and a fixed manufacturing base. The company also told investors in July 2026 that it already had substantially all the orders it needed for 2027 and a large number for 2028 (ASML second-quarter 2026 earnings call, 15 July 2026). In a business that sells a few hundred systems a year, revenue arrives in large uneven lumps, and a quarter can be moved by whether a machine was accepted on the final day of it or the first day of the next. Read the year, not the quarter.

The third is the backlog. ASML ended 2025 with a backlog of 38.8 billion euros, after quarterly net bookings of 13.2 billion euros in the fourth quarter, of which 7.4 billion euros was EUV (results published 28 January 2026). For a company with roughly a year of revenue already ordered, backlog is the closest thing to visibility that exists anywhere in this stack.

### The installed base is the annuity

The line that most casual readers skip is the one that makes the business durable. ASML does not only sell machines; it services, upgrades and supplies the machines already installed. That business — service contracts, spares, software and performance upgrades — was put at 8.2 billion euros in 2025, growing at about 26 per cent, in Counterpoint Research's analysis of the results.

Why that matters is easiest to see with round numbers.

Suppose the tool sells for 200 million currency units and costs 100 million to build: a gross margin of 50 per cent on the sale, earned once. Suppose it then runs for fifteen years, and over that period the maker sells the owner 6 million a year of service and spares at an 80 per cent margin, plus two mid-life upgrades at 20 million each at a 60 per cent margin. The sale contributes 100 million of gross profit, in one lump, in one year. The service contributes 72 million and the upgrades 24 million — 96 million more, arriving slowly, in years when nobody may be ordering new machines at all.

That is the shape of the real business, and it explains something that puzzles people who look only at unit shipments. The installed base converts a cyclical capital-goods company into something closer to an infrastructure company with a cyclical order book attached. It also compounds: every machine shipped this year raises the floor under revenue for the next fifteen. When you assess any equipment maker in this layer — and chapter eleven looks at the others — separate system sales from installed base revenue before you form a view of the margin.

### What the reader lost when the bookings line went away

One disclosure change is worth recording plainly, without any suggestion of impropriety. ASML no longer discloses quarterly net bookings, so no bookings figure for the second quarter of 2026 exists to be quoted (as noted in coverage of the second-quarter 2026 results, July 2026). Companies are entitled to decide what they disclose beyond the required minimum, and a lumpy quarterly order number can mislead as easily as it informs — one large order slipping across a quarter-end can produce a headline that says nothing about demand.

But the reader should notice what has been given up. Bookings were a leading indicator; revenue is a lagging one; backlog sits between them and is now disclosed less frequently. The practical response is to watch the annual backlog figure, the company's own statements about how much of future years is already ordered, and the capital expenditure guidance of the three or four customers who account for most of the demand. That last one is why the January 2026 episode worked the way it did: with the supplier disclosing less, the customer's budget became the signal.

One more item belongs in the accounts, and it points forward to Part Six. In September 2025 ASML invested 1.3 billion euros — about 1.5 billion US dollars — in the French artificial intelligence company Mistral AI, taking a stake of about 11 per cent and becoming its largest shareholder, in a round that valued Mistral at roughly 11.7 billion euros, with ASML's chief financial officer Roger Dassen joining Mistral's strategic committee (CNBC and Bloomberg, 9 September 2025). A lithography company holding the largest stake in a model company is a small example of a pattern this book returns to repeatedly: at this point in the cycle, cash generated at the bottom of the stack is being invested back up it.

## The licence that is not on the balance sheet

Everything above describes a commercial position of remarkable strength. This section describes the thing that could remove a third of it by administrative decision, and it has no carrying value in any financial statement.

ASML's most advanced machines require export licences. The Dutch government approved the export of EUV technology to China in 2018, but from 2019 the United States and Dutch governments worked together to keep EUV systems out of China, and ASML's most advanced products were already effectively barred well before any formal broadening of the rules. On 30 June 2023 the Dutch government published new semiconductor export controls, effective 1 September 2023, extending licence requirements to certain deep-ultraviolet immersion systems (contemporaneous reporting, 2023). In January 2024 ASML said it had been blocked from shipping some machines to Chinese customers (CNBC, 2 January 2024). In September 2024 the Dutch trade minister Reinette Klever extended the licensing requirement to the 1970i and 1980i immersion systems (reported 6 September 2024).

The financial consequence is visible in the mix. China accounted for 41 per cent of ASML's sales in 2024 and 33 per cent in 2025 — 36 per cent in the fourth quarter of 2025 alone — and the company guided China's share to about 20 per cent of revenue in 2026, expecting China sales to decline significantly against the very strong business it did there in 2024 and 2025 (ASML's 2025 results and accompanying commentary, January 2026, as reported by Caixin Global and the South China Morning Post). At the same time it guided total 2026 revenue up, not down. The demand replacing it comes from customers building capacity for artificial intelligence.

That is the exposure stated factually: roughly a third of a company's revenue, in a single geography, governed by licences that three governments have modified four times in six years, and which are not an asset the company owns. Reported proposals in the United States during 2026 to tighten restrictions further moved the shares. This book takes no position on whether any of these measures are wise. It records that the risk is real, that it is political rather than commercial, and that it cannot be hedged by the company that bears it.

There is a symmetrical exposure on the other side, and it should be described with the same care. Since December 2025 there has been reporting that a state-backed Chinese consortium coordinated by Huawei and Shenzhen SiCarrier operated a functional EUV prototype in Shenzhen, using laser-induced discharge plasma rather than the laser-produced plasma architecture ASML uses, with the Harbin Institute of Technology responsible for the light source, the Changchun Institute of Optics for the optical system and Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment for integration, and more than three thousand researchers involved. The reported light output is 100 to 150 watts, against the 250 watts ASML's source achieved in 2017 and a current benchmark of around 600 watts. Reporting put Beijing's target for chip production using the tool at 2028, with sources quoted in the same coverage suggesting 2030 was more realistic (Asia Times, December 2025, and subsequent industry analysis through 2026).

Treat all of that as reporting, because that is what it is. None of it has been independently verified, no such tool is known to have printed production wafers, and the gap between a prototype and a qualified production system is precisely the twenty-year gap this chapter has just described in ASML's own case. What it does establish is the honest form of the claim. "There is no substitute" is a statement about the present, held in place by physics, by one optics supplier, by accumulated practice and by law. Three of those four can in principle change, and one of them — the law — can change in a week.

## What to watch

The figures in this chapter are snapshots. The market value was five hundred billion dollars in January 2026, about six hundred and seventy-four billion in June, roughly six hundred and seventy-three billion on companiesmarketcap in August. Guidance was raised twice in six months. None of those numbers will be current when you read them, and looking them up takes less time than reading this paragraph.

What is worth following, in order of how much it tells you:

Backlog and the company's statements about how much of the coming two years is already ordered, since bookings are no longer published quarterly. EUV and High-NA units recognised, rather than euros, because unit counts are harder to flatter with mix. The installed base management line, which tells you what the business earns when nobody is buying. China's share of sales against the roughly 20 per cent guided for 2026, which is where export policy becomes an income statement. And the capital expenditure guidance of the small number of customers who determine the order book — the January 2026 episode showed that this can be the most informative disclosure of all, and it comes from somebody else's results announcement.

The company employed more than forty-four thousand full-time equivalent staff according to its 2025 annual report, drawn from 143 nationalities, most of them in the Netherlands, the United States and Taiwan. It is not a large company by the standards of the businesses that depend on it. It is simply the only one, and for the reasons set out above, that is a fact about optics, plasma physics, institutional memory and export law rather than about pricing power — which is why it has lasted, and why it is more fragile in one specific respect than any commercial advantage would be.
