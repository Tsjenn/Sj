# The Four Layers

Someone types a question into a chat box at eleven at night. Forty words, a blue button, an answer that begins arriving before the sentence has finished being read. Nothing about that transaction looks industrial. Yet between the click and the answer, money moves through four separate businesses, each with its own accounts, its own margins and its own reason to believe it holds the scarce thing.

This book follows that money. Not the technology — there are good accounts of the technology — but the invoices. Who bills whom, for what, on what terms, and which of them is left holding an asset when the excitement subsides. The question that organises the whole book is the one an accountant asks first and last: where does the margin actually end up, and what does it cost to keep it there?

## Following one payment down

Start with the payment itself, because it is the only part of the chain the reader has direct experience of.

A consumer subscription to a frontier chat assistant costs twenty US dollars a month. That has been the standard tier for some time: ChatGPT Plus at twenty dollars a month with a two-hundred-dollar Pro tier above it, and Claude Pro at twenty dollars a month with a team seat at thirty dollars per user per month, per the published pricing compared across vendors in 2026. Prices change; the shape has been stubborn. Twenty dollars a month is ordinary consumer-software pricing, inherited from the last decade of subscription apps rather than derived from what the service costs to run. Keep that in mind. It becomes important three paragraphs from now.

Now follow the twenty dollars.

The company that took the payment must answer the question. Answering costs compute — a fractional slice of a machine, running for a few seconds, several thousand times a day across the customer base. That cost is real, it is recognised in cost of revenue, and it is the single biggest determinant of whether the twenty dollars becomes profit or evaporates.

How real? For Anthropic, figures shared with investors and reported in 2026 by PitchBook and Morningstar put compute at about seventy-one cents for every dollar of revenue in the first quarter of 2026, with a projected fifty-six cents in the second, and gross margin tracking around forty-four per cent. For OpenAI, gross margin was reported at roughly thirty-nine per cent in the first quarter of 2026, up from about thirty-three per cent a year earlier. Both companies are private. Neither figure is audited, neither has been filed with a regulator, and both should be read as what they are: numbers that reached the press through people who had reasons to share them. They are indicative, not authoritative. Chapter five deals at length with how to read a company that has no accounts.

Hold the caveat, but keep the order of magnitude. Somewhere between a third and two-thirds of the twenty dollars leaves the model layer almost immediately and goes down one floor, to the people who own the machines.

The compute layer takes it and buys hardware, buildings and electricity. In 2026 the four largest buyers — Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta — were on course to spend approaching seven hundred billion US dollars between them on capital expenditure, as reported by CNBC in February 2026; other trackers running the same guidance arrived at about seven hundred and twenty-five billion, against a prior-year record of roughly four hundred and ten billion. Company by company, guidance in 2026 put Amazon around two hundred billion, Microsoft near one hundred and ninety billion, Alphabet at one hundred and seventy-five to one hundred and eighty-five billion, and Meta at one hundred and twenty-five to one hundred and forty-five billion after raising its range. Those are guidance figures, not outturns, and guidance in this industry has drifted upward rather than down.

Most of that money goes down one more floor. It buys accelerators, memory, networking and the machines that make them. Nvidia's fiscal year 2026, which ended on 25 January 2026, produced revenue of two hundred and fifteen point nine billion US dollars, up sixty-five per cent, with data centre revenue of one hundred and ninety-seven point three billion against one hundred and fifteen point two billion the year before, GAAP operating income of one hundred and thirty point four billion and net income of one hundred and twenty point one billion, per the company's results announcement of February 2026 and its Form 10-K for the year ended 25 January 2026.

And Nvidia in turn pays a foundry to manufacture what it designs, and that foundry pays a Dutch company for the only machines in the world capable of printing the finest features. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion US dollars, above analyst expectations, in its January 2026 results. A low numerical-aperture EUV lithography system has been reported at around one hundred and seventy million euros; the newer High-NA Twinscan EXE systems were announced at about three hundred and eighty million US dollars, with 2026 reporting putting the most advanced tools above four hundred million euros.

That is the whole chain, from a twenty-dollar subscription to a four-hundred-million-euro machine. Four floors. The book is organised on them.

## The four layers

| Layer | What it sells | Who pays it | What it must own to matter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Application | An outcome a user or business wants — an answer, a draft, a completed task | End users and enterprises, mostly by subscription or seat | Distribution, workflow, the customer relationship |
| Model | Intelligence as a metered service, by token or by seat | Applications, enterprises, and its own consumer tier | Research talent, training compute, a brand users trust |
| Compute | Time on machines, by the hour or the year | Model builders, enterprises, governments | Capital, power, land, and a place in the chip queue |
| Semiconductor | The physical device and the tools to make it | Everyone above, ultimately | Process technology, fabs, and irreplaceable equipment |

Two things about this frame need saying immediately, before it hardens into something it is not.

The first is that no important company sits neatly on one floor. Alphabet operates on all four: it sells applications to consumers, sells Gemini as a model, rents Google Cloud by the hour, and designs its own tensor processing units. Amazon sells applications, rents the largest cloud, designs silicon, and is one of the largest investors in a model company. Nvidia designs chips, and has also become a significant investor in the companies that buy them. The layers describe activities, not corporate boundaries. Where a company straddles floors, the interesting question is usually which floor its profit really comes from — and the segment note in the accounts is where that argument gets settled.

The second is that the stack is a simplification chosen for a purpose. It leaves out the memory makers, who deserve and get their own chapters; the power industry, which has quietly become a constraint; the networking vendors; the assemblers. Every one of those appears later. The four layers are a filing system, not a theory of everything.

### Bottom floor: where it is physically possible

The semiconductor layer is the only one where the constraint is physics rather than money. A logic transistor at the leading edge cannot be produced by wanting it badly. It requires a fab that takes years to build, a process that takes years to qualify, and lithography tools that come from a single supplier with no substitute.

This is also the layer with the longest planning horizon. Capacity decided in 2026 arrives in 2028 or 2029. That lag is the source of nearly every boom and bust the industry has ever had, and it is why the semiconductor cycle gets its own part of this book. When you read that a fab operator has raised its capital expenditure, you are not reading news about this year. You are reading a forecast about the year after next, expressed in concrete.

### Second floor: where it runs

The compute layer converts capital into rentable hours. It buys accelerators by the tens of thousands, puts them in buildings, connects them, powers them, cools them, and sells access.

The scale is now visible in the utility statistics. The International Energy Agency's 2024 electricity report projected that data centre consumption, including cryptocurrency mining, could exceed one thousand terawatt-hours in 2026; its later Energy and AI analysis put global data centre demand at around nine hundred and forty-five terawatt-hours by 2030. Set those two side by side and notice that they disagree — different scopes, different vintages, different assumptions. That disagreement is not a flaw in the sources. It is the ordinary condition of forecasting a fast-moving industry, and a reader who does not check the scope of a number will happily quote two incompatible ones in the same paragraph.

The compute layer is where accounting stops being bookkeeping and starts being argument. A machine bought this year is charged against profit over several years, and the number of years is an estimate made by management. Chapter thirty-two is devoted to that single estimate, because it decides more reported profit in this industry than any other judgement.

### Third floor: where intelligence is made

The model layer is the youngest and the strangest. Two private companies now sit close to the valuations of listed multinationals. Anthropic closed a sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, as reported by CNBC in May 2026. OpenAI closed a one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round in March 2026 at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, with Amazon reported as investing fifty billion dollars in that round, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes.

Their revenue is growing at rates that have no obvious precedent in enterprise software. Anthropic told investors its annualised revenue run rate had climbed above sixty-five billion dollars in July 2026, reported by CNBC in August 2026. OpenAI's annualised run rate was reported at around forty billion dollars as at August 2026.

Note the words "annualised run rate". That is a monthly figure multiplied by twelve. For a company growing this fast it is not the same as revenue for the year, it is not audited, and it is not comparable to the trailing twelve-month figures reported by listed companies. Comparing a run rate with an audited annual result is one of the commonest errors in coverage of this industry, and this book will keep flagging it.

### Top floor: where the user pays

The application layer is where the money enters the system. Every dollar in every layer below arrives, eventually, from somebody at the top buying something: a subscription, a seat, an API call embedded in a product they already pay for, or an advertisement funded by a business that hopes to sell something else.

This is also the layer with the least protection. The subscription price has converged across vendors. Switching is a matter of cancelling one direct debit and starting another. Enterprises negotiate, and their negotiating position improves every time a competent competitor appears. The application layer has the closest relationship with the customer and the weakest claim on scarcity, and the book's central argument follows from that tension.

## Why the value has been moving down

The conventional expectation, at the start of a technology cycle, is that value accrues upward. The infrastructure becomes cheap and commoditised, and the companies that own the customer relationship take the profit. That is roughly what happened with personal computers, with web hosting, and with mobile applications.

So far, this cycle has run the other way.

The clearest evidence is in the market's own ranking. As at mid-August 2026, Nvidia was the world's most valuable company at roughly five point three to five point four trillion US dollars, per the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings, with Apple at roughly four point four one trillion, Alphabet at roughly four point two zero trillion and Microsoft at roughly three point six six trillion over the same period and from the same sources. Below them, TSMC stood at roughly two point one seven trillion US dollars as at August 2026 on companiesmarketcap, Broadcom at roughly one point six one trillion and AMD at roughly two hundred and sixty billion in early 2026 per statista and alpha-sense rankings, and ASML crossed five hundred billion US dollars in market value for the first time in January 2026, per Reuters and Yahoo Finance. All of those figures move daily, which is a point the next section takes seriously.

A ranking is not an argument, though. The argument is in the margins.

| Company or layer | Gross margin | Period | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nvidia | 71.1 per cent GAAP | Fiscal year ended 25 January 2026 | Audited annual results, February 2026 |
| Nvidia | 75.0 per cent GAAP | Quarter ended January 2026 | Company results announcement, February 2026 |
| TSMC | 67.7 per cent | Second quarter 2026 | Company results, up 9.1 points year on year |
| TSMC | 66.2 per cent | First quarter 2026 | Company results, April 2026 |
| Anthropic | around 44 per cent | Tracking, first half 2026 | Reported from investor figures, 2026; unaudited |
| OpenAI | around 39 per cent | First quarter 2026 | Reported 2026; unaudited |

Read down that column. The company whose product is most easily described — a physical device — earns roughly seventy cents of gross profit on the dollar. The companies whose product sounds most like magic earn something closer to forty, and only after several years of falling inference costs. The two sets of figures are not strictly comparable — one set is audited and one is not, the periods differ, and what each company includes in cost of revenue differs — but the gap is far too wide to be an artefact of definitions.

The mechanism is scarcity, and scarcity in this cycle lives at the bottom.

At the top of the stack, a customer can switch this afternoon. In the middle, the leading models have converged enough in everyday capability that price and reliability increasingly decide the purchase, and the price per unit of output has fallen steeply. One floor down, compute is closer to a commodity than the model layer would like, but its supply is fixed years in advance by construction schedules and grid connections. At the bottom, there is one company printing the finest features on a wafer at volume, one company supplying the machines that do the printing, and a small number of firms making the memory that feeds an accelerator. Substituting any of them is a project measured in years and tens of billions.

Where supply cannot respond, price does. That is the entire argument, and it is not new economics — it is the oldest economics there is, wearing a new suit.

There is a fair case against, and this book puts it properly later rather than dismissing it here. The compute layer is not simply passing money through: Google Cloud reported second-quarter 2026 revenue of twenty-four point eight billion dollars, up eighty-two per cent, with operating income of eight point eight billion on a thirty-five point six per cent operating margin and a sales backlog of five hundred and fourteen billion dollars against one hundred and six billion a year earlier, per reporting from The Motley Fool and The Globe and Mail in August 2026; AWS grew thirty-seven per cent to forty-two point two billion in the same quarter and Azure grew forty-three per cent. Those are not the numbers of a business being squeezed. And the bottom of the stack pays for its margin with the worst cyclicality in the industrial economy: capacity ordered into a shortage arrives, reliably, into a glut.

The claim in this book is therefore specific and bounded. Value has moved down the stack during this cycle, for reasons that can be read in the accounts. It is not a law of nature, it has reversed before in other industries, and the last part of the book is about what would make it reverse again.

## The method: five questions per company

Every company chapter in this book asks the same five questions, in the same order. The order matters, because each answer constrains the next.

**Origin.** Where the company came from, and what it was built to do before it was built to do this. Nvidia's early backers in 1993 were Sequoia Capital and Sutter Hill Ventures — not SoftBank, a correction worth making early because several popular accounts get it wrong. Origins explain culture, and culture explains why a company said no to something obvious.

**Strategy.** What the company is actually trying to own, as distinct from what it says in its marketing. Strategy is best read backwards from where the capital goes.

**Accounts.** What the financial statements say, in the order an accountant reads them: revenue quality first, then margin, then the balance sheet, then cash. This is the section other books do not have, and it is the reason this one exists.

**Challenge.** The thing that could go wrong, described mechanically rather than dramatically. Concentration, cyclicality, a customer building its own version, an accounting estimate that turns out to be optimistic.

**Opportunity.** What is genuinely open to the company, with the constraint attached. Not a forecast, and never a share price.

That last point is worth stating once, plainly, so it does not need repeating in every chapter. This book is not investment advice. It contains no price targets, no recommendation to buy or sell anything, and no prediction of where any share price is going. Its ambition is narrower and, hopefully, more useful: to teach a reader to open a filing and work it out for themselves.

## Six line items that do most of the work

Most of what matters about a company in this industry can be found in six places. Learn where they sit, and the rest of the book becomes a series of variations.

| Line item | What it really tells you | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | How scarce the thing being sold is | A margin rising while volume rises means pricing power, not efficiency |
| Capital expenditure | Management's forecast, in cash, of demand two years out | Guidance revised upward mid-year; capex growing faster than revenue |
| Depreciation and useful life | Where an assumption becomes reported profit | Any change in estimated useful life, and the quantified effect |
| Inventory and purchase commitments | What has been promised to suppliers before customers commit | Commitments growing faster than the order book |
| Customer concentration | How much of the business depends on a handful of decisions | A named or unnamed customer crossing ten per cent of revenue |
| Cash from operations against net income | Whether the profit is real | Profit rising while operating cash flow does not |

Two of those deserve a demonstration now, because they recur constantly.

### Depreciation: the assumption that becomes profit

When a company buys a server, it does not charge the cost against profit in the year it pays. It spreads the cost over the years it expects to use the machine. That expectation is an accounting estimate made by management, and changing it changes reported profit without changing a single thing about the business.

Take an illustrative company, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real firm. It spends twelve billion on servers in a year. Over a four-year life, that is three billion of annual depreciation. Extend the assumed life to six years and the charge falls to two billion. Reported operating profit rises by a billion, cash is identical, and the machines are exactly the same machines.

This is not a hypothetical concern. Microsoft extended the depreciable useful life of server and network equipment in its cloud infrastructure from four years to six with effect from the start of its 2023 fiscal year. Meta extended the useful life of certain servers and networking assets to five and a half years from the four to five years previously used, a change expected to reduce depreciation expense by two point nine billion dollars in 2025. Amazon went the other way, revising the useful life of certain servers and networking equipment from six years to five with effect from 1 January 2025, a change it disclosed on 7 February 2025 as expected to reduce 2025 operating income by zero point seven billion dollars.

Note what those three facts do and do not show. They show that the estimate is genuinely uncertain and that reasonable companies disagree about it. They do not show that anybody has done anything improper — each change was disclosed, quantified and audited, which is the system working as designed. The reason to watch the line is that the same fleet of accelerators can produce materially different reported profits depending on an assumption, and the assumption is the one part of the accounts that a reader can check for themselves in ten minutes.

### Customer concentration: who can end the party

Nvidia's Form 10-K for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026 disclosed four direct customers, each accounting for more than ten per cent of total revenue, together representing about sixty-one per cent of the business. Earlier in the same fiscal year, two unnamed customers accounted for close to forty per cent of quarterly revenue.

That is a disclosure, not an accusation. Concentration of this kind is a normal consequence of selling a scarce industrial input to the handful of firms that can afford it, and the customers in question are among the most creditworthy on earth. But a business where four decisions determine three-fifths of revenue is a different business from one with a hundred thousand customers, and it should be valued and stress-tested differently. Every one of those four customers is also, publicly, designing silicon of its own.

## Every number in this book has a date

Here is the rule that governs everything that follows, and the reason the sentences in this book are heavier with dates and attributions than most prose deserves to be.

A market capitalisation is a photograph, not a fact. It is the price of the last share traded, multiplied by the number of shares. It changes every second the market is open. When this chapter says that Nvidia was worth roughly five point three to five point four trillion US dollars as at mid-August 2026 on the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings, it is reporting the state of a moving object at a moment, from a named observer. By the time the sentence is read, it will be wrong in the third digit and possibly the first. That is not a defect to be apologised for. It is what the number is.

The same discipline applies with more force to private valuations. A nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation is not a market price. It is arithmetic: the price agreed for a slice of preferred stock, in a negotiated round, divided by the fraction of the company that slice represents. It carries whatever rights, preferences and protections the investors negotiated, none of which apply to ordinary shares, and it reflects the views of the specific investors who wrote the cheque rather than of a market of buyers and sellers. Chapter five takes this apart properly. For now, the habit to build is simple: when you see a valuation for a private company, ask who paid, for what, and when.

Three further habits go with it.

Check the fiscal year. Nvidia's fiscal 2026 ended in January 2026. Microsoft's fiscal year ends in June. Comparing "2026" across companies without checking what 2026 means to each of them produces confident nonsense.

Check whether a figure is audited, guided, reported or estimated. An audited annual result has been examined by an auditor. Guidance is management's forecast. Reported figures for private companies have reached the press from interested parties. Industry estimates are models. The 2026 high-bandwidth memory market was estimated at about fifty-four point six billion US dollars, up roughly fifty-eight per cent year on year, with SK Hynix holding the largest share and capacity reported sold out through 2026 across major suppliers, per industry analysis published in 2026. That is an estimate, and it is useful, and it is not the same kind of object as a filed income statement.

Check the denominator on a percentage. Year to date in 2026, Samsung was reported up about one hundred and fourteen per cent, SK Hynix about one hundred and eighty-six per cent and Micron about one hundred and forty-one per cent, per IG and industry reporting in mid-2026. Those are share-price moves from the start of a particular year, which is an arbitrary starting line chosen by the calendar. Change the starting date and the ranking can change with it.

Where a figure in this book cannot be verified, the book says so rather than filling the gap. Some things that would be genuinely useful to know are simply not disclosed: the price a specific cloud pays for a specific accelerator, the split of a model company's revenue between its consumer and enterprise businesses, the unit economics of a single data centre. Where that is the case, the text says it is not disclosed and explains what can be inferred from the disclosure that does exist — and where nothing can be, it says that too.

For anything current, the primary sources are where they have always been. Listed United States companies file annual reports on Form 10-K and quarterly reports on Form 10-Q. Foreign private issuers, including TSMC and ASML, file on Form 20-F and furnish results on Form 6-K. Every one of those companies publishes the same material on its own investor relations pages, usually with a slide deck and a transcript that are easier to read than the filing and less precise. Private companies file nothing, which is precisely why the chapters about them are written differently.

## How the book is arranged

The first part, of which this chapter is the opening, is the toolkit: what a market capitalisation is, how to read a chip company's accounts, how to read a cloud company's accounts, how to read a company with no accounts at all, and why the dating rule matters enough to have its own chapter.

The four middle parts take the layers from the bottom up — semiconductors, compute, models, applications — because that is the order in which the constraints bind. A chapter about an application company that has not established what its compute costs is a chapter about marketing.

The sixth part maps the ownership. This industry's companies do not merely trade with each other; they own pieces of each other, finance each other, and in some cases sell to businesses they have invested in. Amazon has committed up to thirty-three billion dollars to Anthropic and Google up to forty-three billion, including ten billion in April 2026, per 2026 reporting; those stakes have been reported by CNBC in August 2026 as distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them. SoftBank sold its entire remaining Nvidia stake in November 2025 for five point eight three billion US dollars to fund investment in OpenAI and Stargate, per CNBC in November 2025 — having previously built a roughly four point nine per cent stake in Nvidia through its Vision Fund in 2017 and sold it in January and February 2019 for about three point three billion dollars, a profit of roughly two point six billion, per CNBC in February 2019. Cross-holdings of this kind are legal, disclosed and increasingly central. They are also the structure that makes revenue hardest to read, and the book explains the mechanics without alleging anything that public disclosure does not support.

The seventh part is about cycles and risk: what has broken this industry before, what the bubble argument actually says, what the case against it says, and which specific failures would matter. The last part is about what remains true after the numbers in this book have gone stale.

They will go stale. The largest company on earth changed twice in the years before this was written, the second most valuable private company in the world was worth a fraction of its current mark eighteen months earlier, and the memory business went from oversupply to sold out in less time than it takes to build a fab. What should survive is not the figures but the method: find the layer, find the scarcity, read the margin, check the date.

Start at the bottom, where it is physically possible.
