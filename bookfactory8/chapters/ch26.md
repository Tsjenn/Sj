# Google Cloud and the TPU

In 2013, engineers at Google worked through an uncomfortable piece of arithmetic. If a hundred million Android users spoke to their phones for three minutes a day, and each of those minutes ran through the neural networks that turned speech into text, the computation required would exceed the capacity of every data centre Google operated — doubled. That was the projection Jeff Dean and his colleagues put in front of the company, by Google's own later account of the episode, and it reframed the problem entirely. This was not a research question about better models. It was a facilities question, and behind the facilities question a capital question: how many buildings full of processors do you want to own, and what will each unit of arithmetic inside them cost you?

Most companies facing that arithmetic would have ordered more of what they already had. Google decided to design a chip. The decision looks visionary in hindsight, and parts of it were, but it is better understood as a cost accountant would understand it: a make-or-buy decision, taken at a moment when the "buy" option was priced for a different job. The chip that resulted — the Tensor Processing Unit — is the reason Google now occupies a position no other company in this book holds. It operates on all four layers of the stack, it is the only hyperscaler that has run its own AI accelerator at scale for a decade, and it has begun, cautiously, to sell that accelerator to the very rivals and customers it once built it to avoid depending on anyone for.

This chapter explains what the TPU is, why it exists, what owning your own silicon does to a cost structure when demand explodes, and why the most vertically integrated company in artificial intelligence is nonetheless a very large customer of the layer below it.

## Fifteen months from decision to deployment

The TPU was not Google's first thought. The company had considered building a custom chip for neural networks as early as 2006, according to the technical paper its engineers later published; at that time the workloads were too small to justify it, and spare capacity in ordinary data centres absorbed the demand. The 2013 projection removed that comfort. Google started the design that year, and by its own account took the first TPU from drawing board to production deployment in about fifteen months — an unusually short cycle for a server chip — under a team whose lead architect was Norman Jouppi, a veteran processor designer.

The chips went into Google's data centres in 2015 and ran in silence. They accelerated translation, photo search and speech recognition for over a year before anyone outside the company knew they existed. Google announced the TPU publicly at its I/O conference in May 2016, and disclosed the engineering detail a year later in a paper presented at the ISCA computer-architecture conference in June 2017.

That paper is worth pausing on, because it explains what a TPU actually is better than any press release since. The first TPU was not a general-purpose processor and not a graphics chip. It was an accelerator for one job: inference, the running of an already-trained neural network. At its heart sat a matrix-multiplication unit of 65,536 eight-bit multiply-accumulate circuits — a large grid of very simple arithmetic elements — with a peak throughput of 92 trillion operations per second, per the 2017 paper. Neural networks spend most of their time multiplying grids of numbers together, and the insight behind the TPU is that a chip which does only that, at low precision, with the data marching through the grid in a fixed pattern, can be far more efficient than a chip which keeps options open.

The paper quantified the gain. Against the contemporary hardware in Google's own data centres — a server-class Intel CPU and an Nvidia K80 GPU — the TPU ran the company's production inference workloads on average fifteen to thirty times faster, and delivered thirty to eighty times more operations per watt, on benchmarks the authors said represented ninety-five per cent of Google's data-centre inference demand at the time. Those are Google's own measurements of Google's own workloads on hardware two generations out of date by publication, and they should be read with that caveat attached. But the shape of the result has held up: for the specific arithmetic of neural networks, specialised silicon beats general-purpose silicon by a wide margin, and the electricity bill notices even when the buyer of the chip does not.

The watt figure mattered more than the speed figure. Data centres are constrained by power before they are constrained by space, a subject the compute layer returns to repeatedly. A chip that does eight times the work per watt is, to a first approximation, eight data centres you do not have to build. That was the 2013 problem, answered.

## The make-or-buy decision

Set aside the engineering and consider the decision as a purchasing question, because that is what it was, and it is the question every large buyer of accelerators has faced since.

A merchant chip — one you buy from a vendor — has three great virtues. It exists now; you can order it this quarter and deploy it next year, and in a market moving this quickly, time is the scarcest input of all. It arrives with an ecosystem: software, libraries, tools, and a labour market full of engineers who already know how to use it, which for Nvidia's CUDA platform has been the deepest moat in the industry. And its risks are the vendor's: if the chip is late, or slow, or broken, you cancel the order rather than write off the programme.

Its one great vice is the invoice. A merchant chip is priced by the seller, and the seller in this market has had extraordinary pricing power. Nvidia's fiscal year 2026, ended 25 January 2026, produced revenue of 215.9 billion US dollars and GAAP operating income of 130.4 billion, per the company's results announcement of February 2026 — which is to say that roughly sixty cents of every dollar its customers paid was margin above the company's entire cost base, cost of goods, research and all. That is the arithmetic of buying from a monopolist in a shortage. Every accelerator a cloud company purchases carries the seller's economics inside it, and depreciates on the buyer's books at the full invoiced price, seller's margin included. Chapter thirty-two examines what that does to reported profit; here it is enough to note that the margin exists and someone pays it.

Custom silicon inverts every term. There is no seller's margin, because there is no seller: the chip reaches your data centre at something close to manufacturing cost plus your own design spend. But the design spend is enormous, it is committed years before the first chip works, and it buys you nothing transferable if the project fails. You must also build the software yourself — compilers, libraries, tooling — and persuade your own engineers, and eventually your customers' engineers, to use a stack the rest of the world does not. A custom chip programme is a bet that your future volume will be large enough, and stable enough, to amortise all of that fixed cost down to a unit economics that beats the merchant vendor's price.

To see the mechanism, take an illustrative case, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Suppose a merchant accelerator sells for 30,000 dollars and costs its maker 10,000 to produce. Suppose a custom alternative of similar capability could be manufactured for 12,000 dollars a unit — somewhat more, because the in-house designer lacks the vendor's scale — after a design programme costing two billion dollars. At ten thousand units, the custom route costs 320 million dollars in silicon plus the two-billion-dollar programme: a catastrophe next to a 300-million-dollar purchase order. At one million units, the custom route costs about 14 billion dollars all-in against 30 billion at the vendor's price. The crossover sits wherever the volumes and margins put it, but the principle is general: custom silicon is a fixed-cost machine, and fixed-cost machines reward exactly one thing — scale you are certain of.

In 2013 almost nobody on earth had that certainty. Google did, because it could see its own internal demand curve: search, translation, photos, speech, advertising models, all growing, all running on infrastructure it already owned, none of it dependent on winning an external customer. The first TPU did not need to beat Nvidia in the market. It needed to beat Nvidia's invoice inside Google's own buildings, for Google's own workloads, where the volume was guaranteed by the company's own products. That is why Google, alone, built an AI accelerator a decade before the AI boom: it is not that Google predicted 2023. It is that Google was the only company whose 2013 accounts already contained the problem.

## Ten years of generations

What began as an inference accelerator became a family. The record of public announcements runs as follows, and the dates are worth a table because the cadence itself is the point.

| Generation | Public disclosure | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| TPU v1 | Deployed 2015; announced May 2016 (Google I/O) | Inference only; eight-bit arithmetic; the 2017 ISCA paper documents it |
| TPU v2 | Announced 2017 | Added training, and with it the bfloat16 number format |
| TPU v3 | Announced May 2018 | Roughly doubled per-processor performance, per Google's announcement |
| TPU v4 / v5 family | Rolled out through the early 2020s | The generations Apple later disclosed using at scale |
| Trillium (v6) | Announced at Google I/O, May 2024; generally available late 2024 | A major widening of the matrix unit |
| Ironwood (v7) | Unveiled April 2025; general availability late November 2025 | Built for the inference era at pod scale |

Ironwood, the seventh generation, shows how far the line has travelled. A single Ironwood "pod" connects 9,216 liquid-cooled chips into one machine, each chip carrying 192 gigabytes of high-bandwidth memory — six times its predecessor — with Google claiming more than four times Trillium's per-chip performance, per the company's announcement and coverage by CNBC and HPCwire in November 2025. The claims are the vendor's own and unaudited by anyone, but the direction is not in dispute: the TPU is no longer a modest internal cost-saver. It is a frontier-scale training and inference machine, and the pod — not the chip — is the product.

Two disclosures along the way established that the TPU had become credible beyond Google's walls. In July 2024, Apple published a technical paper revealing that it had trained the foundation models behind Apple Intelligence on Google's TPUs — 8,192 TPU v4 chips for its server model and 2,048 v5p chips for the on-device one, as reported by CNBC in July 2024. Apple, a company with every incentive to avoid advertising a rival's infrastructure, had quietly chosen it anyway. And Google's own Gemini models are trained and served on TPUs, per the same 2024 reporting — meaning the flagship model of one of the world's largest AI companies runs end to end on silicon that no competitor designed and no vendor priced.

## What owning the silicon does when demand explodes

For most of the TPU's life, its financial effect was invisible. Google does not disclose what a TPU costs to make, what the programme has cost cumulatively, or what margin the chips save against buying merchant parts; none of that is in the accounts, and this book will not guess. What the accounts do show is the segment the TPU sits inside, and in 2026 that segment stopped being a footnote.

Google Cloud reported revenue of 24.8 billion US dollars for the second quarter of 2026, up eighty-two per cent year on year, with segment operating income of 8.8 billion against 2.8 billion a year earlier, per Alphabet's results reported by CNBC and Yahoo Finance in July 2026. The growth rate itself had been accelerating — reported at forty-eight per cent in the fourth quarter of 2025, sixty-three per cent in the first quarter of 2026, then eighty-two — which is the signature of a business whose constraint is supply, not demand. Alphabet raised its 2026 capital-expenditure guidance to 195 to 205 billion dollars at the same results, and its shares fell on the announcement, per CNBC's July 2026 coverage: the market applauding the revenue and wincing at what it costs to serve.

Sit with those two numbers — an operating margin of roughly a third on the quarter's cloud revenue, derived from the two figures above, and a capital budget of two hundred billion dollars — and the value of owning the silicon becomes visible even without a single disclosed TPU cost. When demand explodes, a cloud that buys merchant accelerators expands at the vendor's price, seller's margin included, in the vendor's queue. A cloud that owns its accelerator expands at manufacturing cost, in a queue it controls, on a chip tuned to its own workloads. Every incremental unit of capacity is cheaper than a rival's incremental unit, and the gap compounds precisely when volumes are largest — which is to say, precisely now. The decade of fixed cost that made the TPU look extravagant in 2016 is amortising across the steepest demand curve the industry has ever seen.

There is a second, quieter effect. A company that designs its own silicon chooses its own trade-offs. Merchant chips are designed for the average customer; the TPU is designed for Google's workloads, Google's data centres, Google's power envelopes. When the binding constraint shifted from chip supply to electricity — as it visibly has, and as a later chapter examines — the perf-per-watt obsession that produced the first TPU became an advantage again, for the same reason it was one in 2013.

None of this makes the TPU's economics knowable from outside. It is possible that the fully loaded cost of the programme, honestly accounted, would embarrass some of the enthusiasm. The point is narrower and stands on the public record: the only hyperscaler that spent a decade paying silicon's fixed costs entered the boom with a marginal cost of compute that no invoice from a vendor can touch, and its cloud segment's reported margin has expanded while its growth accelerated. Those are the disclosed facts, and they are consistent with the mechanism.

## The oddest position in the industry

Here is the strategic oddity the chapter's title promises. Alphabet is, by a distance, the most vertically integrated company in artificial intelligence. It operates on every layer of this book's stack: applications used by billions, a frontier model in Gemini, the third-largest cloud, and its own accelerator. No other company holds all four. And yet Alphabet is simultaneously one of the largest customers of the layer below it — in three separate ways, each instructive.

First, the TPU is not designed alone. Broadcom has been Google's design partner on the TPU programme across its generations, a relationship industry coverage in 2026 — including Tom's Hardware's May 2026 survey of the custom-silicon landscape — describes as the longest-standing of Broadcom's custom-chip engagements, sitting alongside newer ones for Meta, OpenAI and others. Google supplies the architecture and the workloads; Broadcom supplies the physical design, integration and much of the surrounding componentry, and takes a margin for it. Broadcom's market value stood at roughly 1.61 trillion US dollars in early 2026, per statista and alpha-sense rankings, and the TPU programme is one reason. Vertical integration, examined closely, has a subcontractor.

Second, the TPU is not manufactured by Google at all. Google owns no fab. Its chips are made by the same leading-edge foundry ecosystem as everyone else's, compete for the same advanced packaging capacity, and use the same high-bandwidth memory that is, per 2026 industry analysis, sold out across major suppliers through the year. Owning your own silicon design removes the seller's margin at one link of the chain; it does not exempt you from the queue at the bottom of the stack. When the constraint is TSMC's packaging lines or the HBM supply covered in the memory chapters, Google waits in line like Nvidia's customers do — sometimes behind them.

Third, and most strikingly, Google still buys the merchant product it built the TPU to avoid. Google Cloud offers Nvidia GPUs to its customers alongside TPUs, and has done so continuously — in 2024 it was announcing availability of Nvidia's H200 chips even as it showcased Trillium, per TechRadar's coverage at the time — because a public cloud must sell what customers demand, and much of the world's AI software is written for CUDA. The most vertically integrated player in the industry thus appears in Nvidia's revenue as a customer, in Broadcom's as a customer, and in the foundries' order books as a customer, all while fielding the most serious in-house alternative any of those suppliers faces. The four-layer stack describes activities, not loyalties.

## The chip becomes a product

For nine years the TPU was a cost structure. In its tenth year it became a revenue line.

The turn is easy to date. On 23 October 2025, Google and Anthropic announced an expansion of Anthropic's use of Google Cloud TPUs to as many as one million chips, bringing well over a gigawatt of capacity online in 2026, in a deal CNBC reported as worth tens of billions of dollars. Anthropic — whose models already ran across Google's TPUs, Amazon's Trainium and Nvidia's GPUs — cited the TPU's price-performance and efficiency, per the companies' announcement. A frontier lab, spending other people's capital under intense scrutiny, had examined the market's alternatives and ordered a million units of the chip Google built for itself. Note, and hold for the ownership chapters later in this book, that Google is also one of Anthropic's largest investors, with commitments reported in 2026 at up to 43 billion dollars including a 10 billion dollar investment in April 2026: the customer buying the chips and the shareholder funding the customer are the same company, a circularity examined properly in Part Six.

A month later came the report that moved a trillion-dollar market. In November 2025, The Information reported that Meta was in talks to spend billions of dollars on Google TPUs for its own data centres from 2027, and to rent TPU capacity from Google Cloud before that, as summarised by Yahoo Finance and Forbes at the time. Nvidia's share price fell about twelve per cent across November 2025, per the Motley Fool's December 2025 accounting of the month, with the TPU report widely cited among the causes — a reminder, after chapter two, that a market capitalisation is a price of expectations, and the expectation being repriced was Nvidia's share of its largest customers' future spending. Subsequent reporting in early 2026, including SiliconANGLE in February, described a multibillion-dollar Meta–Google agreement as struck; the companies' own confirmations were thinner than the coverage, and the reader should treat the details as reported rather than filed.

Then Google made it policy. On the first-quarter 2026 earnings call in April 2026, Sundar Pichai said Google would begin delivering TPUs to a select group of customers for installation in their own data centres — citing demand from AI labs, capital-markets firms and high-performance computing — with chief financial officer Anat Ashkenazi noting that some revenue would land in 2026 and more in 2027, per The Register's April 2026 coverage. By the July 2026 results, the first such deliveries had happened. Quietly, and to a "select group," Google had become what it spent a decade declining to be: a merchant silicon vendor.

The logic and the tension are both plain. The logic: the TPU programme's fixed costs are already sunk, every external unit sold spreads them further, and every workload that leaves CUDA for Google's software stack weakens the deepest moat of the company Google competes with for AI leadership. The tension: every TPU delivered to Meta is capacity Google Cloud cannot rent by the hour, in a year when Google's own capital budget says it is short of capacity; and a company selling chips to its competitors while running competing models, clouds and applications has more conflicts of interest than any purely horizontal vendor. How Google prices, allocates and accounts for that is not yet visible in any disclosure. It will be, in time, in the segment note.

## What to watch in the accounts

The TPU story resolves, as every story in this book does, into things a reader can check.

Watch the Google Cloud segment. Revenue, operating income and their trajectory are disclosed quarterly; the figures above — 24.8 billion of revenue and 8.8 billion of operating income in the second quarter of 2026 — are a snapshot from July 2026 reporting and will be superseded within months. Whether the margin keeps expanding as TPU-heavy capacity comes online is the closest thing to a public test of the ownership thesis.

Watch capital expenditure against depreciation. Alphabet's 2026 guidance of 195 to 205 billion dollars, per the July 2026 results coverage, is spending that will arrive on the income statement over the assumed lives of the assets; chapter thirty-two explains why that assumption decides everything.

Watch for TPU revenue to become visible. As at mid-2026 it is folded into Cloud with no separate disclosure. If external TPU sales grow to matter, accounting materiality will eventually force them into the light, and the moment they appear the buy-versus-build economics of the entire industry gets its first audited data point.

And watch the line below operating income. Alphabet's second-quarter 2026 net income of 112.1 billion dollars included 98 billion of other income from gains on equity securities, per July 2026 results coverage — much of it the paper appreciation of stakes in AI companies, of the kind CNBC reported in August 2026 as distorting the earnings picture across Big Tech. A company that owns its silicon, its cloud, its models, its applications and pieces of its rivals' financiers is a company whose headline profit needs unbundling before it means anything. Alphabet's market capitalisation stood at roughly 4.20 trillion US dollars as at mid-August 2026, per companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings — behind Nvidia's 5.3 to 5.4 trillion, ahead of Microsoft's 3.66 trillion, and, like all such figures, a photograph that had begun ageing before this sentence was printed.

The deeper lesson of the TPU belongs to the whole book. In 2013 a company looked at a demand forecast, refused the vendor's invoice, and paid silicon's fixed costs a decade early. Nothing about that decision required foresight of chatbots. It required only that someone read the cost structure honestly and notice that the buy option priced in someone else's margin forever, while the build option priced in a one-time bet on volume. Value in this industry has moved down the stack toward whoever owns the scarce physical thing; Google is the demonstration that a company from the top of the stack could, with ten years' patience, go down and own a piece of the bottom. Whether anyone can still follow it down — at today's design costs, in today's queue for fabs, against today's incumbents — is the question chapter thirty takes up.
