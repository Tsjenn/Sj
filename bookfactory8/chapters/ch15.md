# Nvidia's Accounts, Line by Line

Nvidia's financial year ends in January. The one that concerns most of this chapter ended on 25 January 2026, and the company announced the results on 25 February 2026: revenue of two hundred and fifteen point nine billion US dollars, up sixty-five per cent, with GAAP gross margin of seventy-one point one per cent, GAAP operating income of one hundred and thirty point four billion and net income of one hundred and twenty point one billion, per the company's results announcement. Data centre revenue for the year was one hundred and ninety-seven point three billion against one hundred and fifteen point two billion the year before.

Those are the numbers everybody quotes. This chapter is about the ones nobody does.

Chapter three set out a toolkit: gross margin as the scoreboard of pricing power, inventory as the first warning of a turn, customer concentration as the risk that does not appear on the front page, and cash conversion as the check on all of it. This chapter runs that toolkit over the accounts of the most valuable company in the world — worth doing precisely because the headline figures are so overwhelming that most readers stop there. Nothing here is investment advice, and nothing here alleges anything beyond what the company itself has disclosed.

## Which document, and in what order

Nvidia is a US domestic filer. It publishes an annual report on Form 10-K, quarterly reports on Form 10-Q, and it releases results with an accompanying CFO commentary that is unusually detailed by the standards of the industry. Several of the figures below first appeared in that commentary, which is the fastest route into the accounts for a reader who does not want the full filing.

Start with the calendar, because it catches people. Nvidia's fiscal 2026 ran to 25 January 2026; its fiscal 2027 first quarter ended on 26 April 2026, with results announced on 20 May 2026. A sentence comparing "Nvidia's 2026" with "Microsoft's 2026" is comparing a year that ended in January with one that ends in June. The habit from chapter six applies with force here: write the period-end date next to every figure, every time.

The second thing to establish is what kind of company you are reading. Nvidia designs chips and does not manufacture them, and that single fact reorganises the whole filing. There is no fab, so the fixed depreciation charge that dominates a foundry's cost of goods sold arrives instead as a purchase price. There is no plant utilisation story. What there is instead is a very large set of promises to suppliers, and a set of equity investments in customers. Those two are where the interesting reading is.

## Gross margin: what seventy-one cents on the dollar means

Take the margin first, because it frames everything else.

| Period | Period end | GAAP gross margin | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fiscal year 2026 | 25 January 2026 | 71.1 per cent | Results announcement, 25 February 2026 |
| Q2 fiscal 2026 | July 2025 | 72.4 per cent | Results announcement, 27 August 2025 |
| Q4 fiscal 2026 | 25 January 2026 | 75.0 per cent | Results announcement, 25 February 2026 |
| Q1 fiscal 2027 | 26 April 2026 | 74.9 per cent | Results announcement, 20 May 2026 |

Read the shape rather than the level. The full-year figure sits several points below the quarters at either end of it, which tells you the year contained something that pulled the average down. It did, and the next section deals with it. The quarterly figures meanwhile rose through the year and then held at roughly three-quarters of revenue.

A gross margin of seventy-five per cent in a hardware business is the interesting part. Chapter three listed the four contests compressed into that one percentage: price and mix, yield, utilisation, and input cost. For a fabless company, utilisation drops out and input cost becomes a negotiation with a foundry, a packaging supplier and the memory makers rather than a fixed charge on its own plant. A margin at that level, sustained while volume grows at the rates shown above, is therefore saying something specific: the company has held or raised price into rising demand while absorbing whatever its own suppliers charged it. That is the definition of pricing power, and it is visible without a word of commentary from management.

Two cautions go with that reading. A fabless company's gross margin measures a two-sided negotiation and only one side is disclosed: what Nvidia pays per wafer, per package, per stack of high-bandwidth memory is not in the accounts and never will be. When the margin holds through a period in which the foundry raises prices, the accounts are telling you the increase was passed on — not by how much, or on what terms. And the most valuable item in the discussion is disclosed by nobody. Yield, the proportion of good die on a wafer, is the industry's most commercially sensitive number. A margin improvement from yield is permanent; one from price is durable only while supply is short. The accounts do not separate them, and a reader who claims to know which is which is guessing.

### The write-down that cannot be written back

The full-year margin sat below the quarterly margins because of a single event, and it happens to demonstrate the most under-appreciated rule in chapter three.

On 9 April 2025 the US government informed Nvidia that a licence would be required to export its H20 product to China and to D:5 countries, and on 14 April 2025 informed the company that the requirement would be in effect for the indefinite future. Nvidia recorded a four point five billion dollar charge in the first quarter of fiscal 2026 for H20 excess inventory and purchase obligations as demand for the product fell away, per the first-quarter results announced on 28 May 2025. The same announcement disclosed that H20 sales had been four point six billion dollars in the quarter before the licensing requirement took effect, and that a further two point five billion of H20 revenue could not be shipped. Excluding the charge, first-quarter non-GAAP gross margin would have been seventy-one point three per cent.

Now watch what happened next. In the second quarter of fiscal 2026, the company recorded a one hundred and eighty million dollar release of previously reserved H20 inventory, arising from roughly six hundred and fifty million dollars of H20 sales to a customer outside China, per the results announced on 27 August 2025. Reported non-GAAP gross margin for that quarter was seventy-two point seven per cent; excluding the release it would have been seventy-two point three.

That is the asymmetry chapter three described, occurring in public, in consecutive quarters. Under ASC 330 a write-down establishes a new and lower cost basis and may not be reversed even when conditions improve. So the parts left the building carrying an artificially low cost, the proceeds fell almost entirely through to gross profit, and reported margin rose by roughly forty basis points. Nothing whatever had changed about the company's pricing power.

Nvidia disclosed all of it, quantified it, and gave the margin both with and without the release, which is more than many companies do. The lesson is not about Nvidia. It is that in any semiconductor business emerging from a bad quarter, some part of the margin recovery is the sale of inventory written down when things looked worse, and only the note will tell you how much. Had the company reported under IFRS, the same facts would have produced a reversal through profit under IAS 2 rather than a flattered margin later. Same events, two frameworks, two different-looking recoveries.

Inventory provisions of zero point eight billion dollars were then recorded in cost of revenue in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, per the Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 April 2026 — far smaller than the H20 adjustment, but a reminder that in a business selling parts with short commercial lives, provisioning is a recurring feature of cost of revenue rather than an exceptional item.

## Customer concentration: two numbers that both mean something

This is the disclosure most worth learning to read properly, because the two published versions of it look like a contradiction and are not.

For fiscal 2026 as a whole, the annual report for the year ended 25 January 2026 disclosed that sales to one direct customer represented twenty-two per cent of total revenue and sales to another direct customer represented fourteen per cent, both primarily attributable to the Compute and Networking segment. Two customers, thirty-six per cent of the year.

For the third quarter of that same fiscal year, the Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 October 2025 disclosed four direct customers each above ten per cent of total revenue, at twenty-two, fifteen, thirteen and eleven per cent. Four customers, about sixty-one per cent of the quarter. The Motley Fool, writing in November 2025, put it in the form most readers met it: concentration among customers at or above ten per cent of sales had risen from thirty-six to sixty-one per cent in a year, on quarterly revenue of about fifty-seven billion dollars, and three customers had each accounted for twelve per cent in the comparable quarter a year earlier.

Both disclosures are correct. They differ because ASC 280 asks the question over the reporting period, and a customer placing one enormous order in October is a large share of that quarter and a smaller share of the twelve months containing it. Averaging over a year smooths lumpy buyers into apparent diversity. The quarterly figure is the more honest picture of how lumpy the business is; the annual figure the more honest picture of the customer base over a full cycle of ordering. Read them together or you will draw the wrong conclusion from either.

A third window on the same risk is the one professional readers check first. Accounts receivable concentration is measured at a single balance sheet date rather than over a period, so it moves faster and shows a change in buying behaviour earlier than the revenue disclosure does.

| Balance sheet date | Direct customers above 10 per cent of accounts receivable | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 26 October 2025 | 22, 17, 14 and 12 per cent | Form 10-Q, quarter ended 26 October 2025 |
| 26 April 2026 | 25, 18 and 13 per cent | Form 10-Q, quarter ended 26 April 2026 |

Four names at the earlier date, three at the later one, with the largest position growing. That is a fact about who owed money on a particular Sunday, not a forecast. But when a receivables table changes shape while a revenue table does not, it changed first for a reason, and the reason is usually worth finding.

### Direct is not the same as final

The word doing the heavy lifting in every one of these disclosures is "direct". A direct customer is the entity Nvidia bills. It is not necessarily the entity that decided to buy, and very often not the entity that will run the machine.

Systems reach end users through original equipment manufacturers, contract assemblers, channel partners and cloud operators who buy on their own account and rent out the result. One disclosed direct customer may be an assembler shipping to a dozen unrelated buyers, in which case the concentration figure overstates the commercial risk. Equally, two disclosed customers may both be building for the same end operator, in which case it understates it. The company's own risk language acknowledges the point: revenue is described as concentrated among a limited number of direct and indirect customers, and the trend as one that may continue.

The consequence for the reader is a discipline, not a conclusion. Do not assume the letters map onto the household names you have in mind. The filing does not say, ASC 280 does not require it to say, and the honest position is that you cannot tell from the accounts alone.

What you can say is structural, and it is enough to be going on with. A business in which a handful of purchasing decisions determine most of a quarter is a different business from one with a hundred thousand customers, whatever the credit quality of those few. That the largest buyers of accelerators are also the best-capitalised companies on earth cuts both ways: they are unlikely to fail to pay, and they are the only organisations with the resources to design a substitute. Each plausible candidate for those letters has a custom silicon programme of its own, which is public record and gets its own chapter.

## Inventory: the physical record of a forecast

The balance sheet at 25 January 2026 carried inventories of twenty-one point four billion dollars — twenty-one thousand four hundred and three million, to use the filing's own units — against two point six billion at the fiscal 2022 balance sheet date, per the fiscal 2026 annual report as compiled by stock-analysis-on.net. The composition was disclosed in the note:

| Category | 25 January 2026 |
|---|---|
| Raw materials | 3,807 million dollars |
| Work in process | 8,822 million dollars |
| Finished goods | 8,774 million dollars |
| Total | 21,403 million dollars |

Chapter three gave the rule for reading that split: a rise concentrated in raw materials and work in progress ahead of a stated ramp is building to a plan, while a rise concentrated in finished goods against flat revenue is product nobody has bought. Here, rather more than half the balance sits in raw materials and work in process, which is the profile of a company building into a ramp rather than one holding unsold stock. The days measure was one hundred and twenty-five days of inventory in fiscal 2026, on the same compilation.

A hundred and twenty-five days is a long time to hold parts where product generations turn over every year or two, and it would be a warning in a company whose revenue was flat. Against sixty-five per cent revenue growth and a data centre business that nearly doubled, it reads instead as building ahead of demand that has so far arrived. The test from chapter three needs no special skill: compare the growth rate of inventory against the growth rate of revenue, two quarters running, and see which is faster. When inventory pulls ahead without a stated ramp behind it, the company is building for demand it has not yet seen. Run that test by hand rather than accepting anyone's summary of it. The inventory line is the earliest number in these accounts that can turn, and it turns before revenue does.

## Purchase commitments: the largest number that is not on the balance sheet

Now the item most readers of these accounts have never looked at, and which is currently larger than anything in the balance sheet discussion. Purchase obligations disclosed in the commitments note of the same annual report were ninety-five point two billion dollars, against sixteen point one billion a year earlier — a figure widely reported in February and March 2026, including by Benzinga and Yahoo Finance in their coverage of the filing. Adding other supply agreements, total commitments were reported at approximately one hundred and seventeen billion dollars. By the following quarter the number had grown again: the Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 April 2026 put commitments at about one hundred and nineteen billion dollars, of which roughly ninety-five billion was payable in the remainder of fiscal 2027 and the balance across fiscal 2028 to 2031, with other vendor commitments of about six billion dollars falling mostly within fiscal 2027.

Set the two side by side. What sits on the balance sheet as inventory is around twenty-one billion. What sits off it, as a promise to buy, is more than four times that. A reader who looks only at the balance sheet is seeing less than a fifth of the company's commitment to its supply chain.

### What the commitment actually buys

Purchase obligations of that kind are not a corporate eccentricity. They are what it costs to reserve capacity that is genuinely scarce and takes years to build. Advanced packaging, high-bandwidth memory allocation, foundry wafer starts at the leading edge: all are allocated in advance, and allocated to whoever will commit. The commitment note is therefore the closest thing in the accounts to a statement of how much capacity the company believes it can sell.

The Michael Burry note that drew wide attention in 2026, reported by Benzinga and Yahoo Finance, read the same disclosure as a warning, drawing a comparison with Cisco Systems in 2000 and arguing that a change of this size in how a company contracts with its suppliers is a change in its risk, not merely in its scale. That is one commentator's interpretation of a public disclosure and is presented here as such. The counter-reading is equally available from the same filing: commitments of this size, entered into by a company whose product has been supply-constrained for years and whose customers are among the most creditworthy in the world, are a rational response to scarcity rather than evidence of overreach.

This book does not adjudicate between those readings. What it can do is state what the commitment means mechanically, because that part is not a matter of opinion.

A purchase obligation is a fixed cash outflow owed whether or not the demand arrives. It converts a variable cost into something much closer to a fixed one for the duration of the commitment. That is why the H20 episode is instructive: the four point five billion dollar charge was recorded for excess inventory *and purchase obligations*. When demand disappeared, the company was left owing for parts it no longer had a buyer for, and the accounts recognised the obligation as a cost immediately. That is the mechanism, demonstrated on a small scale, against a commitment book that has since grown by a factor of six.

### How to read a number like that

Four questions turn the figure into information, and all four can be answered from the filings.

What proportion is cancellable? The commitments note distinguishes obligations that are non-cancellable from those cancellable in specified circumstances, and that distinction is the difference between a liability and a plan.

What is the payment profile? A hundred and nineteen billion dollars payable over six years is a different object from the same sum payable within eighteen months. As at 26 April 2026 the disclosure was much closer to the latter.

How does the commitment compare with the order book? Commitments growing faster than visible demand is the pattern chapter three flagged. Nvidia publishes no formal backlog figure, so the comparison must be made against revenue growth and management commentary — which is worth knowing in itself, because it makes this check weaker here than at a company that discloses backlog.

What does the company say about the direction? The filing's own language, as reported in 2026 coverage, was that these obligations were expected to continue to grow and to become a greater portion of supply. Management telling you a number will get bigger is the cheapest forward-looking information in any annual report, and it is nearly always buried.

## Investments in customers

The third structure worth a second look is an equity investment in an entity that is also a customer. Chapter three introduced it in the abstract. Nvidia has made it concrete on a scale that has few precedents in industrial history.

The public record, with dates and sources, runs roughly as follows. In September 2025 Nvidia and OpenAI announced a non-binding letter of intent under which Nvidia would invest up to one hundred billion dollars, progressively as each gigawatt of a planned ten gigawatts of computing capacity was deployed, with OpenAI leasing chips; 2026 reporting subsequently described the commitment as having been settled at roughly thirty billion dollars. In November 2025 Nvidia committed up to ten billion dollars to Anthropic. In September 2025 it invested five billion dollars in Intel, a position CNBC reported on 9 May 2026 as then worth over twenty-five billion dollars, and which Tom's Hardware, reporting on a first Form 13F in August 2026, put at about thirty billion — the same filing showing a roughly twenty-one billion dollar SpaceX position and a complete exit from Arm stock. Alongside those, 2026 reporting has described a CoreWeave holding of 47.213 million shares valued at about four point seven billion dollars, a two billion dollar Nebius position taken through pre-funded warrants rather than common stock, a right to invest up to two point one billion dollars in IREN and up to three point two billion in Corning.

CNBC's tally on 9 May 2026 put Nvidia's equity commitments in calendar 2026 alone above forty billion dollars. The Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 April 2026 disclosed total investment commitments of about twenty-seven billion dollars, subject to contingencies, expected to be made through the remainder of that fiscal year. In March 2026, Jensen Huang indicated that the company might have written its last large cheques to the leading model developers, as reported by TechCrunch on 4 March 2026.

Every one of those transactions is legal, disclosed and, on its face, strategically coherent. Nothing here suggests otherwise. What an accountant does with the pattern is ask three questions.

**Does the money come back as revenue, and can you tell?** An investment a customer then spends on the investor's product raises revenue and creates an asset, and both effects are real in the accounting sense. The question is whether the disclosure lets you separate connected revenue from the total. Related-party disclosure requirements bite when an investor has significant influence; a minority stake without board representation often does not meet that threshold, in which case the revenue is not separable from outside. Where you cannot tell, the honest answer is that you cannot tell, and that is the answer here.

**Where does the gain live?** Under ASC 321, equity securities with readily determinable fair values are measured at fair value with changes running through net income. A stake bought for five billion and marked at twenty-five or thirty billion therefore produces a great deal of reported profit that has nothing to do with selling chips. It sits below the operating line, it reverses if the price falls, and it is disclosed in the other income note — which is where to go before drawing conclusions about the quality of any quarter's earnings growth. Holdings without readily determinable fair values are commonly carried under the measurement alternative at cost less impairment, adjusted for observable price changes, so they are revalued only when someone else's transaction forces the issue.

**Is the return on the investment being confused with the return on the business?** The easiest question to answer. Investment gains sit outside operating income. If you want to know how the chip business performed, read the operating line and leave the marks alone.

### An illustrative circle

Take an illustrative supplier and customer, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. The supplier invests one hundred in the customer. The customer spends eighty of it buying the supplier's product. The supplier books eighty of revenue at, say, a seventy per cent gross margin, so fifty-six of gross profit, and holds an asset of one hundred.

Note what is and is not true. The revenue is real: goods were delivered and the customer owns them. The cash, though, has made a round trip, and the supplier is worse off by one hundred and better off by eighty. The eighty of revenue is indistinguishable, in the income statement, from eighty sold to an unconnected buyer. And if the customer fails, the supplier loses the asset and the future revenue together, because the two risks were never independent.

None of that is improper and none of it is hidden. It is a structure in which reported revenue and reported investment are correlated in a way that revenue from an unconnected customer is not. The reader's job is to know that, to look for the disclosure that would size it, and to say plainly when that disclosure does not exist.

## Share-based payment and the share count

Stock-based compensation expense in fiscal 2026 was six thousand three hundred and eighty-six million dollars, per the CFO commentary accompanying the fourth-quarter results in February 2026. Against revenue of two hundred and fifteen point nine billion and net income of one hundred and twenty point one billion, that is a small number — which is itself the point worth making.

In most chip design companies, stock-based compensation is a large fraction of operating profit and the argument about excluding it from non-GAAP measures is consequential. Here, profit has grown so much faster than the charge that the argument has become almost academic in arithmetic terms. It has not become academic in principle. Stock-based compensation is a genuine cost, paid in ownership rather than currency, and the payment shows up in the diluted share count rather than in the cash flow statement.

So the place to check is not the expense line. It is the share count over several years, read alongside the cash spent buying shares back. Nvidia returned forty-one point one billion dollars to shareholders in fiscal 2026 through repurchases and dividends, with fifty-eight point five billion remaining under its repurchase authorisation at the end of the fourth quarter, per the same CFO commentary. A company repurchasing on that scale while the diluted count stays flat is converting cash into compensation; one whose count falls is returning capital. That distinction appears in no single line of the income statement, and it takes about five minutes with two annual reports to settle.

## Cash generation: the profit that did not arrive as cash

Chapter three insisted on reading the cash flow statement first, because it is the hardest of the three statements to dress. It is also, in this case, the most interesting.

Net cash provided by operating activities in fiscal 2026 was one hundred and two point seven billion dollars, per the annual report as compiled by stock-analysis-on.net. Net income for the same year was one hundred and twenty point one billion.

Operating cash flow was below net income. In a year of sixty-five per cent revenue growth, that is what should be expected, and it is worth understanding rather than worrying about. Depreciation and the six point four billion of stock-based compensation are non-cash charges added back, which pushes the reconciliation upwards. For the total to land below net income anyway, working capital must have absorbed a considerable amount — and the candidates are visible elsewhere in these same accounts. Inventory at twenty-one point four billion. Receivables from a small number of very large customers. Prepayments and advances to suppliers under the commitment programme described above.

This is growth consuming cash, the normal condition of a company scaling a physical product, and it is the story the inventory note tells arriving through a different door. The reconciliation between net income and operating cash flow in the annual report shows precisely which line absorbed what, and reading it is more informative than any ratio computed from the summary figures.

The warning sign is not this year's gap. It is the gap widening while revenue growth slows, because that combination means working capital is absorbing cash that sales are no longer generating. That is the pattern chapter three described as profit rising while operating cash flow does not, and the only way to see it is to plot both series over several years rather than reading one year at a time.

One further note on where the cash goes. Because the company owns no fabs, capital expenditure is not the dominant investing outflow it is for a foundry. The investing section is instead where the equity investments described above appear. For most semiconductor companies that section is a story about plant. For this one it is increasingly a story about ownership of customers and partners, and it should be read that way.

## What the accounts will not tell you

The boundary is worth stating, because a chapter that has walked through this much disclosure can leave the impression that everything is knowable. It is not.

The accounts do not disclose the price of any individual product, or what any customer paid. They do not disclose yield. They do not name the customers behind the concentration percentages, and ASC 280 does not require it. They do not separate revenue that originated with an entity Nvidia has invested in. They do not give a backlog figure. They do not disclose the terms on which capacity has been reserved with any named supplier, only the aggregate obligation and its maturity profile. And they do not tell you what the commitments would be worth in a downturn, because that depends on cancellation terms described in words rather than quantified.

Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot with a date attached, and every one will have moved. The fiscal year discussed here ended on 25 January 2026; by the time these pages are read there will be later quarters, a later annual report, a different commitment number and a different concentration table. The current versions appear on the company's investor relations pages within days of each results announcement. Go and get the current number, and write the date beside it.

## A reading order for this company

If you have forty minutes and Nvidia's latest filing, the order that extracts the most is close to the general one from chapter three, with two adjustments for this business.

Read the cash flow statement first, all three sections, with attention to the investing section and not only the operating one. Read the balance sheet second, with attention to inventory and its composition. Then go straight to the commitments note, which here is more consequential than any line on the balance sheet, and read the maturity profile rather than only the total. Then the concentration disclosure, in both its revenue and its receivables forms, comparing the quarter with the year. Then the equity investments note and the other income line, so you know how much reported profit came from marks rather than sales. Then the income statement. Then this year's risk factors against last year's, looking for new sentences about supply commitments and about customers. Management's discussion last, when you already know what happened.

The figures will be different when you do it. The order will not be.
