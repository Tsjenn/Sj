# Meta, xAI and the Open-Weight Camp

Somewhere on the internet, right now, a person you have never heard of is downloading the weights of a model that cost hundreds of millions of dollars to train. No invoice will be raised. No licence fee will be collected. The company that paid for the training knows this is happening, published the download link itself, and issued a press release celebrating the milestone when the count passed a billion.

To an accountant this looks, at first sight, like a write-off dressed up as a strategy. A model's weights — the billions of numbers that a training run produces — are the entire output of the most expensive industrial process in the industry. The previous two chapters described companies valued near a trillion dollars each precisely because they own such weights and sell access to them by the token. Giving the same class of asset away, deliberately and repeatedly, needs explaining.

The explanation exists, it is coherent, and it is older than this industry. But it only works for a particular kind of company: one whose income statement lives somewhere other than the model layer. This chapter is about the two most prominent examples — Meta, which sells attention, and xAI, which has become part of something that sells rockets and satellite bandwidth — and about what the open-weight strategy has actually delivered, which is both more and less than its advocates claim.

## What a weight release gives away, and what it keeps

Be precise about the asset first, because the phrase "open source" is doing misleading work in most coverage.

A weight release publishes the trained parameters of a model, usually with inference code and a licence. It generally does not publish the training data, the data-processing pipeline, the training code in usable form, or the accumulated judgement of the people who ran the training. In software, "open source" means you can rebuild the thing from what is published. For most released models you cannot; you can only run and adapt the finished artefact. "Open weights" is the honest term, and this book uses it.

Now the economics. Training is close to a pure fixed cost: it is incurred once, in advance, whether one person or a hundred million people use the result. Copying the finished weights costs effectively nothing. An asset with that cost structure supports two opposite strategies, and the industry has split cleanly between them.

The first strategy keeps the weights private and sells access. Every token of usage passes through the owner's metering, and the owner's gross margin is the difference between the price of the token and the compute cost of producing it. This is the OpenAI and Anthropic model described in the preceding chapters, and it is why their reported revenue run-rates — over sixty-five billion dollars annualised for Anthropic as at July 2026, per CNBC's August 2026 reporting, around forty billion for OpenAI in the same trade coverage, both unaudited — attract valuations near a trillion dollars. Access to the weights is the product.

The second strategy publishes the weights and forgoes that revenue entirely. What the publisher buys with the forgone revenue is a change in the market itself: if a good model is free, the price of good-enough models falls towards the cost of the compute that runs them. Anyone whose business is selling model access watches their pricing power erode. Anyone whose business is selling the things models are used *with* — advertising audiences, chips, clouds, cars, phones — watches those things become more valuable.

Strategy writing has a name for this: commoditise your complement. Two products are complements when they are consumed together, and when the price of one falls, demand for the other rises. Cheap petrol is good for carmakers. Cheap flights are good for hotels. A software strategist observed a quarter of a century ago that smart companies work to drive the price of their complements down towards zero, because the money their customers no longer spend on the complement becomes money available for the thing they actually sell. Free models are good for anyone who sells the layer above them or the layer below them — and ruinous only for the companies in the middle whose product the model is.

That single observation predicts almost everything in this chapter: who gives weights away, who funds the companies that do, and why the strategy wobbles the moment the giver starts wanting model revenue of its own.

## Meta: the accounts of an attention business

Meta's second quarter of 2026 — the quarter ended June 2026, reported on 29 July 2026 — shows a company whose money comes from exactly one place. Revenue was 60.80 billion US dollars, up twenty-eight per cent year on year; advertising accounted for 59.36 billion of it, per the company's results and CNBC's same-day coverage. Some 3.60 billion people used at least one of its apps daily in the period. Set those two figures side by side and the strategic position is not subtle. Meta does not sell tokens, seats or model subscriptions in any amount that registers on its income statement. It sells the attention of nearly half the planet, priced by auction, to advertisers.

For a company built that way, artificial intelligence is a complement twice over. Models make the advertising machine better — targeting, ranking, ad creative, content recommendation — and models threaten to create rival destinations for attention. Both cases argue for the same policy: make good models cheap and ubiquitous, and make sure no rival's proprietary model becomes a tollbooth Meta must pass through. If the model layer earns thin margins everywhere, Meta loses nothing, because it never earned its living there. Its rivals in the model business lose a great deal.

There is also a quieter accounting point. Meta guided its 2026 capital expenditure to 130 to 145 billion US dollars, a range it raised at the same July 2026 results, with 31.1 billion spent in the second quarter alone. That money buys data centres and accelerators — assets that go on the balance sheet and depreciate over years, and that serve the advertising business regardless of what happens to any given model. The incremental cost of the open-weight programme, seen against a capital budget of that size, is a rounding item spent on machines the company was buying anyway. Giving away the weights does not give away the fleet that trained them. The fleet is the asset; the weights are, in a sense the accountant can respect, exhaust.

The same results, it should be said, show what the strategy costs to sustain. Total costs and expenses in the quarter rose fifty-five per cent to 42.03 billion dollars, including 2.40 billion of legal charges and 1.18 billion of severance attached to a reduction of roughly eight thousand roles, and the operating margin compressed to thirty-one per cent from forty-three a year earlier, per the results coverage of July 2026. The market's reaction — CNBC's headline noted the shares falling on light revenue guidance and dwindling free cash flow — is a reminder that even an advertising machine of this size feels the weight of AI-scale capital expenditure. Chapter four's tools apply here in full.

## The Llama years

Meta's open-weight campaign was not a single decision but an escalation, and the sequence rewards attention because each step reveals what the strategy was for.

| Date | Event | Source |
|---|---|---|
| February 2023 | First Llama released to researchers under a non-commercial licence; the weights promptly circulate without authorisation via BitTorrent | contemporary reporting, 2023 |
| July 2023 | Llama 2 released free for research and commercial use, with Microsoft as "preferred partner" | Meta newsroom, July 2023 |
| July 2024 | Llama 3.1, including a 405-billion-parameter model presented as the first openly available model at the frontier, alongside Zuckerberg's essay "Open Source AI Is the Path Forward" | Meta newsroom, July 2024 |
| December 2024 | Meta reports 650 million cumulative Llama downloads | reported by TechCrunch, March 2025 |
| March 2025 | Cumulative downloads pass one billion | Zuckerberg announcement, reported by TechCrunch and Forbes, March 2025 |
| April 2025 | Llama 4 Scout and Maverick released, Meta's first mixture-of-experts family; the largest variant, Behemoth, previewed as still in training. Downloads reported at 1.2 billion | TechCrunch and launch coverage, April 2025 |
| June 2025 | Meta pays 14.3 billion dollars for a forty-nine per cent non-voting stake in Scale AI; founder Alexandr Wang joins to lead a new superintelligence group | CNBC, June 2025 |
| April 2026 | Meta Superintelligence Labs ships Muse Spark — closed weights | 2026 trade coverage |
| August 2026 | Meta returns partway: Muse Glimmer, a thirty-billion-parameter model, released under an open licence | 2026 trade coverage |

The first row is the strategy's accidental origin. The first Llama was never meant to be open in any commercial sense; it escaped. What Meta learned from the leak was that an ecosystem forms around free weights at astonishing speed — tooling, fine-tunes, hobbyist infrastructure — and that the ecosystem's output flows back. By July 2023 the leak had been converted into policy, and by July 2024 into doctrine, with Zuckerberg's essay arguing that open models would become the industry standard the way Linux had in operating systems, and that openness prevented power concentrating "in the hands of a small number of companies," per the essay's publication on Meta's newsroom in July 2024.

The download counts deserve a professional caveat. One billion downloads, reported in March 2025, is a genuinely large number and a genuinely strange metric. A download is not a user, not a deployment and not a dollar; the same developer pulling the same model onto twelve servers is twelve downloads. No auditor would let a company book it as anything. It is best read the way Meta plainly intended it: as evidence of standard-setting, the only currency an open-weight strategy earns directly.

Then the sequence turns. Llama 4's April 2025 release met a mixed reception, and Behemoth — the two-trillion-parameter teacher model previewed alongside it — was delayed through 2025 and never publicly shipped, per the 2026 trade press that tracked its status. Meta's response was not a bigger open model but a chequebook: the 14.3 billion dollars for just under half of Scale AI, a company whose business is manufacturing training data, valuing it above twenty-nine billion dollars, and the recruitment of its twenty-eight-year-old founder to run a new lab, per CNBC's June 2025 reporting. A hiring campaign followed that the trade press covered largely in the vocabulary of football transfers.

And in April 2026, the doctrine broke. Meta Superintelligence Labs shipped its Muse Spark frontier model with closed weights — twenty-one months, as one 2026 commentary counted it, after the essay that had declared open source the path forward. The partial reversal of August 2026, a capable thirty-billion-parameter model released openly while the frontier line stayed closed, per 2026 trade coverage, settles the question of what the strategy had always been. Open weights were an instrument, not a principle. When Meta believed its frontier model was a complement to its advertising business, it gave the weights away. The moment it began to believe the model might be a product — something with its own revenue line — it behaved exactly as OpenAI and Anthropic do. Watch the licence, not the essay.

## xAI: the bet with a rocket attached

xAI's version of the open question runs through stranger corporate geography, and it is worth walking slowly because the entity that exists in 2026 is unlike anything else at the model layer.

The company was founded in 2023. In March 2024 it released the weights of Grok-1 under the permissive Apache 2.0 licence: a 314-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, per xAI's own announcement and trade coverage of March 2024. Note what was actually published — the raw base checkpoint from a pre-training run that had concluded in October 2023, months earlier, without the fine-tuning that made the deployed Grok product usable. It was a real release and a careful one: the commercially live model stayed closed, and subsequent frontier Groks were sold, not published. Where Meta's openness was doctrine for a while, xAI's was a gesture — a well-aimed one, made while its founder was publicly litigating with OpenAI over that company's own abandoned openness.

The structure moves mattered more than the weights. In March 2025, xAI acquired X — the social platform, also controlled by Elon Musk — in an all-stock transaction valuing xAI at eighty billion dollars and X at thirty-three billion, being forty-five billion less twelve billion of debt, per Musk's announcement and CNBC and Bloomberg reporting of March 2025. Whatever else that deal accomplished, it welded a model company to an attention asset: distribution to X's users, and a live feed of text and, later, video to train on. In January 2026 came a twenty-billion-dollar Series E at a 230-billion-dollar valuation, upsized from a fifteen-billion target, with Nvidia and Cisco — both suppliers to xAI — among the investors, per Yahoo Finance's January 2026 coverage. A supplier investing in its own customer is a structure this book returns to in Part Six; note it here and read the later chapter before deciding what it means.

Then, in February 2026, SpaceX acquired xAI in an all-stock transaction that valued the model company at 250 billion dollars inside a combined entity reported at roughly 1.25 trillion, per 2026 profiles compiled by valueaddvc and others. Across roughly nine rounds from a late-2023 seed to the Series E, the same profiles count about forty-five billion dollars raised. All of these are private-market figures of the kind Chapter Five taught you to handle with gloves: prices of the last shares sold, on terms outsiders cannot see, reported by parties with reasons to report them. None of it is audited, and none of it is investment advice in either direction.

What is the bet, exactly? Meta's logic is legible in its income statement: free models defend an advertising machine. xAI's revenue, by contrast, is not disclosed in any audited form, and the group it now sits inside earns its money from launches, satellite connectivity and a social platform. The most concrete public clue to the economics arrived in May 2026, when SpaceX was reported to have leased the entire computing capacity of the original Colossus data centre in Memphis — over three hundred megawatts and some 220,000 Nvidia accelerators — to Anthropic, of all customers, while xAI's own training moved to a successor site, per 2026 industry profiles of the company. Read that as an accountant reads it: the group's most bankable AI asset, the one generating arm's-length revenue from a third party, is not the model. It is the compute underneath it, rented — in a fine irony — to a closed-weight rival. The book's argument about value moving down the stack rarely gets a cleaner exhibit.

## The rest of the camp, and the day the theory went to market

Meta and xAI are the largest names attached to open weights, but the camp's purest member is French. Mistral has built its identity on open-weight releases and on the customers they attract — governments and enterprises that want models they can run on their own hardware, inside their own borders, for data-sovereignty reasons no API contract can satisfy.

Look at who pays for that position. Mistral's September 2025 Series C of 1.7 billion euros was led by ASML — the lithography monopolist of Chapter Ten — which put in 1.3 billion euros for an eleven per cent fully diluted stake and became the largest shareholder, at a post-money valuation of 11.7 billion euros, per CNBC's September 2025 reporting; Nvidia was among the participating investors. A further raise of 3.5 billion dollars at a twenty-billion-dollar valuation followed in June 2026, per 2026 reporting. The pattern is the complement logic wearing a cap table: the companies funding free-model economics are, to a striking degree, the ones that sell the layers beneath. Cheap abundant models mean more chips fabbed, more lithography tools sold, more cloud hours rented. Mistral's actual model revenue — an estimated four hundred million dollars annualised as at January 2026, up from roughly sixteen million at the end of 2024, per Sacra's estimates, unaudited — is growing fast from a small base, and is smaller than Anthropic's reported run-rate by two orders of magnitude. The valuation is not a claim about today's revenue. It is a claim about position.

And then there is the one day on which the commoditisation theory stopped being a theory. On 27 January 2025, after the Chinese lab DeepSeek released its R1 reasoning model with open weights and a free assistant app that climbed past ChatGPT on Apple's US App Store, Nvidia's shares fell nearly seventeen per cent and the company lost 589 billion dollars of market value — the largest single-day loss any company had recorded, per CNBC and Yahoo Finance on the day. Contemporary press coverage reported the model as some twenty to fifty times cheaper to use than the leading closed alternative, claims that came from the lab itself and were never audited. Chapter Forty-Two examines DeepSeek properly. Here, one observation belongs: the most measurable commercial consequence any open-weight release has ever produced was not revenue for anyone. It was the temporary destruction of half a trillion dollars of market value at the silicon layer, on the theory — largely retracted by the market within months — that cheap models meant less demand for chips. Even the strategy's greatest demonstration of force expressed itself on somebody else's layer.

## What open weights have actually achieved

An honest audit, since the brief of this book is to read results rather than manifestos.

**What the strategy has demonstrably done.** It set standards: over a billion cumulative Llama downloads by March 2025 and 1.2 billion by that April, per TechCrunch's contemporaneous reporting, built the largest developer ecosystem at the model layer around one family of models. It capped prices: every closed lab prices its API knowing a free alternative sits one download away, and the gap it can charge is the gap in capability, not the full value of the service — a discipline the closed labs' customers should thank Meta for. It created a real market segment: the sovereignty and on-premises demand that Mistral monetises exists only because open weights exist. It armed the layers below: silicon and compute sell more of everything when models are abundant, which is precisely why silicon and compute companies keep turning up on open-weight cap tables. And once, in January 2025, it moved a market as violently as any earnings report in history.

**What it has not done.** It has not made anyone significant money *at the model layer*. Meta's second quarter of 2026 shows 59.36 billion dollars of advertising inside 60.80 billion of revenue; whatever Llama contributed, it is invisible in the segmentation. Mistral's estimated four hundred million dollars of annualised revenue as at January 2026, per Sacra, against Anthropic's reported sixty-five-billion-plus run-rate as at July 2026, per CNBC, is the fairest available like-for-like — both unaudited — and it is not close. It has not held the frontier: the open releases have consistently trailed the best closed models, and the one company that promised frontier-level openness delayed its flagship through 2025, never shipped it, and went closed in April 2026. And it has not proven durable as doctrine: the moment Meta's leadership saw a path to selling intelligence rather than complementing advertising with it, the weights stopped coming, and what reopened in August 2026 was a mid-sized model, not the frontier.

The pattern that survives the audit is simple, and it is the one the two-strategy split at the top of this chapter would predict. Open weights are not a business model. They are a pricing weapon, wielded rationally by companies whose profit pool sits on another layer — attention for Meta, the ecosystem of chips and tools for Nvidia and ASML as investors, compute for the group around xAI — and abandoned, just as rationally, the moment the wielder starts to believe the model itself could be the profit pool. The weights go free when the money is elsewhere. Follow the money, and you will always know in advance which it will be.

For the reader keeping score of this book's larger argument: the model layer's most celebrated act of generosity turns out, on inspection of the accounts, to be another mechanism moving value down the stack. The current figures — every valuation, run-rate and download count above — will have moved by the time you read this; the company results pages, the funding coverage of CNBC and the trade press, and the download milestones Meta publishes itself are where to find today's numbers. The logic of complements will not have moved at all.
