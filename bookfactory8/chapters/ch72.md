# The Memory Decade

On 10 July 2026, the chief executive of SK Hynix, Kwak Noh-jung, said that the coming year would be, in his words as reported, "the worst year in the industry's history from the supply perspective", and that customer demand would continue to outrun his company's capacity beyond 2030. The remarks were carried by Reuters and reproduced by US News and Yahoo Finance that day; Tom's Hardware noted that they were made on the day the company's shares began trading on Nasdaq. Four weeks later, on 7 August 2026, the board of the same company approved about fifty-four trillion won — roughly thirty-eight billion US dollars — of new plant, as reported by CNBC and the Korea JoongAng Daily.

Put the two events in the order they occurred and you have the whole of this chapter's subject. A man whose business is selling a scarce product forecast that it would get scarcer, and then his board committed the largest sum in the company's history to making it less scarce. Neither statement is cynical and neither is inconsistent. They are the two halves of the machine this book described in its semiconductor part, operating exactly as it has operated since the 1970s.

The question is whether the machine is the same machine.

## What is actually being claimed

By this point the reader has three things the earlier chapters supplied. The history: four decades in which memory was arguably the worst structural business in semiconductors, with five collapses in thirty-five years and a graveyard of some twenty producers. The mechanism: high-bandwidth memory, the stacked, qualified, contracted component that turned an interchangeable part into one the buyer cannot substitute inside a product generation. And the companies: three survivors, differently exposed, all of them spending.

So the claim on the table can be stated precisely, and it is worth stating precisely, because it is usually stated loosely.

The loose version is that memory has "become a growth industry" or that "the supercycle is structural". Neither of those is a testable proposition. The strict version is this: **the qualification requirements and manufacturing difficulty of HBM have changed the relationship between price and supply, such that a price signal no longer produces the volume of supply that historically destroyed it.**

That is a claim about a lag and a slope. It says the industry's supply response has become slower, smaller, or both. And it comes in two strengths, which are constantly conflated:

- **Broken.** The mechanism no longer functions. Price rises no longer summon a supply response capable of collapsing the price. Memory becomes an industry with a permanently favourable structure, like leading-edge lithography or foundry at the frontier.
- **Lengthened.** The mechanism still functions, but slowly. The interval between committing capacity and receiving it has grown, the fraction of capacity that converts into sellable product has fallen, and contracts smooth what remains. The sawtooth is stretched and its teeth are shallower, but it is a sawtooth.

Those two claims have identical evidence up to the point where they diverge, and that point has not arrived. Anyone who tells you which of them is true is telling you a preference. What can be done — and what an accountant is trained to do — is to take each protection the bull case relies on, ask what precisely it protects against, and read the end date printed on it.

## The record, compressed

Before the arguments, the base rate, because it is the thing most easily forgotten in a good quarter.

Memory's downturns did not happen in a distant era of undisciplined competition. The most recent one happened to the same three companies now reporting the best numbers in the industry's history, three years before they reported them. For calendar 2023, SK Hynix reported an annual operating loss of 7.73 trillion won and a net loss of 9.14 trillion, per its results published in January 2024. Micron lost 5.8 billion dollars in fiscal 2023 on revenue that nearly halved, finishing the year with a gross margin of minus 9.1 per cent — that is, selling chips for less than they cost to make. The same company reported a gross margin of 84.6 per cent for its fiscal third quarter of 2026, announced in June 2026.

Ninety-four percentage points of gross margin, in the same company, in three years, with the same fabs and largely the same products. No management team is that good or that bad. That spread is the cycle, photographed at two points, and it is the single most useful fact a reader of these accounts can hold in mind.

The reason 2023 matters more than 1996 or 2008 is that it happened after consolidation. The comfortable theory said that three producers who can see each other would not repeat the behaviour of twenty-three who could not. Three producers who could see each other perfectly delivered one of the worst downturns on record anyway, because each of them faced the same small trap: cutting output supports the price for all three at the sole expense of the one who cuts. Discipline among three is real. It is also slow, and it arrives after the losses, not instead of them.

## The case that HBM breaks it

Now the strongest version of the argument that this time the structure is genuinely different. It is a serious argument and it deserves to be put at full strength.

**Capacity is not supply until a customer's tests are passed.** This is the central point and it is genuinely new. In commodity DRAM, a wafer of finished chips was saleable to anyone who wanted that density and speed grade. In HBM it is saleable only to a customer who has qualified that specific part on that specific accelerator, and qualification is a programme measured in months against a moving specification. The evidence is not theoretical: Reuters reported in May 2024 that Samsung's HBM3 and HBM3E parts were failing Nvidia's qualification tests on heat and power — Samsung disputed the characterisation — and it was August 2024 before Reuters reported that its eight-layer HBM3E had cleared for use, with the twelve-layer version still pending. The largest memory manufacturer on earth, with the deepest balance sheet in the industry, spent something close to eighteen months unable to sell its newest product to the customer that mattered. In the old business, capacity was share. In this one, capacity that has not passed a test is a fixed cost with no revenue attached.

**More wafers do not mean proportionally more bits.** HBM consumes roughly three times the wafer capacity of standard DDR5 per gigabyte of output, per Tom's Hardware's 2026 reporting — the dies are laid out generously for bandwidth and thermal reasons, and stacking discards more of what is made. TrendForce made the consequence explicit in its 30 July 2026 outlook: because HBM requires significantly more wafer input than conventional DRAM, higher wafer starts do not translate proportionally into additional bit output, which constrains overall DRAM bit supply growth even as capacity rises. That sentence attacks the historical mechanism directly. The classical bust was a bit flood; if the industry's new product converts wafers into bits at a third of the old rate, the flood is a third of the size for the same concrete.

**The supply response is slower than it has ever been.** The same TrendForce outlook noted that although several suppliers plan to bring capacity online in 2027, construction schedules, equipment installation and materials will delay meaningful ramps until the second half of that year, with substantial output not expected until 2028. SK Hynix's own August 2026 approvals put the first Yongin cleanroom in June 2029 and the Cheongju NAND cleanroom in December 2028. A supply response that arrives three years after the price signal is a weaker regulator than one that arrives in eighteen months.

**And the terms have changed, which is where an accountant's interest sharpens.** At its fiscal third-quarter 2026 results in June 2026, Micron disclosed sixteen strategic customer agreements — take-or-pay arrangements running from calendar 2026 through 2030 — covering roughly twenty per cent of its DRAM volume and about a third of its NAND volume. Fourteen of the sixteen carried cumulative revenue at minimum prices of about one hundred billion dollars over their remaining term. Against them the company expected cash deposits and related financial commitments of about twenty-two billion dollars, of which approximately eighteen billion was cash and four billion letters of credit, and management said that when the agreements were fully executed roughly forty per cent of revenue would sit under fixed-price or price-ceiling terms struck at the market levels of the second quarter of 2026. Those figures were reported by Reuters, carried by US News, and by Investing.com in its account of the company's results slides, and appear in the company's own prepared remarks; they are the company's characterisation of its own contracts, not an audited schedule.

Read that disclosure the way chapter three taught you to read a filing rather than a headline. Eighteen billion dollars of customer cash, received before the product is made, is not revenue — it sits as a contract liability until the chips ship. But it is unconditional cash in the business, and it converts a supplier's most dangerous exposure, the price of a commodity two years from now, into a contractual minimum. Take-or-pay means the customer owes the money whether or not it takes the chips. In every previous downturn the memory maker's protection against a price collapse was its own cost position. In this one, part of the protection is a signed instrument.

**Finally, the demand is not a forecast.** It is an order book, and it derives from a physical constraint rather than a fashion. Inference — the metered, billable half of the AI workload — is rationed by memory bandwidth, and bandwidth is the thing these companies sell. That is the argument chapter twenty-one set out, and it is the reason the previous chapter's framework matters here: when the binding constraint moves, the profit follows it, and for the present generation of machines the constraint sits inside a stack of DRAM.

Add those together and the bull case is not credulous at all. It says: the supply response is slower, smaller per unit of capital, gated by a customer's tests, and partly pre-sold at contracted prices to buyers who have already paid deposits. Each of those five statements is documented.

## The case that it merely lengthens it

Now the counterweight, argued with the same seriousness. The structure of the argument is simple: take each protection above and find its expiry date.

**Every "sold out" is a statement about a period.** Capacity was reported pre-sold across all major suppliers for calendar 2026, and by mid-2026 the trade press was reporting commitments extending into 2027. Note what that does and does not say. It says nothing whatever about 2029, when the Yongin cleanroom opens, or 2030, when Micron's agreements end. Capacity being built now is not sold out, because most of it does not exist and the machines it will feed have not been designed. The sold-out condition is a description of a two-year window that moves forward one quarter at a time, and the industry has been in such windows before — at the top of every previous cycle.

**Contracts insure both parties.** A take-or-pay agreement with a price band protects the seller against a collapse and the buyer against a spike. Micron's own description makes this explicit: fixed prices or ceilings, struck at the market levels of a specific quarter. If prices keep climbing, the supplier has sold forty per cent of its revenue at 2026 prices into a 2028 market. If prices fall, the customer has committed to volumes it may not want at prices above the spot. Which party got the better of that insurance will be knowable only afterwards, and the answer will appear in the accounts of both. That is not a criticism of the contracts. It is the definition of what a hedge is: giving up the tail in exchange for the floor. An industry that hedges is an industry that expects prices to be volatile, which is not the behaviour of one that believes the cycle is over.

**Qualification slows a rival's entry. It does not stop qualified rivals from over-building.** This is the hinge of the whole argument, and the evidence has already turned. Reuters reported in 2026 that Nvidia had certified Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron for HBM4 supply on its Vera Rubin platform. TrendForce reported in January and February 2026 that SK Hynix was expected to supply about two-thirds of Nvidia's HBM4 with Samsung's initial share projected in the mid-twenties per cent, and that Samsung would begin shipping after the Lunar New Year. Set that beside Samsung's eighteen-month failure in the previous generation. The qualification barrier is real, but it is a barrier each supplier crosses once per generation, and all three have now crossed it for the generation that matters. Three qualified suppliers is exactly the number that produced 2023.

**The conventional half of the business never stopped being a commodity.** All three companies still earn a large share of revenue from ordinary DRAM and NAND, priced the old way. TrendForce's 30 July 2026 outlook is instructive precisely because it is not uniform: it described a diverging 2027, with DRAM supply remaining tight while NAND flash supply conditions ease. Within the same companies, in the same year, one product line is forecast to stay short and another to loosen. A supercycle that is already normalising in one of its two main products is a supercycle with an internal counter-example.

**The wafer-intensity argument runs both ways.** If HBM consumes roughly three times the wafers per gigabyte, then a slackening in HBM demand releases capacity that produces roughly three times the bits when redirected to conventional memory. The same conversion factor that damps the boom amplifies the bust. No figure is being asserted here; the arithmetic of the ratio simply does not care which direction it is read in. This is the mechanism to have in mind if HBM demand ever grows more slowly than the capacity built for it: the surplus does not appear as unsold HBM, it appears as a flood of ordinary DRAM.

**And the fourth player is arriving, funded by a state.** Chapter twenty told this story twice: America lost the business to Japan, Japan lost it to Korea, and on each occasion the entrant was an outsider with patient, politically motivated capital and nothing to lose from a price war. CNBC reported on 31 July 2026 on the market debut of CXMT, China's principal DRAM maker, in Shanghai. Tom's Hardware reported in 2026 that the company was targeting around thirty per cent of the global DRAM market by 2030 with a sixth mega-fab, and separately reported research suggesting its wafer capacity was approaching Micron's during 2026 — with the caveat, in the same reporting, that its plans are constrained by access to advanced chipmaking tools. Those are trade reports of third-party research and company ambition, not audited figures, and the tool constraint is a genuine one that chapter sixty-nine describes. But an entrant does not need to reach the technical frontier to break a price. It needs only to be adequate in the commodity segment and indifferent to returns. That is precisely how the previous two rotations of this industry began.

### What the depreciation does next

Here is the part of the argument that belongs to this book's profession, and it is the reason the capital commitments matter more than the price forecasts.

Capital expenditure is an act of will. Depreciation is what that act becomes, automatically, later, whatever anyone then wants. When a memory maker commits thirty-eight billion dollars to plant that opens in 2029, it is not only deciding to add supply; it is scheduling a fixed charge against profit that begins on the day the plant is placed in service and continues for its assumed useful life, at a rate nobody can adjust downwards because demand disappointed.

Take an illustrative memory maker, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Suppose it commits twenty billion dollars of plant that enters service in 2029, depreciated over ten years: two billion dollars a year of new fixed cost, arriving on schedule. Suppose the plant, when it opens, is capable of producing product that would have earned four billion dollars a year at the prices ruling when it was approved. At those prices it is a fine investment. Now suppose that by the time it opens, prices for its output are thirty per cent lower, because three companies each approved a comparable plant in the same eighteen months. Revenue is two point eight billion; depreciation alone is two billion of it; and the cash costs of running the fab have not moved. The plant is still worth running — chapter twenty explained why an asset with sunk costs runs anyway — but the reported margin from that plant has gone from comfortable to marginal without a single operational failure. Nothing went wrong. Three boards each made a rational decision in 2026, and arithmetic did the rest in 2029.

That illustrative example is the entire content of the phrase "a supercycle is still a cycle". The cycle is not a mood or a failure of nerve. It is a fixed cost with a start date, created by a decision taken three years earlier, meeting a price the decision could not see.

And the decisions have been taken. TrendForce projected in November 2025 that DRAM industry capital expenditure would rise from 53.7 billion dollars in 2025 to 61.3 billion in 2026 — SK Hynix at 20.5 billion, up seventeen per cent, Samsung at about 20 billion, up eleven, and Micron at 13.5 billion, up twenty-three. Data Centre Dynamics separately reported Samsung seeking to expand production capacity by around fifty per cent in 2026. The August 2026 approvals at SK Hynix sit on top of those projections, not inside them: about 35.2 trillion won for the Yongin Y2 DRAM fab, construction beginning July 2027 with a first cleanroom due June 2029, and about 19.1 trillion won for the M17 NAND fab at Cheongju, breaking ground February 2027 with a cleanroom due December 2028, within a larger Yongin blueprint reported at 600 trillion won whose completion target the company pulled forward from 2045 to 2033.

A company that declines to build hands share to the two that do not decline, and the punishment for building too much arrives years after the punishment for building too little.

## So: broken, or lengthened?

The honest answer is that the public record as at August 2026 supports lengthened, and does not support broken — and the reason is structural rather than a matter of judgement.

Consider what "broken" would actually require. It would require at least one of three things to be true. First, that capacity could be added in months rather than years, so that supply tracked demand instead of overshooting it — the opposite of what every construction schedule above states. Second, that a supplier were permanently unsubstitutable, so that price could be held by a single hand — contradicted by three suppliers now certified on the same platform. Or third, that demand growth would permanently outrun any feasible supply response, so that the overshoot never arrived.

The third is what the bull case is really claiming, and it is worth saying plainly that it is not an absurd claim. It is, however, a claim about the future of a demand curve, which no filing discloses and no chapter of this book will forecast. It has been made at the top of every previous shortage in this industry and it has been wrong every time; it has also never previously been made about a product whose demand derives from a physical bottleneck in the world's fastest-growing category of capital spending. Both of those sentences are true simultaneously, which is uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the accurate position.

What the evidence does establish is narrower and more durable. Qualification, wafer intensity and construction lead times have made the supply response slower and less bit-productive per dollar than at any previous point in the industry's history. Contracts and deposits have moved a meaningful share of two suppliers' revenue out of the spot market for several years. Those are genuine changes, they are documented, and they mean that if the turn comes it should be shallower and later than the ones in chapter twenty. None of them touches the two facts that generate a cycle: capacity is decided years before it is needed, and each supplier decides separately while watching the same customers.

This book has no view on any share price and offers none here. The mechanism, though, can be assessed on the record, and on the record the machine has been slowed rather than dismantled.

## The decade, and what to watch inside it

Call it a decade because the commitments now signed are decade-length, and that is the honest unit of analysis. Micron's agreements run to 2030. SK Hynix's Yongin cluster is targeted for completion in 2033, with the first cleanroom in 2029. The chief executive quoted at the top of this chapter forecast demand outstripping supply beyond 2030. Whatever answer arrives will arrive inside that span, and it will be visible in the accounts before it is visible in the headlines.

Chapter twenty-two set out the standing watch-list for these companies — inventory days, the gap between capital expenditure and depreciation, the mix between contracted HBM and spot-priced conventional memory, and the customer-concentration notes. Those remain the foundation and are not repeated here. What this chapter adds are the four items specific to the question it has been asking, each of which is a place where "broken" and "lengthened" produce different disclosures.

**The contract horizon, not the contract.** The interesting number is not that Micron has sixteen agreements running to 2030. It is what fraction of capacity is committed beyond the current window, and on what terms, at each renewal. A supplier extending its committed book further out at firm prices is evidence of durable structure. A supplier whose committed book stays the same length while capacity grows is running an increasing share of output into the spot market, whatever the headline says. Watch the disclosed duration and coverage, quarter by quarter.

**The customer deposit balance.** This is the newest and least-watched line in the sector. Deposits and advance payments sit as contract liabilities on the balance sheet, and they are the customers' own view of scarcity expressed in cash. A rising balance means buyers are still paying to be at the front of a queue. A flat balance while capacity expands, or an unwinding one, means the queue has stopped being worth paying for — and it would show months before any price index moved. Note also the accounting subtlety: as those deposits are applied against shipments, revenue rises while cash does not, so a company drawing down deposits can report growth with deteriorating cash conversion. Compare operating cash flow with reported profit whenever this line is large.

**Share reallocation at each generational hand-off.** In a business where each generation resets the race, the erosion of an advantage looks like a share table, not a price chart. The HBM4 allocations reported by TrendForce in early 2026 — roughly two-thirds to SK Hynix, an initial share in the mid-twenties per cent to Samsung, the remainder to Micron — are already a wider distribution than earlier generations achieved. If the next generation distributes wider still, the qualification moat is doing less work, and the premium it supports will follow. This is one of the few places where an early warning appears as a fact rather than an opinion.

**The fourth entrant's qualification, not its capacity.** Chinese DRAM capacity is widely reported and its bit output will be argued about endlessly. The observable event that matters is narrower: whether a Chinese supplier's memory is qualified into a non-Chinese customer's high-volume product. Capacity is not supply until a customer's tests are passed — that is the bull case's own central proposition, and it applies to entrants exactly as it applies to incumbents. Until that happens, a new entrant pressures the commodity segment. After it happens, it pressures everything.

To which one date deserves to be written down on its own: June 2029, when the first Yongin cleanroom is due. The concrete was ordered in a shortage during 2026. It will open into whatever 2029 turns out to be, and it will begin depreciating on the day it does, at a rate decided by an assumption made years earlier. That is the sentence this industry has typed at the top of every cycle it has ever had, and the only thing genuinely in question is how long the interval has become between typing it and reading it back.

Three companies spent forty years learning to survive the worst business in semiconductors, and then found themselves selling the one component the most expensive machines on earth cannot work without. They have earned every point of the margins in chapter twenty-two. They are also, at this moment, doing the thing that has ended every previous shortage — carefully, deliberately, with full knowledge of the history, and in triplicate.
