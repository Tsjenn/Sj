# What a Market Capitalisation Actually Is

A headline says that Nvidia is worth five point three trillion dollars. It is
the most repeated sentence in this industry and it is doing far less work than
it appears to. Nobody has valued Nvidia. Nobody has paid five point three
trillion dollars for it, and nobody could. The number is the output of a
multiplication, performed automatically, using two inputs: the number of shares
in issue, and the price at which the most recent handful of them changed hands.

That is the whole of it. Everything else — the comparisons to the economies of
countries, the talk of a company being "bigger than" an entire industry — is
commentary laid on top of a product of two numbers, one of which is set by a
few thousand shares trading on a Tuesday afternoon.

This chapter is about learning to hear that sentence properly. Not to dismiss
it: market capitalisation is a real and useful number, and this book quotes it
often. But it measures something narrower than most readers assume, it moves
for reasons that have nothing to do with the company, and it is close to
useless as a way of ranking two businesses against each other. By the end of
this chapter the aim is a mild, permanent flinch whenever a headline leads with
it.

## The formula, and nothing more

Market capitalisation is the last traded price of one share multiplied by the
number of shares in issue. Both inputs are observable. Neither is stable.

The clearest proof that the number is a product rather than a measurement is a
stock split. On 22 May 2024 Nvidia announced a ten-for-one forward split of its
common stock; holders of record at the close on 6 June 2024 received nine
additional shares each, and trading began on a split-adjusted basis on 10 June
2024 (Nvidia's own filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, May
2024). Overnight, every shareholder held ten times as many shares, each worth
roughly a tenth as much. Nothing happened inside the company. No factory was
built, no chip was designed, no customer signed anything. The share price fell
by about ninety per cent and the market capitalisation did not move at all,
because the split changed one input and the other input adjusted to compensate.

Hold on to that. A number that can be cut by ninety per cent by an
administrative action, with no effect on the total, is not measuring the
company. It is measuring one slice of the company.

### Which shares count

The share count sounds like the easy half. It is not.

Start with the distinction between shares issued and shares outstanding.
Companies buy back their own shares and hold them in treasury; those shares
still exist legally but are excluded from the count. Then there is dilution
running the other way: technology companies pay a large part of employee
compensation in stock, so new shares appear every quarter. The two forces run
against each other, and the net result is a share count that drifts rather than
sits still.

Nvidia's weighted-average diluted share count was about 24.4 billion for the
financial year ended 25 January 2026, slightly below the prior year, as
buybacks more than offset shares issued to employees (compilations of Nvidia's
filings by GuruFocus and Macrotrends, 2026). Take that count and the share
price of 214.72 dollars quoted for 22 August 2026 (Investing.com quote data),
multiply the two, and you get roughly 5.24 trillion dollars — near enough to
the headline figure to show that the formula is the formula, and that nothing
mysterious is going on underneath it.

Apple shows the same arithmetic pointed in the other direction over many years.
The company had roughly 26 billion split-adjusted shares outstanding when Tim
Cook became chief executive, and roughly 15 billion at the end of its 2025
financial year, after repurchasing about 90.7 billion dollars of stock in that
year alone (The Motley Fool, August 2026, and Macrotrends compilations of
Apple's filings). If the share count falls by two or three per cent a year and
the market capitalisation stays flat, each remaining share has quietly become
more valuable without the business changing at all. The headline number hides
that; the share count does not.

Three further traps in the share count are worth knowing before you trust any
ranking:

**Dual-class structures.** Some companies have share classes that are not
listed at all. Alphabet's Class B shares, held by insiders, do not trade on any
exchange and therefore have no market price of their own; a market
capitalisation for the whole company has to price them off a class that does
trade. This is a convention, not a measurement.

**Multiple listings.** A company can be listed in one country and carry
depositary receipts in another. The market capitalisation belongs to the whole
company, not to the receipts. Counting only the shares visible in your own
market gives an answer that is wrong by a large multiple.

**Currency.** A market capitalisation quoted in US dollars for a company that
reports in another currency contains an exchange rate. When the dollar moves,
the ranking moves, and no share has traded.

### Which price counts

The price input is stranger still. It is the price of the last trade, which is
to say the price agreed between one buyer and one seller for one parcel of
shares, applied retrospectively to every share in existence.

Take an illustrative company, with round numbers chosen for clarity and
belonging to no real business. It has one hundred million shares in issue.
Yesterday the last trade of the day was at ten dollars, so its market
capitalisation was one billion dollars. Today, on a quiet afternoon, two
hundred thousand shares — one fifth of one per cent of the company — change
hands at eleven dollars. The market capitalisation is now 1.1 billion dollars.
One hundred million dollars of value has been created by a trade worth 2.2
million dollars.

That is the mechanism, and it is not a flaw. Prices at the margin are how
markets work, and for a liquid company the marginal price is a reasonable guide
to what the next parcel would fetch. But it carries an assumption that is
almost never stated: that the price would hold if everyone tried to sell at
once. It would not. Nobody has ever tested a trillion-dollar market
capitalisation by liquidating it, and nobody ever will. The figure is the price
of the marginal share, extrapolated across shares that are not for sale.

## It is a claim about the future, not a measure of the present

Once you see the number as a price rather than a valuation, the next question
is what the price is a price of. It is a price for a claim on all the cash the
company will produce from now until it ceases to exist, discounted back to
today.

Every term in that sentence points forward. Yesterday's profits are already in
the bank; the share does not entitle you to them. What you are buying is
tomorrow's. This is why a company with no profits at all can carry a large
market capitalisation, and why a highly profitable company in a shrinking
industry can carry a small one. The market is not scoring the past. It is
pricing a forecast, and doing so through the aggregated guesses of everyone
willing to trade.

The crudest way to see how much future is embedded in a price is to divide the
market capitalisation by the most recent year's profit. What follows is
arithmetic on two figures, each given with its date and source below; it is not
a reported statistic, and the caveats after the table matter as much as the
table.

| Company | Market capitalisation | Last reported full-year net income | Cap ÷ net income |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nvidia | ~5.3 trn USD, mid-Aug 2026 | 120.1 bn USD, FY ended 25 Jan 2026 | ~44× |
| Apple | ~4.41 trn USD, mid-Aug 2026 | 112.0 bn USD, FY ended 27 Sep 2025 | ~39× |
| Alphabet | ~4.20 trn USD, mid-Aug 2026 | 132.2 bn USD, year ended 31 Dec 2025 | ~32× |
| Microsoft | ~3.66 trn USD, mid-Aug 2026 | 133.7 bn USD, FY ended 30 Jun 2026 | ~27× |
| TSMC | ~2.17 trn USD, Aug 2026 | 55.2 bn USD, year ended 31 Dec 2025 | ~39× |

Sources: market capitalisations from companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool
rankings, mid-August 2026, all of which move daily; Nvidia net income from its
fourth-quarter and fiscal 2026 results release, February 2026; Apple from its
fourth-quarter results, October 2025; Alphabet from its fourth-quarter and full
year 2025 release, 4 February 2026; Microsoft from its fourth-quarter fiscal
2026 release, 29 July 2026; TSMC from its fourth-quarter 2025 results filing,
January 2026, where 2025 net income of NT$1,717.88 billion was given as 55.21
billion US dollars.

A multiple of around forty says, very roughly, that the price today is forty
times last year's profit. It does not mean investors expect to wait forty years
to get their money back; it means they expect profits to be much larger than
last year's, and they are paying now for that expectation. A multiple of
twenty-seven says the same thing more cautiously.

Now the caveats, which are the actual lesson. The five fiscal years in that
table end on five different dates spread across ten months. Nvidia's year ended
in January 2026 and Microsoft's in June 2026; between those two dates the
industry moved considerably. The market capitalisations are all from one week
in August 2026, so every row compares a current price against a profit figure
of a different vintage. Worse, one of the profit figures is not what it looks
like: Microsoft disclosed that net gains from its investment in OpenAI
increased full-year net income by 4,963 million dollars and diluted earnings
per share by 67 cents in fiscal 2026 (Microsoft's results release, 29 July
2026). That is a gain on an investment, not a dollar of software sold, and CNBC
reported in August 2026 that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic have
been distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them.
Divide by a number that contains that, and the multiple you get is answering a
question you did not ask.

None of this makes the exercise useless. It makes it a starting point that
demands the second question: profit of what, measured how, over which period?

## What it is not: money the company has

The most common misreading of a market capitalisation is that it is a pile of
money somewhere. It is not. Not one dollar of it sits in the company's bank
account, and not one dollar of it can be spent by management.

The comparison is worth doing precisely. Nvidia's headline market
capitalisation in mid-August 2026 was roughly 5.3 trillion dollars
(companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings). Its actual cash, cash
equivalents and marketable securities at its financial year end on 25 January
2026 were 62.6 billion dollars (Nvidia's annual report on Form 10-K for that
year). The headline is about eighty-five times the cash. The cash is the part
the company can use to pay wages, build inventory or buy a business. The
headline is the price other people are placing on its shares.

Cash flow is closer still to the point. Nvidia reported revenue of 215.9
billion dollars for fiscal 2026, up 65 per cent, with operating cash flow of
102.7 billion dollars and free cash flow of 96.6 billion (Nvidia results
release, February 2026). Those are figures the company generated. They belong
on the same page as the market capitalisation and they say something different
and more durable.

Market capitalisation is not entirely inert, though, and it would be wrong to
teach that it does not matter to the business. Three things flow from a high
share price. First, shares become an acquisition currency: a company whose
stock is highly priced can buy another company by issuing paper rather than
paying cash, and it pays away less ownership to do so. Second, share-based
compensation becomes cheaper in dilution terms — the same dollar value of stock
handed to an employee represents fewer shares. Third, a high and liquid market
value widens access to debt markets and to index inclusion, which brings
buyers who are not expressing any opinion at all.

So the price has consequences. It is still not a bank balance, and a headline
that treats it as one is teaching the reader something false.

### Market capitalisation and enterprise value

There is a second number that professionals reach for when market
capitalisation will not do the job. Enterprise value is market capitalisation
plus debt, less cash. The logic is that if you were buying the whole business,
you would inherit its borrowings and you would get to keep the cash in the till,
so the effective price is neither the market capitalisation alone nor the cash
alone.

The practical consequence is that two companies with identical market
capitalisations can be very differently priced. One financed by borrowing and
one sitting on net cash are not the same purchase. Neither ranking tables nor
headlines make this adjustment, because it requires opening the balance sheet.
This book will make it where it matters, and will say so when it does.

## Why it moves when nothing has happened

The share count changes slowly. The price does not, and it responds to
information that has nothing to do with the company whose shares are being
priced.

On 27 January 2025, Nvidia lost 589 billion dollars of market value in a single
trading session, its shares falling about 17 per cent — at the time the largest
one-day loss of value by any company in market history, and more than double
Nvidia's own previous record of 279 billion dollars set on 3 September 2024
(Bloomberg and CNBC, 27 January 2025). The cause was the release of a model by
the Chinese laboratory DeepSeek, which raised the question of whether frontier
capability required as much hardware as had been assumed.

Read that carefully, because it is the clearest illustration in recent memory of
what the number is. Nvidia shipped the same chips on 28 January that it had
shipped on 24 January. Its order book, its customers, its manufacturing
capacity, its people and its intellectual property were unchanged. Nothing
inside the company was different. What changed was the market's forecast of how
much of its product the world would need — and since the price is a claim on
the future, a change in the forecast is a change in the price. Five hundred and
eighty-nine billion dollars is not money that was lost. It is a number that was
revised.

The same mechanism runs upwards. Nvidia became the first company to close above
a five trillion dollar market capitalisation on 29 October 2025; CNBC reported
at the time that the company had been worth four trillion three months earlier
and one trillion a little over two years before that. By mid-August 2026 it sat
at roughly 5.3 to 5.4 trillion, with the shares quoted at 214.72 dollars on 22
August 2026 against a fifty-two-week range of 164.07 to 236.54 dollars
(Investing.com quote data). That range is worth pausing on. Over one year the
price moved through a band of more than forty per cent from low to high, which
means the same company carried market capitalisations differing by well over a
trillion dollars depending on the day you looked.

There are at least four reasons a market capitalisation moves that have nothing
to do with events inside the company.

**The discount rate.** The price is future cash discounted to today. When
interest rates rise, the discount is harsher, and every long-dated claim falls
in value simultaneously. A central bank meeting can re-price an entire industry
without a single company doing anything.

**Sentiment about the sector.** Investors trade themes. When one company in a
supply chain disappoints, the others move with it, because the market is
adjusting its estimate of a shared future rather than assessing each business.

**Flows rather than opinions.** Index funds buy a company because it is in the
index, in proportion to its float-adjusted weight. Those purchases contain no
view. When a company's weight rises, buying follows automatically, and the
price rises without anyone forming a judgement.

**Currency and location.** For companies priced in one currency and quoted in
another, the exchange rate does part of the work.

This is why a book of this kind is careful with the word "worth". A company is
not worth its market capitalisation in any sense an accountant would sign off.
It is priced at its market capitalisation, on a given day, by the last people
who traded.

## Why comparing two market capitalisations tells you so little

Ranking tables invite a comparison that they cannot support. Consider two rows
from the figures already given: Nvidia at roughly 5.3 trillion dollars in
mid-August 2026, and TSMC at roughly 2.17 trillion in August 2026
(companiesmarketcap). The obvious reading is that Nvidia is about two and a
half times the company TSMC is.

Now put their operating figures beside them. Nvidia reported revenue of 215.9
billion dollars for the year to 25 January 2026 (company results release,
February 2026). TSMC reported 2025 revenue of 122.42 billion US dollars, up
35.9 per cent, with a gross margin of 59.9 per cent and net income of 55.21
billion US dollars (TSMC's fourth-quarter 2025 results filing, January 2026).
The revenue gap is smaller than the market capitalisation gap, and TSMC's gross
margin — the subject of chapter three — is a number most manufacturers on earth
would regard as impossible.

The market is not saying TSMC is a worse business. It is saying something about
the expected trajectory of the two, the capital each must spend to grow, and
the durability of each position. TSMC guided 2026 capital expenditure to 52 to
56 billion US dollars, above analyst expectations (company results, January
2026); a business that must spend that much to stay in place converts revenue
into free cash differently from one that does not. Every one of those judgements
is contestable, and the market capitalisation compresses all of them into a
single figure that shows none of its working.

So compare the working instead. When you want to know which of two companies is
the stronger business, the figures that repay attention are, roughly in order:

Revenue, and whether it is growing or merely large. Gross margin, which is the
scoreboard of pricing power. Operating cash flow, which is harder to flatter
than profit. Capital expenditure, because cash spent on plant is not available
to shareholders and reveals what the business must pay to keep its position.
Free cash flow, which is what is left. And customer concentration, which no
ranking table has ever shown and which can matter more than all the rest.

Those figures are comparable across companies in a way prices are not, because
they are prepared under accounting standards, audited, and published on a
schedule. They are also duller, which is why headlines lead with the price.

To see how far apart two identically-priced companies can be, take two
illustrative businesses, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to
no real company. Both carry a market capitalisation of two hundred billion
dollars on the same day.

| Illustrative figures (not real companies) | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Market capitalisation | 200 bn | 200 bn |
| Revenue | 40 bn | 40 bn |
| Gross margin | 60% | 30% |
| Operating cash flow | 14 bn | 9 bn |
| Capital expenditure | 2 bn | 8 bn |
| Free cash flow | 12 bn | 1 bn |
| Net cash / (net debt) | 20 bn | (30 bn) |
| Largest customer | 8% of revenue | 55% of revenue |

The ranking table records these two as equals, because on that day they were
priced the same. They are not remotely the same business. Company A converts
most of its margin into cash it can hand to owners; Company B must put most of
what it earns back into plant merely to stay in the game, and would lose more
than half its revenue if one telephone call went badly. Adjust for the balance
sheets and the effective purchase price of B is thirty billion dollars higher
than A's, not equal to it. Everything that distinguishes them is in the
accounts and none of it is in the headline.

One further distortion belongs here: free float. The market capitalisation
counts every share, but only some of those shares are available to trade.
Founders, families, states and long-term holders sit on blocks that never come
to market. Where the float is small relative to the total, the marginal price
that sets the value of the whole is being discovered on an unusually thin
slice, and it moves further on less news. Index providers weight companies by
float rather than by total shares for exactly this reason. Headlines do not.

One more warning about ranking tables, and it applies to the one in this
chapter as much as any other: they are assembled from figures gathered at
different moments. The market capitalisations quoted here are from a single
period in August 2026, drawn from companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings,
and they move every day the market is open. Broadcom stood at roughly 1.61
trillion dollars and AMD at roughly 260 billion in early 2026 per statista and
alpha-sense rankings — figures from a different point in the year, which is
exactly why they should not be lined up in the same column as the August ones
and treated as a ranking. This book gives the figures it has verified, with the
date attached, and leaves the gaps visible rather than filling them with
estimates.

## The number that is not a market capitalisation at all

Two of the most consequential companies in this book have no market
capitalisation, because they have no listed shares. What they have is a
valuation, and the two are not the same thing.

Anthropic closed a 65 billion dollar Series H at a 965 billion dollar
post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly making it the most valuable private
company in the world (CNBC, May 2026). OpenAI closed a 122 billion dollar round
in March 2026 at an 852 billion dollar valuation, with Amazon reported as
investing 50 billion dollars in that round (valueaddvc and Forbes reporting,
2026).

A post-money valuation is calculated by taking the price paid for the newest
shares and applying it to every share in the company, including shares issued
years earlier on entirely different terms. It is the same extrapolation as a
market capitalisation, performed on a single negotiated transaction rather than
a continuous market — and the terms of that transaction, which may include
liquidation preferences and other protections for the new money, are usually
not public. A listed company's price is set daily by strangers with no
relationship to the company. A private valuation is set occasionally, by
investors who negotiated it and who benefit from it being high.

That is not an accusation of anything. It is a description of a mechanism, and
it is why this book reports private valuations as what someone paid, when, and
as reported by whom — never as an audited measure of worth. Chapter five takes
this apart properly.

## How to read a market-cap headline

Everything in this chapter reduces to a short discipline. When a market capitalisation
appears in front of you, ask five questions before you let it mean anything.

**What date is this?** A price without a date is not information. This
industry re-prices weekly, and figures in a printed book are photographs of a
moving object. Every figure in this book carries a date for that reason.

**Who said so?** Rankings are compiled by data providers, not audited. Two
providers can differ on the same company on the same day because they treat
share classes or currencies differently.

**What is the share count, and is it moving?** Buybacks shrink it, employee
compensation swells it, and the direction of travel tells you something about
how a company treats its owners.

**What is the price a price of?** A claim on future cash flows, priced at the
margin by whoever traded last. Not a bank balance. Not an appraisal. Not a
promise.

**What are the cash flows?** The only figures that let you compare two
companies honestly are the ones prepared to a standard and published on a
schedule.

None of this is a reason to ignore the number. Market capitalisation is
genuinely useful: it tells you what expectations are attached to a business, and
a change in it tells you those expectations have shifted. It is a thermometer
of belief, and belief moves capital. Later chapters use it constantly, because
the story of this industry is partly a story of where belief has migrated —
from the applications at the top of the stack towards the silicon at the bottom.

But it is a thermometer, not a balance sheet, and it should never be the last
number you look at. It should be the first, and the one you leave behind
quickest.

For what it is worth: every figure in this chapter will be wrong by the time it
is read. Market capitalisations move daily and are published continuously by
data providers and by the exchanges themselves; the company figures behind them
are updated each quarter in filings that any reader can pull up and check for
free. Where this book quotes a price, treat it as an illustration of a
mechanism, not as a fact about today. Where it quotes a filing, the figure is
fixed but the period it covers is not the period you are living in.

And a statement that belongs here and will be repeated: this book gives no
price targets, recommends no purchase or sale of anything, and makes no
prediction about where any share price goes next. Its purpose is narrower and
more useful — to leave the reader able to open the accounts and reach their own
conclusion.
