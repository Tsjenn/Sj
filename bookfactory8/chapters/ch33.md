# What a Model Company Actually Sells

In mid-August 2026, Anthropic told its investors that its annualised revenue run rate had reached sixty-five billion US dollars at the end of July — roughly a sevenfold increase on a year earlier — and that preliminary second-quarter revenue had exceeded eleven and a half billion dollars, against seven hundred and eighty-seven million for the same quarter of the previous year, as reported by CNBC in August 2026. Those are remarkable numbers by any standard in the history of commercial software. Now notice the thing about them that matters for this part of the book: not one of them is audited, not one was filed with a regulator, and every one of them reached the public because the company decided it should.

That is the condition under which the next several chapters operate. The model layer is dominated by two private companies that were valued, in their 2026 fundraising rounds, close to a trillion dollars each — Anthropic at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, as reported by CNBC, and OpenAI at eight hundred and fifty-two billion in March 2026, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes. Companies of that reported size would ordinarily come with decades of filings to read. These come with none. Before telling their histories, then, it is worth doing something this book has done for every other layer: set out what the business actually is. What does a model company sell, to whom, on what terms, and where — if anywhere — does the margin live?

The answer is stranger than it looks from the outside, because a model company sells one product in five different wrappers, and the economics of the five wrappers are not the same.

## One product, five ways to bill for it

Strip away the branding and a frontier model company produces a single thing: the capacity to answer. A trained model, running on rented or partner-owned computers, converting a request into a response. Everything on the revenue side of the business is a different way of charging for access to that capacity.

The first wrapper is the consumer subscription. An individual pays a flat monthly fee — twenty US dollars a month has been the standard tier at both major labs, with OpenAI's two-hundred-dollar Pro tier above it, per the published pricing compared across vendors in 2026 — and receives access that is, within limits, unmetered. This is the line the public knows, because it is the line the public pays. It is also, from an accountant's chair, the oddest one. Classic software subscriptions work because the marginal cost of serving one more user is close to nothing; the subscription is almost pure gross margin once the product exists. A model subscription inverts that. Every answer costs real compute, so the cost of serving a subscriber varies with how much the subscriber uses the product, while the price does not. A light user is profitable. A heavy user may cost more to serve than they pay. The usage caps, the throttles and the tier structures that annoy subscribers are not product decisions; they are gross margin management, applied one customer at a time.

The second wrapper is the business seat: the same product sold to a company rather than a person, at a higher price per user — Claude's team seat was thirty dollars per user per month against twenty for the individual tier, per the same published 2026 pricing — with administration, security and billing wrapped around it. The economics resemble the consumer line with two improvements. The price per seat is higher for a serving cost that is broadly similar, and the customer churns more slowly, because a tool bought by a company and wired into its working habits is harder to cancel than a personal direct debit.

The third wrapper is the API: access sold to developers and product companies, metered by the token and priced per million of them. This is the purest line in the business and the one an analyst should always look for first. Revenue is price times volume; cost is compute times volume; the two move together, so the unit economics are visible in a way the flat-rate lines never allow. It is also the line with the least protection. An API customer can route each request to whichever model is cheapest or best that week, and an entire small industry of routing and orchestration tools exists to help them do exactly that. Metered pricing is honest pricing, and honest pricing invites comparison.

The fourth wrapper is the enterprise agreement: a negotiated contract with a large corporation or a government, typically involving committed spend over a term, volume discounts, service commitments and terms that neither side publishes. From outside, this line is almost perfectly opaque. What can be said is structural: committed contracts smooth revenue, deepen the relationship, and move the business away from the per-token spot market — and they are where a growing share of the money is. Anthropic in particular built its book this way: the company said in October 2025 that it had more than three hundred thousand business customers, and third-party profiles published in 2026, including Sacra's, put roughly eighty per cent of its revenue on the API and business side rather than the consumer side.

The fifth wrapper is licensing to a cloud: the model made available through a hyperscaler's own platform, with the cloud provider billing its customers and the model company taking a negotiated share. This line trades margin for distribution. The cloud brings customers the lab could not reach and capacity the lab does not own; in exchange it stands between the lab and the customer, and takes its cut. The extreme case was the arrangement between OpenAI and Microsoft, which for years ran in both directions — Microsoft with exclusive rights to resell OpenAI's models through Azure, and revenue-sharing flowing both ways. In April 2026 the two companies restructured it: OpenAI's payments to Microsoft were set at twenty per cent of revenue through 2030 and capped at thirty-eight billion dollars, Microsoft stopped paying a revenue share to OpenAI, Microsoft's licence to the models was extended to 2032 but made explicitly non-exclusive, and OpenAI became free to sell through any cloud, as reported by CNBC and Forbes in April 2026. Chapter thirty-five treats that relationship in full; for now the point is simply that "licensing to a cloud" is a real revenue line with real, negotiated, private terms — and that those terms can move billions between two income statements without a single customer noticing.

Set side by side, the five lines look like this.

| Revenue line | Who pays | How it is priced | Cost behaviour | What to watch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer subscriptions | Individuals | Flat monthly fee | Cost varies with use; price does not | Usage caps and tier changes — margin repair in public |
| Business seats | Firms, per user | Per seat per month | As consumer, at a better price | Seats sold vs price per seat |
| API usage | Developers, product companies | Metered per token | Cost and revenue move together | Price per token against cost per token |
| Enterprise agreements | Large corporations, governments | Negotiated commitments over a term | Discounted, smoothed, private | Committed spend and renewal terms, where disclosed |
| Licensing to a cloud | A hyperscaler, reselling | Negotiated share of revenue | Depends who owns the machines | Who owns the customer relationship |

One feature is common to all five. In every case the direct cost of earning the revenue is compute — inference, the running of the trained model — and in every case that compute is mostly bought from the layer below, from clouds that are variously the lab's landlord, investor, distributor and competitor. The model layer's cost of sales is the compute layer's revenue. Keep that in mind through everything that follows, because it is the thread that ties this part of the book to the last one.

## The margin lives in inference

Ask where a model company's gross margin comes from and the instinctive answer is wrong. The instinct says training: the vast, headline-grabbing cost of creating the model. In fact, gross margin has almost nothing to do with training. It is decided by inference — the cost of answering, request by request, after the model exists.

The mechanics are simple. Revenue is what the five lines above bring in. Cost of revenue is dominated by the compute consumed serving customers: the machines, mostly rented, that run the model every time somebody asks it something. Gross margin is the difference. Training sits elsewhere in the income statement, as a research and development cost, below the gross margin line — a placement dealt with in the next section, because it is a choice with consequences.

What makes the model layer unusual is how large that cost of serving is. In software as the last two decades knew it, cost of revenue was a rounding item and gross margins of eighty per cent and above were ordinary. In the model layer, on the reported figures, serving costs consume most of the revenue dollar. For Anthropic, figures shared with investors and reported in 2026 by PitchBook and Morningstar put compute at about seventy-one cents per dollar of revenue in the first quarter of 2026, a projected fifty-six cents in the second, and gross margin tracking around forty-four per cent. For OpenAI, gross margin in the first quarter of 2026 was reported by The Information at roughly thirty-nine per cent, up from about thirty-three per cent a year earlier. Both sets of figures are unaudited, both reached the press through people with reasons to share them, and the two companies do not necessarily define the measure identically — a private company's "gross margin" is whatever its spreadsheet says it is, and there is no auditor to argue. Treat them as indicative of scale, not as comparable accounting facts.

Indicative of scale is still useful. It says that a model company in 2026 was keeping perhaps forty cents of each revenue dollar before paying for research, training, staff, sales or anything else — against the eighty-plus cents a mature software business would keep. It also says the number was moving quickly, and in a favourable direction, at both companies. That movement has three drivers worth separating, because they will behave differently from here.

The first is engineering: serving a given model gets cheaper over time, through better utilisation of the machines, smarter batching of requests, quantisation and distillation of the models themselves. The second is mix: the wrappers do not earn the same margin, so as revenue shifts between subscriptions, seats, API and enterprise commitments, the blended margin moves with it — which is one reason the labs push so hard on the business lines. The third is pricing power, and it is the fragile one. Price per token is set in a competitive market where the customer can switch mid-week. A margin built on engineering gains is durable; a margin built on charging more than the lab across the street is not.

There is one more implication, and it reaches down the stack. Because cost of revenue is compute, a model company's gross margin is partly set by someone else's price list. The rent charged by the compute layer, which is itself a function of accelerator prices, power contracts and the depreciation assumptions examined in chapter thirty-two, flows directly into the model layer's cost of sales. When you read that a lab's margin improved, part of what you are reading may be its landlord's pricing, not its own efficiency. The accounts of the two layers are coupled, and no published document shows the coupling.

## The cost that looks like an asset

Now to training, and to a question with an accountant's shape: when a company spends billions of dollars creating a model that will earn revenue for years, why is that spending not on the balance sheet?

Consider what training produces. A completed frontier model is identifiable, it is controlled by the company, it was created at measurable cost, and it is expected to generate future economic benefit — which is, almost word for word, the textbook test for recognising an intangible asset. A factory that spent five billion dollars building a production line would capitalise it and depreciate it over its useful life. A model looks, on first inspection, like the same kind of thing: a large, deliberate expenditure that creates a productive asset. The capitalisation argument is not silly. That is precisely why it needs to be taken apart.

It mostly fails, and for reasons that reveal what kind of asset a model actually is. Accounting frameworks are deliberately hostile to capitalising research: under US practice, research and development cost is generally expensed as incurred, with only narrow exceptions, and under international standards development cost can be capitalised only once technical and commercial feasibility are demonstrable. A frontier training run sits awkwardly against those tests. When the run starts, nobody knows whether the result will be a product or a lesson; frontier training is research in the plain sense that it might not work. And even where the tests could arguably be met, two practical judgements push the same way. The useful life of a frontier model is brutally short — each generation is superseded by the next, often within a year or two, sometimes by the company's own release — so any capitalised amount would be amortised over a life so brief that the profit relief barely justifies the exercise. And a management that capitalises its research is choosing to report better profits now against worse ones later, in an industry where every observer knows the trick; the optics of conservative treatment are worth more than the deferral.

So the spending is, overwhelmingly, expensed as it happens. The consequence is an income statement that looks worse than the underlying economics and a balance sheet that shows almost nothing for the most valuable thing the company owns. The model that generates the entire revenue line is carried, in effect, at nil.

An illustration makes the reporting effect concrete. Take an illustrative lab, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It spends three billion dollars in Year One training a model, and earns nothing from it that year. In Year Two the model earns four billion dollars of revenue at a forty per cent gross margin — one point six billion of gross profit — while the lab spends five billion training its successor. Expensed, the accounts show a three billion dollar loss in Year One and a three point four billion dollar loss in Year Two, even though each model, taken alone, more than pays for itself over its life. Capitalised and amortised over two years, the same facts would show a nil result in Year One and a modest loss in Year Two. Cash is identical in every version. The choice of treatment changes when the pain is reported, not whether it exists — and the expensing convention front-loads all of it, which is why a fast-growing lab's income statement can look catastrophic while its unit economics quietly improve.

There is a final asymmetry worth savouring, because it connects this chapter to chapter four. When a cloud provider buys a GPU, the cloud capitalises it and depreciates it over an assumed life of several years. When a model company rents that same GPU to run a training job, the rent is an expense, immediately. The identical machine-hour is an asset in one set of accounts and a cost in another, and the border between the two treatments is a contract. A model company that builds its own data centres would move that spending from expense to asset and transform its reported losses without changing its economics at all. Watch for that migration in the disclosures; it matters more than it will be made to sound.

## Growing and burning at once

The structure above produces the model layer's signature financial shape, the one that confuses newspaper coverage more than any other: revenue growth and cash consumption that are both enormous, at the same time, in the same company, without contradiction.

The reported figures for early 2026 show the shape plainly. OpenAI's first quarter of 2026 brought revenue of five point seven billion dollars — and a cash burn of about three point seven billion in the same three months, as reported by The Information in 2026. Anthropic's preliminary second-quarter revenue exceeded eleven and a half billion dollars against seven hundred and eighty-seven million a year earlier, per CNBC in August 2026 — a fourteenfold increase — while the company was in the middle of spending the proceeds of the largest private funding round on record. Reuters reporting across 2025 and 2026 traced Anthropic's run rate from roughly one billion dollars at the start of 2025 to more than five billion by August 2025, thirty billion by April 2026 and past forty-seven billion in early May — a curve with no obvious precedent in enterprise software, climbed while the company remained, on any conventional measure, deeply cash-negative.

Three mechanisms produce this, and they compound.

The first is that growth itself costs money at the gross margin line. In most software, a new customer is nearly free to serve, so growth generates cash. Here, every new customer arrives with an inference bill attached, and at a forty-something per cent reported gross margin, sixty-ish cents of each new revenue dollar goes straight out of the door to the compute layer. Growth helps, but it helps at half strength.

The second is the training treadmill. The revenue being earned today comes from models trained one or two years ago; the money being spent today trains the models that will earn revenue one or two years from now; and each generation has cost more than the last. A lab that stopped training would see its costs collapse and its margins bloom — for the year or so it took its models to become uncompetitive. The burn is not waste. It is next year's product, expensed this year, exactly as the previous section described.

The third is that compute is increasingly bought in advance. Capacity commitments to clouds and chipmakers are contracted years forward and paid for on schedules that do not wait for the revenue they enable. The cash leaves before the tokens are served.

Put the three together and a striking conclusion follows: for a frontier lab in this period, the faster the growth, the larger the absolute burn, even while the unit economics improve. That is why the funding rounds are the size they are. Anthropic's sixty-five billion dollar Series H in May 2026 and OpenAI's one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round in March 2026 — with Amazon reported as putting fifty billion into the latter, per valueaddvc and Forbes — are not signs of distress, and they are not signs of health either. They are the working capital of the treadmill, and the identity of the providers is the tell: Amazon committed up to thirty-three billion dollars to Anthropic and Google up to forty-three billion including ten billion in April 2026, per 2026 reporting — the same clouds that sell the labs their compute. The money goes in as investment and comes back as rent. Chapter five called this pattern circular; the chapters on each company will weigh how much of it is circular in substance and not merely in shape.

Whether the shape resolves was, in 2026, beginning to be visible in the reported numbers — Anthropic's margin trajectory above being the clearest case — but "beginning to be visible in unaudited numbers" is a phrase to hold at arm's length, which brings this chapter to its real subject.

## The reporting standard for everything that follows

Here is what exists, and what does not, for the two companies at the centre of this part of the book.

There are no audited public financial statements. There are no regulatory filings of accounts, no segment notes, no cash flow statements, no audit opinions. What exists instead is a stream of disclosures the companies have chosen to make — investor updates, fundraising materials, statements to journalists — and a secondary stream of figures that reached the press through investors and counterparties who had their own reasons for sharing. Every number in the last three sections came from one of those two streams, and every number was labelled with who reported it and when, because that label is the only quality-control information available.

The discipline this demands was set out in chapter five, and it bears restating in compressed form, because the next several chapters will apply it constantly.

First, a run rate is not revenue. The sixty-five billion dollar figure that opened this chapter is a monthly number multiplied by twelve. For a company growing severalfold in a year, it is not comparable to any listed company's annual revenue, and adding twelve actual months would give a much smaller number. When this book quotes a run rate it will say so.

Second, a private valuation is a price, not a worth. Nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollars is what the May 2026 round implied for the whole of Anthropic if every share were worth what the newest, best-protected shares sold for — which, given the preferences attached to late-stage rounds, they are not. The same applies to OpenAI's eight hundred and fifty-two billion.

Third, an unaudited margin is a claim. The forty-four per cent and thirty-nine per cent figures above are useful, directionally credible, and entirely unverifiable. Where two private companies' figures may be defined differently, this book will compare their direction, not their level.

Fourth, disagreement between third-party estimates is information. The 2026 trade compilations of OpenAI's revenue mix, for instance, do not agree with one another — some put consumer subscriptions at around two-thirds of revenue, others showed business revenue approaching parity with consumer — and the honest reading of that disagreement is that outsiders do not know, because the company has not said. This book will report the disagreement rather than pick a side of it.

Fifth, the nearest thing to an audited window is a shareholder's accounts. When Microsoft reported, in October 2025, that its quarterly net income of twenty-seven point seven billion dollars would have been three point one billion higher but for its share of the losses of OpenAI — a disclosure covered by CNBC in October 2025 — that was, unlike anything above, a figure that had passed through a listed company's controls. Sparse as they are, those windows are precious, and CNBC reported in August 2026 that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had grown large enough to distort the earnings picture of the companies holding them — which means the windows are getting bigger. The chapters on Microsoft, Amazon and Google will use them.

None of this is a complaint. Private companies are entitled to be private, and both labs have been more forthcoming than the law requires. It is simply the epistemic ground on which this part of the book stands, stated once, plainly, so that it does not need restating every paragraph: what is known about these companies is what they, or people around them, have chosen to say — and it should be handled accordingly. Nothing in this chapter, or this book, is investment advice; it is an attempt to show what the disclosed numbers mean and where their edges are.

So: a model company sells access to a trained model through five wrappers; its gross margin is decided by the cost of answering, not the cost of learning; its largest investment is expensed the moment it is made, which makes its losses look larger and its balance sheet emptier than its economics; and its growth consumes cash in proportion to its success, which is why the capital keeps arriving in such extraordinary quantities from the layers below. That is the machine. The next chapters are about the people who built the two largest examples of it, and the decade of decisions — structural, commercial and occasionally chaotic — that produced two private companies briefly worth more than almost every public one.
