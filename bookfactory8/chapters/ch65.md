# The Case That This Is a Bubble

In the second quarter of 2026, Alphabet spent more cash on capital expenditure than its operations generated. Capital expenditure of about 44.9 billion US dollars against operating cash flow of about 39.1 billion left free cash flow at minus 5.9 billion — the first negative quarterly free cash flow since the company listed in 2004 — and on the same call management raised full-year capital expenditure guidance to between 195 and 205 billion dollars, from 180 to 190 billion, per coverage of the second-quarter results by Tom's Hardware and other outlets in July 2026.

Read that twice. The most reliably cash-generative advertising business ever built spent a quarter in deficit, and the response was to raise the spending plan. Note also that the same company's 2026 capital expenditure was being compiled at 175 to 185 billion dollars at the start of the year, per the 2026 guidance compilations discussed in chapter one. Guidance in this industry has drifted upward, not down, and it drifted upward again in the middle of a quarter that produced a cash deficit.

None of that is proof of anything. Companies run cash deficits to build assets all the time; that is what building assets means. But it is the concrete fact that the bear case starts from, and this chapter is going to make that case as well as it can be made.

A word on what this chapter is. It argues one side deliberately. It is not balanced, and it is not meant to be. The next chapter argues the opposite side with the same commitment, and then declines to adjudicate between them, because adjudication is the reader's job and the book has no view on any security. Nothing here is investment advice, no share price is predicted, and nothing improper is alleged against any named company. What follows is the strongest version of an argument, assembled from what the accounts and the public record actually say.

## What the serious bear case is not

Most of what circulates under the heading "AI bubble" is not worth engaging with, and dismissing it is not the same as answering the real argument. Three positions, in particular, are not the bear case.

It is not the claim that the technology does not work. The serious version concedes that the models are genuinely capable, improving, and used daily by hundreds of millions of people. A bubble does not require a useless product. Railways worked. Fibre worked. The internet worked.

It is not the claim that there is no revenue. There is a great deal of revenue, growing at rates that have no clean precedent in enterprise software, and chapter sixty-six sets it out. The bear case concedes that too.

And it is not the claim that anyone is committing fraud. Nothing in the disclosures supports such a claim, this book does not make it, and the argument does not need it. Every structure described below is legal, disclosed to the extent the rules require, and defended in public by serious people.

The serious bear case is narrower and entirely financial. It says: the capital being committed today is priced against revenue that does not yet exist, funded increasingly by credit and by the sellers of the equipment, depreciated on an estimate that management chooses, concentrated in a handful of buyers, and valued at multiples that require several difficult things to go right at the same time. Take any one of those and it is manageable. The claim is that they are correlated — that they would fail together, because they are all functions of the same expectation.

## Claim one: the spending is years ahead of the revenue

Start with the size of the commitment. The four largest buyers — Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta — were on course in 2026 to spend approaching seven hundred billion US dollars between them on capital expenditure, as reported by CNBC in February 2026, against a prior-year record of roughly four hundred and ten billion on the same compilations. Goldman Sachs has been cited, by Forbes in August 2026, projecting roughly 7.6 trillion dollars of cumulative capital expenditure on AI infrastructure between 2026 and 2031 across computing, data centres and power generation. That last figure is a forecast by a bank, not an outturn, and should be read as such.

Against that, the revenue. Bain and Company's sixth annual Global Technology Report, published in September 2025 and covered by Bloomberg on 23 September 2025, put the arithmetic plainly: by 2030 the industry would need roughly two trillion US dollars in combined annual revenue to fund the compute required to meet projected demand, and on Bain's own generous assumptions it would fall roughly eight hundred billion short.

That is the whole bear case in one sentence, from a consultancy that sells to the industry.

### The arithmetic of a dollar of capital expenditure

Why does capital expenditure need so much revenue behind it? Take an illustrative data centre programme, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company.

Spend one hundred billion on accelerators, servers, networking and buildings. Depreciate it straight-line over five years to nil: twenty billion a year of depreciation. Require a modest eight per cent return on the average capital invested over the life of the assets — average invested capital of fifty billion, so four billion a year. Assume power, cooling, staff and maintenance run at twenty-five per cent of revenue.

Revenue must then cover its own operating cost, the depreciation and the return. Solve it: revenue of about thirty-two billion a year, every year, for five years. One hundred and sixty billion of cumulative revenue to justify one hundred billion of spending, at a return most boards would consider unambitious.

That is roughly thirty-two cents of annual revenue for every dollar of capital expenditure. Apply the same illustrative ratio to a single year in which the industry spends seven hundred billion, and that one year's spending alone carries an annual revenue requirement in the region of two hundred and twenty billion dollars — and the following year's spending adds its own layer on top, and the year after that adds another. This is illustrative arithmetic, not a forecast, and the real ratios differ by asset mix, utilisation and financing. But it is the shape of the problem, and it is how Bain arrives at two trillion by a longer route.

The bear's observation is that the revenue side of this equation is being asserted, and the cost side is being incurred. One is a cash outflow that has already happened. The other is a plan.

### What the promoters themselves have said

The most persuasive material for the bear case comes not from short sellers but from the buyers.

In November 2025, Sam Altman said OpenAI had about twenty billion dollars of annualised revenue run rate and roughly 1.4 trillion dollars of data centre commitments over the following eight years, per TechCrunch on 6 November 2025. Set those two numbers beside each other without comment; the gap is the argument.

The commitments were then revised. In February 2026, CNBC reported that OpenAI had reset expectations with investors, targeting compute spending of around six hundred billion dollars by 2030 and revenue of about two hundred and eighty billion in 2030, against 13.1 billion dollars of revenue in the prior year. A reset of that size, arriving within months of the original figure, tells you how firm the original figure was. And in April 2026, the Wall Street Journal reported that the company had missed its own internal monthly sales targets for early 2026 and fallen short of an internal goal for weekly ChatGPT users, with an internal disagreement over whether revenue could support the commitments.

None of that is disqualifying. Fast-growing companies miss internal targets routinely, and internal targets are set to be stretching. The bear's point is about the direction of the revisions and what depends on them: the commitments were made public to justify the buildout, and when the commitments moved, they moved down while the buildout carried on.

On the demand side, the most-cited piece of evidence is MIT's NANDA initiative report, *The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025*, published in August 2025, which found that about ninety-five per cent of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable profit-and-loss impact. Treat that number carefully: it rests on fifty-two executive interviews, a survey of one hundred and fifty-three leaders and analysis of three hundred public deployments, which is a small base for a claim about the whole economy, and pilots failing is the normal condition of pilots. The bear does not need the ninety-five per cent to be exactly right. The bear needs only the weaker claim that enterprise adoption is at the pilot stage in most places while the capital is being spent as though it were at the deployment stage.

## Claim two: too much of the money moves in a circle

Chapter fifty-seven set out the mechanism; this is the bear's use of it.

In September 2025, Nvidia announced it intended to invest up to one hundred billion dollars in OpenAI, progressively, as each gigawatt of Nvidia systems was deployed, per CNBC on 22 September 2025. In November 2025, CNBC reported Nvidia's own statement that there was "no assurance" of a final agreement despite the pact. In October 2025, AMD granted OpenAI a warrant over up to one hundred and sixty million shares — around ten per cent of the company on the then share count — vesting in tranches tied to deployment and to rising share-price milestones, with the final tranche at six hundred dollars a share, per CNBC on 6 October 2025, alongside an agreement for OpenAI to deploy six gigawatts of AMD accelerators.

Move up a layer. Amazon was reported as investing fifty billion dollars in OpenAI's March 2026 round, which valued the company at eight hundred and fifty-two billion, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes. Amazon has committed up to thirty-three billion to Anthropic and Google up to forty-three billion, including ten billion in April 2026. In August 2026, CNBC reported that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun to distort the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them.

Now the financing. Meta placed its largest data centre project into a joint venture with Blue Owl Capital — the Hyperion campus in Louisiana, known as Project Beignet — in which Blue Owl funds own eighty per cent and Meta twenty, with roughly 27.3 billion dollars of debt issued through the vehicle, as reported by Bisnow in 2025. Because Meta is not the primary beneficiary of the entity, the debt does not consolidate onto Meta's balance sheet. Oracle set out plans in February 2026 to raise fifty billion dollars, per Axios on 2 February 2026. CoreWeave's total debt reached about thirty-five billion dollars as at 30 June 2026, per Capacity in 2026.

Put those together and the bear's claim is not that anyone has done anything wrong. It is that the same expectation is being counted several times.

| The money moves | Where it appears |
|---|---|
| Supplier invests in a model developer | Investment on the supplier's balance sheet; cash on the developer's |
| Developer commits to buy the supplier's systems | Purchase commitment in the developer's disclosures; backlog talk from the supplier |
| Systems are delivered and paid for | Revenue and margin in the supplier's income statement |
| Supplier's shares rise on that revenue | Unrealised gain on the supplier's other holdings; index weight rises |
| Developer raises again at a higher valuation | Fair-value gain for every earlier investor, including the supplier |

Each row is ordinary accounting. The bear's point is about the last column read downwards: the same dollar of expectation supports a revenue line, an asset value, an earnings figure and a valuation, in different companies, at the same time. That is not double-counting in any technical sense. It is correlation where a careless reader assumes independence — and in a downturn, correlation is the whole story, because the revenue, the marks and the credit all turn on the same day.

The credit market has begun to price this. Five-year credit default swaps on Oracle reached about two hundred and twelve basis points in July 2026, described by Bloomberg on 20 July 2026 as near an eighteen-year high on concern about the company's AI-related debt. Read that fairly in both directions: CNBC reported on 2 February 2026 that the same spreads fell sharply when the company set out its financing plan, so this is a market forming a view and changing it, not a verdict. But it is the first layer of the stack where an independent party prices the risk rather than describing the opportunity, and CNBC reported in July 2026 that bond market anxiety over AI capital spending was growing.

## Claim three: the depreciation assumption is doing too much work

This is the part of the bear case an accountant finds hardest to dismiss, because it turns on a number that is not a fact.

Depreciation is an estimate. Management decides how many years a server is useful for, the auditors test the reasonableness of that judgement, and the resulting charge falls to the income statement. On tens of billions of assets, a change of one year in the assumption moves reported profit by billions — without a single machine behaving differently.

The disclosed history is not consistent. Microsoft extended the depreciable life of server and network equipment from four years to six with effect from the start of its fiscal 2023, an expected favourable effect on that year's operating income of about 3.7 billion dollars, per the company's announcement in 2022. Alphabet moved to six years from January 2023, an expected reduction in that year's depreciation of about 3.4 billion, per its January 2023 announcement. Meta extended certain servers and network assets to five and a half years from four to five in January 2025, expected to reduce 2025 depreciation by about 2.9 billion, per Bloomberg's reporting at the time. In the same month, Amazon went the other way: it shortened the useful life of a subset of servers and networking equipment from six years to five, effective 1 January 2025, citing the increased pace of technology development in AI and machine learning, and disclosed a 1.4 billion dollar increase in depreciation and a one billion dollar reduction in net income for 2025, primarily affecting AWS — per the company's own disclosure of the change, summarised in 2026 filing analyses by Hudson Labs and Calcbench.

Same hardware. Same month. Opposite conclusions. They cannot both be right, and the difference between them is reported profit.

In November 2025, Michael Burry made the sharper version of the argument in public, accusing hyperscalers of understating depreciation by around one hundred and seventy-six billion dollars between 2026 and 2028 by extending useful lives against a chip cycle that turns over far faster, and estimating that profits at Oracle and Meta could be overstated by roughly twenty-seven and twenty-one per cent respectively by 2028, as reported by CNBC on 11 November 2025. Those are one investor's estimates, not disclosed figures, and he has a position and an audience. But the mechanism he describes is the standard one, and the disclosures above show the assumption moving in both directions across companies buying the same equipment.

### A sensitivity that management chooses

Return to the illustrative programme — one hundred billion of assets, an eight per cent return requirement, operating costs at twenty-five per cent of revenue — and vary only the assumed life. Round numbers, no real company.

| Assumed useful life | Annual depreciation | Annual revenue required |
|---|---|---|
| Three years | 33.3 billion | about 49.7 billion |
| Four years | 25.0 billion | about 38.7 billion |
| Five years | 20.0 billion | about 32.0 billion |
| Six years | 16.7 billion | about 27.6 billion |

Moving from a six-year assumption to a three-year one raises the revenue the programme must earn by about eighty per cent. Nothing physical changed. An estimate changed.

Two further points make this worse rather than better for the bear.

The first is timing. Depreciation on assets bought in 2026 largely lands in 2027 and later. A company can therefore report record profit in the year it spends the most, because the spending is on the cash flow statement and the balance sheet while only a fraction of it has reached the income statement. Alphabet's 195 to 205 billion of 2026 capital expenditure is a charge that builds through the second half of this decade, whatever the assumed life.

The second is residual value. Straight-line depreciation to nil assumes the machine is worth nothing at the end. Extending lives implicitly assumes an active second life. The counter-argument — that older accelerators still rent profitably years after launch — is a real one and chapter sixty-six makes it. The bear's reply is that a rental price is not a carrying value: what matters is the rate the older fleet earns, and its utilisation, against the book value still sitting on the balance sheet. Reported GPU rental rates have fallen materially from their peaks, and the resale market for accelerators at scale is thin and largely unobserved. An asset with no deep second-hand market has a residual value that is an assumption, not a price.

## Claim four: the demand is concentrated in a handful of budgets

Nvidia's most recent disclosures show how narrow the base is. Four direct customers each accounted for more than ten per cent of revenue — twenty-two, fifteen, thirteen and eleven per cent — together about sixty-one per cent of the total, in the third quarter of fiscal 2026, as reported by the Motley Fool in November 2025 from the company's quarterly filing.

Sixty-one per cent of the revenue of the world's most valuable company, from four customers, each of which is a capital budget approved annually by a board.

The bear's framing is precise here, and it matters. The claim is not that demand for AI collapses. The claim is that four to six capital allocation decisions, taken in ordinary board meetings for ordinary reasons — a weak quarter in advertising, a change of chief executive, a credit market that closes, a regulator, a decision to pause and digest — determine the revenue of the entire chain beneath them. Reduce combined hyperscaler capital expenditure by a quarter and the effect does not fall gently across a diversified customer base. It falls on the accelerator vendor, then the foundry, then the memory makers, then the equipment suppliers, each of which has already committed capacity years ahead. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion dollars, above analyst expectations, in its January 2026 results. High-bandwidth memory capacity has been reported sold out across major suppliers through 2026, and SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, per CNBC in August 2026. Capacity decided now arrives in 2028 and 2029, regardless of what demand does in between. That is the semiconductor cycle of chapter sixty-three, and this time the orders sit above an unusually small number of buyers.

The concentration extends to the ownership of the shares. The Magnificent Seven accounted for roughly thirty-four per cent of the S&P 500 as at June 2026, per Forbes' investor hub in June 2026 — meaning that anyone who owns the index owns this concentration, whether they formed a view on it or not.

Institutions have said so in their own words. The IMF and the Bank of England both warned in October 2025 of the risk of a sharp market correction if sentiment towards AI turned, as reported by CNBC on 9 October 2025. In its Financial Stability Report of July 2026, the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee said valuations had become more stretched and set out a scenario in which a sharp correction in AI-related equities spilled into the United Kingdom, with an effect on UK GDP of as much as 2.2 percentage points. Central banks are not short sellers, and a scenario in a stability report is a stress test rather than a forecast. But it is the same argument in institutional language.

## Claim five: the prices assume everything goes right at once

This section contains no recommendation, no target and no prediction. It does arithmetic on figures that are disclosed and dated, and shows what those figures imply if you believe them.

| Company | Value, dated and sourced | Earnings or revenue basis | Arithmetic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nvidia | roughly 5.3–5.4 trillion US dollars, mid-August 2026 (companiesmarketcap / Motley Fool rankings) | GAAP net income of 120.1 billion for the fiscal year ended 25 January 2026 (results announcement, February 2026; Form 10-K) | about forty-four to forty-five times trailing earnings |
| Anthropic | 965 billion post-money, May 2026 (CNBC, May 2026) | annualised run rate above 65 billion in July 2026 (CNBC, August 2026) | about fifteen times an annualised run rate |
| OpenAI | 852 billion, March 2026 (valueaddvc / Forbes, 2026) | annualised run rate around 40 billion as at August 2026 (2026 trade coverage) | about twenty-one times an annualised run rate |

Every caveat in this book applies to that table at once. The market capitalisations move daily. The private valuations are the price struck in a single negotiated transaction, not an audited value, and a preferred share with liquidation rights is not the same instrument as a common share. A run rate is one month multiplied by twelve; it is not revenue for a year, it is not audited, and it is not comparable with the trailing figures of a listed company. The multiples in the last column are arithmetic performed here on the two sourced figures beside them, shown so that the reader can redo it, not quoted from anyone.

With all that said, the bear's point is not that these numbers are absurd. Forty-four times trailing earnings is a demanding multiple, not a ridiculous one, for a company that grew revenue sixty-five per cent in its last fiscal year. The point is what has to hold simultaneously for it to be justified: revenue growth continues at something like the current rate; gross margin holds while the price of a unit of intelligence keeps falling; customers keep funding capital expenditure at present levels; the depreciation assumption is not reset downwards; no competitor commoditises the accelerator or the model; supply is not interrupted; and the credit that funds the marginal buyer stays available and cheap.

Assign each of those a high individual probability — say, ninety per cent — and multiply seven of them together. You get less than half. That calculation is illustrative and the events are not independent, which cuts both ways: correlated good outcomes are likelier than the naive product suggests, and so are correlated bad ones. But it is the reason the bear talks about flawless execution. A price that is reasonable on the central case can still be expensive once you price the joint probability of the whole chain performing.

One structural point belongs here, because it is the most uncomfortable. The price of a unit of the product is falling fast. Whether the decline is roughly threefold or roughly tenfold a year depends entirely on which models you compare and on which date, and this book will not assert a figure it cannot pin to a source. The direction is not in dispute. A business whose unit price falls that quickly must grow volume faster still merely to stand still — while the cost of the asset that produces the units does not fall on the same schedule, because it was bought with cash, at the top of a shortage, and is being depreciated over five or six years.

## The macro version of the argument

The last move in the serious bear case is to widen the frame, and it is the most quietly alarming part.

Harvard economist Jason Furman estimated that investment in data centres and information-processing software accounted for about ninety-two per cent of US GDP growth in the first half of 2025, and that without it annualised growth would have been about 0.1 per cent, as reported by Fortune on 7 October 2025.

If that estimate is even approximately right, the spending is not merely responding to demand. For a time, in the aggregate, the spending is the demand. Data centre construction employs people who spend money. Equipment orders are recorded as output. Power contracts are signed. Some meaningful part of the economic strength that makes the buildout look affordable is itself produced by the buildout.

The bear's conclusion follows without any need for drama. A slowdown in capital expenditure would not be a discrete event affecting a few technology companies. It would remove a large share of the growth that supports the earnings that support the valuations that support the confidence that funds the capital expenditure. That is a loop, and loops run in both directions.

## What the bear case concedes

An argument that concedes nothing persuades nobody, and the most serious proponents of this one concede a great deal.

They concede the revenue is real, growing quickly and being paid for by customers in cash. They concede that the principal buyers are among the most profitable enterprises in history, funding this largely from operating cash flow rather than from the debt-laden balance sheets of 2000 — a distinction that chapters fifty-eight and sixty-six treat properly. They concede that much of the capacity is contracted years ahead under take-or-pay terms, which is a materially better position than building speculatively. They concede that capability is still improving, that the enterprise adoption curve is early, and that the buildings and the power interconnections retain value even when the accelerators inside them do not.

And they concede the thing that matters most in practice: that a bubble can inflate for years, that being early is indistinguishable from being wrong, and that the identical arguments were made in 1996 about the internet by people who were right about the eventual outcome and wrong about everything in between. The bear case is an argument about the relationship between price and assumptions. It is not a timetable, and anyone offering one should be treated with suspicion.

## The honest summary

Strip the rhetoric out and the bear case reduces to five statements, each of which can be checked against a disclosure.

Capital expenditure is running years ahead of the revenue that must eventually service it, on an industry-wide arithmetic that Bain put at a two trillion dollar annual revenue requirement by 2030 with an eight hundred billion shortfall. A meaningful share of the money moves in a circle, from supplier to customer and back as revenue, with the same expectation supporting revenue, asset marks and valuations at once. Reported profit depends on a depreciation assumption that management chooses, that the largest buyers have recently moved in opposite directions, and that has never been tested through a full hardware transition. The demand is concentrated in a handful of annual capital budgets — four customers, sixty-one per cent of Nvidia's revenue in the third quarter of fiscal 2026. And the prices being paid, in public and private markets, require most of that to go right at the same time.

Every figure above is a snapshot with a date attached to it, and every one of them will have moved by the time you read this. The market capitalisations move daily; look them up. The customer concentration is restated every quarter in the filings. The useful-life assumption is disclosed in the property, plant and equipment note and in the summary of significant accounting policies, and a change to it must be disclosed with its effect on income — that note is the single most informative paragraph in any of these accounts, and almost nobody reads it. The capital expenditure guidance is updated on every earnings call, and has moved upward more often than down.

This chapter has argued one side as hard as the evidence allows. It has not proved it, and it has been careful not to pretend otherwise: nothing here alleges misconduct, recommends any action, or predicts any price. Chapter sixty-six now takes the other side with the same seriousness, and then hands you the specific observable facts that would move a careful reader toward one conclusion or the other. The verdict is not the book's to give. The arithmetic is.
