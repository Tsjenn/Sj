# Anthropic III: Nearly a Trillion, and the IPO Question

In May 2021, Anthropic raised one hundred and twenty-four million US dollars in a Series A led by Dustin Moskovitz and Jaan Tallinn, as reported at the time by Crunchbase News. In May 2026, five years later almost to the month, it closed a sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation, and was, briefly, the most valuable private company in the world, as reported by CNBC in May 2026. That is a rise of roughly seven thousand seven hundred times in the headline number, in five years, without a single audited account being published along the way.

Both halves of that sentence deserve equal weight. The rise is extraordinary. So is the absence of accounts. This chapter takes the valuation ladder rung by rung, then asks the three questions the brief demands of any number this large: what revenue must the price be assuming, what do the terms attached to late-stage money do to the headline, and what would change on the day the company files to go public — a day which, by the time the Series H closed, was visibly approaching.

The usual caution applies with more than usual force. Every figure in this chapter is dated and attributed, most of them describe a private company, and none of them is audited. They are snapshots that had already begun to move as they were reported. Nothing here is investment advice, and this book gives none.

## The ladder

Anthropic's capital history compresses the entire venture ladder — seed to pre-IPO — into half a decade, at a slope that has no obvious precedent at this scale. The rounds that were publicly disclosed and reported look like this.

| Date | Round | Raised | Post-money valuation | As reported by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| May 2021 | Series A | $124 million | not widely reported | Crunchbase News, 2021 |
| April 2022 | Series B | $580 million | ~$4 billion | Private Equity Wire and Crunchbase News, 2022 |
| May 2023 | Series C | $450 million | ~$4.1 billion | TechCrunch-era coverage, 2023 |
| March 2025 | Series E | $3.5 billion | $61.5 billion | company announcement; Bloomberg and CNBC, March 2025 |
| September 2025 | Series F | $13 billion | $183 billion | company announcement; CNBC, September 2025 |
| February 2026 | Series G | $30 billion | $380 billion | company announcement, February 2026 |
| May 2026 | Series H | $65 billion | $965 billion | CNBC, May 2026 |

Three features of the ladder are worth pausing on.

The first is the early history, because it carries a warning about who shows up first with money. The Series B of April 2022 was led by Sam Bankman-Fried of FTX, with the bulk of the five hundred and eighty million reported as coming from Alameda Research, per 2022 coverage from Private Equity Wire and Crunchbase News. FTX collapsed in November 2022, and Bankman-Fried was later convicted of fraud and sentenced to twenty-five years, as widely reported in 2024. Anthropic itself was accused of nothing; its misfortune was purely the identity of an early shareholder, whose stake became an asset of a bankruptcy estate to be sold for the benefit of creditors. The lesson generalises: a capitalisation table is a list of people who may not always be the shareholders you would choose, and a company does not control what happens to its own shares once sold.

The second feature is the acceleration. The company took roughly four years, from 2021 to March 2025, to reach a sixty-one and a half billion dollar valuation, per the March 2025 round announcement. It then took fourteen months to add roughly nine hundred billion more: one hundred and eighty-three billion in September 2025, three hundred and eighty billion in February 2026, nine hundred and sixty-five billion in May 2026, per the round reporting cited in the table. Each step roughly doubled or tripled the last, at intervals of five to seven months. Whatever else that pattern reflects, it reflects investors repricing the same company faster than any audited reporting cycle could have informed them. Between September 2025 and May 2026 there was no annual report to read, because there are no published annual reports. The repricing ran on figures shared privately with investors and on reported revenue momentum — which is the subject of the next section but one.

The third feature is what the ladder leaves out. The gap between the Series C of May 2023 and the Series E of March 2025 was not empty: it contained the two commitments that earlier chapters of this part treat at length — Amazon's, ultimately reported at up to thirty-three billion dollars, and Google's, at up to forty-three billion including ten billion in April 2026, per 2026 reporting. Strategic money of that kind does not always arrive as a tidy lettered round with a press release, and public reporting of the intermediate steps is patchier than the table's neatness suggests. The Series G, similarly, was reported by Bloomberg and the Wall Street Journal in January 2026 as a roughly ten billion dollar raise at a three hundred and fifty billion dollar valuation before it closed the following month at thirty billion raised and three hundred and eighty billion post-money, per the company's February 2026 announcement — a round that grew by a factor of three between first report and close. Reported round sizes are moving targets while the round is open. Alongside the Series G primary raise, an employee tender offer in the range of five to six billion dollars was reported in early 2026, allowing current and former staff to sell shares at around the three hundred and fifty billion dollar mark — a detail that matters later in this chapter, because tender offers are the private company's substitute for a listing, and their size and frequency tell you something about how long a company expects to stay private.

## What a post-money number is

Before asking what nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollars implies, be precise about what it is, because the arithmetic is simpler and stranger than the headlines suggest.

A post-money valuation is the price per share paid by the newest investors, multiplied by the fully diluted share count. That is all. In the Series H, investors bought newly issued preferred shares for sixty-five billion dollars; the price they paid, spread across every share and option and warrant in existence, annualises the whole company at nine hundred and sixty-five billion. Nobody valued the whole company at that price in the sense of offering to buy all of it. A minority of new shares changed hands — sixty-five billion of primary capital against a nine-hundred-and-sixty-five billion total implies the new money bought roughly six or seven per cent of the company, on simple division of the two reported figures — and the price of that slice was extrapolated across the rest.

An accountant should immediately notice two things about that construction.

First, it is a marginal price treated as a total value. Public market capitalisations have the same weakness — the last trade prices every share — but a public share trades continuously, in both directions, among thousands of participants who can sell as easily as buy. A private round is a single negotiated trade, one way, among a handful of participants, none of whom can exit until the company lets them. The information content of the price is thinner.

Second, and more important, the shares bought at the margin are not the same instrument as the shares the headline number prices. Series H investors bought preferred stock with contractual rights attached. The employee who holds common stock, and whose personal net worth the headline implies, holds something junior to every one of those rights. How much junior is the subject of the section after next. First, the revenue question.

## The revenue the price assumes

A valuation is a compressed forecast. Uncompress it, and the Series H price becomes a statement about how much revenue, at what margin, for how long, with what certainty. The reported revenue trajectory is the place to start.

At the beginning of 2025, Anthropic's run-rate revenue was reported at roughly one billion dollars, a figure the company itself cited in its September 2025 round announcement. By August 2025 the reported run rate had passed five billion, per the same announcement. In May 2026 it was reported at forty-seven billion, and by the end of July 2026 the company told investors the annualised run rate had reached sixty-five billion dollars — roughly a sevenfold increase on a year earlier — with a preliminary, unaudited second-quarter revenue figure of eleven and a half billion dollars, all as reported by CNBC in August 2026. The same report put revenue for the whole of 2025 at roughly ten billion dollars.

Every caveat this book has attached to the phrase "annualised run rate" applies here at full strength. It is a monthly figure multiplied by twelve. It is unaudited, it is not a trailing-twelve-month number, it reached the press because the company shared it with investors, and for a company growing this quickly the run rate and the actual year's revenue are very different quantities — the reported 2025 outturn of roughly ten billion sat far below the run rates being quoted during 2025, precisely because the run rate describes the latest month, not the year. With that said, the direction and rough magnitude were corroborated across multiple outlets, and they are the only revenue picture available. Use them with their labels on.

Now do the division the price invites. Nine hundred and sixty-five billion of valuation against a sixty-five billion run rate — taking the May 2026 price and the July 2026 revenue figure, both as reported above — is just under fifteen times annualised revenue. Against the roughly ten billion of reported 2025 revenue, it is closer to ninety-seven times the last full year. Both numbers are simple arithmetic on the reported figures, and the gulf between them is the point: the multiple depends entirely on which revenue you believe is the "real" one, and for a company whose revenue was reported as multiplying sevenfold in a year, that choice swings the answer by a factor of six.

What does fifteen times a run rate assume? No formula converts a multiple into a forecast without assumptions, so treat what follows as orientation, not calculation. Mature listed software businesses have traded across a wide range of revenue multiples depending on growth and margin, and this book will not pretend there is a "correct" one. But the direction of the logic is fixed: a buyer at fifteen times revenue is not paying for present profits — Anthropic's own reported economics, per the PitchBook and Morningstar figures cited in chapter one, had compute alone consuming about seventy-one cents of every revenue dollar in the first quarter of 2026 — so the price only resolves into sense if revenue keeps compounding steeply and the cost of serving it falls as a share of sales. The Series H investor is underwriting both legs: continued hypergrowth and a structural margin improvement that the reported quarterly figures had only begun to show. If either leg stalls, the multiple does not gently compress; it re-prices.

For comparison, and from the same period: TSMC, which manufactures at the layer where all of this physically happens, stood at roughly two point one seven trillion dollars of market capitalisation as at August 2026, per companiesmarketcap. The market was pricing one privately held model company, with unaudited accounts and reported 2025 revenue of roughly ten billion dollars, at a touch under forty-five per cent of the world's dominant contract chipmaker. Whether that ratio is visionary or extravagant is exactly the question a public listing would put to a much larger jury.

One more comparison is worth the arithmetic, because it involves the company's nearest rival. OpenAI closed a one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round in March 2026 at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes, and its annualised run rate was reported at around forty billion dollars as at August 2026 in trade coverage. On those reported figures — all unaudited, all moving — the market's last private marks put Anthropic just under fifteen times its reported run rate and OpenAI at roughly twenty-one times its own. Read no more into that than it can bear: two unaudited numerators, two unaudited denominators, measured months apart. But it does say that Anthropic's higher headline valuation was not simply exuberance layered on a smaller business; on the reported figures, the higher-priced company was, per dollar of claimed run rate, the cheaper of the two.

## The preference stack, or what the headline is not

The nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar figure prices every share as if it were a Series H preferred share. It is not, and the difference is the most under-reported feature of every late-stage valuation story.

Late-stage preferred stock typically carries some combination of the following rights, negotiated round by round. A liquidation preference: the right to be repaid the invested amount — sometimes a multiple of it — before junior shareholders receive anything in a sale or winding up. Seniority: later rounds often rank ahead of earlier rounds, so the stack pays out top-down, newest money first. Anti-dilution and ratchet provisions: if the company later raises, or lists, at a lower price, the earlier investor receives extra shares to compensate. Participation rights, where they exist, let the preferred holder take its preference and then share in the remainder as well. Which of these attach to Anthropic's Series H specifically has not been publicly disclosed, and this book will not guess. What can be said with confidence is generic: sixty-five billion dollars of late-stage capital does not arrive without contractual protection, and every protection it has is a claim that ranks ahead of the common stock.

To see what that does to a headline number, take an illustrative company, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It has one billion fully diluted shares. A late investor pays one hundred dollars a share for one hundred million new shares: ten billion dollars in, at a post-money "valuation" of one hundred billion (one hundred dollars times one billion shares, plus the new issue — keep it round). The investor's shares carry a one-times liquidation preference, senior to everything.

Now run three futures through that structure.

| Outcome for the illustrative company | Late investor (paid $10bn) | Common shareholders |
|---|---|---|
| Sold for $150bn | Converts to common: ~$13.6bn — a gain | ~$136bn shared: the headline was, if anything, low |
| Sold for $50bn — half the "valuation" | Takes its $10bn preference first — whole, no loss | $40bn shared: worth ~60% less per share than the headline implied |
| Sold for $10bn — a 90% collapse | Takes the full $10bn — still whole | Nothing |

The late investor loses money only below the point where the company's entire sale price fails to cover the preference stack. In every other outcome it is either whole or profiting. The common shareholder — the employee, the founder, the early backer who converted — absorbs the downside first and in full. Now reverse the inference, because this is the part that matters for reading headlines: an investor with that protection can rationally pay a higher share price than an unprotected buyer of common stock would, precisely because the structure caps their downside. The headline valuation, which mechanically multiplies that protected price across all shares including the unprotected ones, is therefore biased upward as a description of what the whole company would fetch. How much upward depends on terms nobody outside the deal has seen. That is not an allegation about any real company's terms; it is arithmetic about the instrument class, and it is why this book keeps writing "post-money valuation" where the coverage writes "worth".

There is one honest counterweight. Anthropic ran a reported five-to-six billion dollar employee tender at around a three hundred and fifty billion dollar valuation in early 2026, per reporting at the time — and a tender prices common stock, not new preferred. Secondary sales of common shares are the cleanest private evidence of what unprotected equity actually trades for. The tender's reported price sat at the Series G level, months before the Series H headline nearly tripled it. Whether common would have cleared at the higher mark was, at that point, untested.

## Briefly the most valuable private company

The Series H made Anthropic, briefly, the most valuable private company in the world — briefly, because the title had belonged to OpenAI at its March 2026 mark of eight hundred and fifty-two billion, and in this market such rankings hold only until the rival's next round, per CNBC's May 2026 reporting of the milestone.

The milestone is worth a paragraph of scepticism precisely because it was so widely repeated. A ranking of private companies by valuation is a ranking of last negotiated prices, struck at different dates, on different terms, with different preference stacks, none of them continuously tested. Two public companies ranked by market capitalisation are being measured by the same yardstick at the same instant. Two private companies ranked by their last rounds are being measured by different yardsticks at different times. "Most valuable private company" is a genuinely interesting fact about investor appetite; it is a weak fact about relative value.

The scale, though, had consequences that reached well beyond the two companies themselves. By mid-2026, stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic were reported as large enough to distort the earnings picture of the public companies holding them, per CNBC in August 2026. The mechanism, treated properly in this book's accounting chapters, is that a holder that marks its stake through the income statement records gains when its investee's paper valuation rises — profit from a private price, struck in a round the holder may itself have participated in, flowing into audited public earnings. Amazon's reported commitment of up to thirty-three billion dollars and Google's of up to forty-three billion, per 2026 reporting, sit on exactly this fault line: each new Anthropic round revalues stakes held by the very companies that also supply its compute and participate in its financing. Nothing about that arrangement is improper, and none of it is hidden — but a reader of Amazon's or Alphabet's accounts now needs an opinion about a private company's valuation to interpret a public company's earnings, which is a novel and uncomfortable dependency. Chapter one called the four layers a filing system; this is a place where the filing system's drawers have started leaking into one another.

## The IPO question

By the time the Series H closed, the public listing was no longer hypothetical. The Financial Times had reported that Anthropic was planning an IPO as early as 2026. The company was reported by Fortune to have filed a confidential registration statement with the US securities regulator on the first of June 2026. Bloomberg reported in June 2026 that Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs had been picked to lead the offering, with JPMorgan also involved, and in August 2026 that Citigroup was being added to the top line of banks. CNBC reported in July 2026 that bankers were scheduling meetings with prospective investors, with a debut considered possible as early as October 2026. All of that reporting can age badly — mandates shift, windows close, filings sit — but the direction was unambiguous: the company was building the machinery of a listing.

So end where the accountant would: not with whether the IPO happens, or at what price — this book predicts neither — but with what a listing forces into daylight that nine rounds of private capital never did.

**Audited accounts, with comparatives.** A registration statement requires audited financial statements for multiple years. For the first time, "revenue" would mean revenue under a stated recognition policy, tested by an external auditor — not a run rate shared with investors. The relationship between the run rates reported through 2025 and 2026 and the audited outturns for those periods would become checkable, retrospectively, by anyone. Every company that has fed the press flattering unaudited numbers faces this moment of reconciliation; it is one of a prospectus's quiet public services.

**Cost of revenue, stated.** The reported compute economics — the roughly seventy-one cents per revenue dollar of early 2026, per the PitchBook and Morningstar figures — would be replaced by an audited cost of revenue and a gross margin the company must stand behind, restated every quarter thereafter. The single most contested number in the model layer's economics would stop being a leak and start being a line item.

**Related parties, disclosed.** This is the disclosure with the most at stake. Anthropic's two largest strategic investors are also, by the up-to-thirty-three and up-to-forty-three billion dollar commitments reported in 2026, among its most important suppliers of compute. Securities disclosure regimes require related-party transactions to be described: the nature of the relationship, the amounts involved. How much of the revenue and how much of the cost base runs through shareholders would move from inference to disclosure. Chapter one promised that circular structures would be explained rather than alleged; a prospectus is where such structures must explain themselves.

**Customer concentration.** Public companies disclose customers above ten per cent of revenue. An enterprise-weighted model business would have to show whether its reported growth is broad or resting on a small number of very large contracts — a distinction invisible in a run rate and decisive for its durability.

**The preference stack, resolved.** A listing typically converts preferred stock to common, extinguishing the preferences — sometimes on negotiated terms, occasionally with ratchet adjustments if the offer prices below a protected round. The gap this chapter has laboured, between the price of protected shares and the value of common, closes on listing day: one class, one price, marked continuously by a market that includes sellers. Whatever the Series H terms were, the IPO price would be the first number in the company's history to price every share alike.

**A market that can say no, daily.** The private ladder repriced the company upward seven times in five years, each time by mutual agreement between a willing company and willing buyers. A listed share is repriced every day by people with no stake in agreeing. That is the real meaning of the IPO question — not whether the nine hundred and sixty-five billion was "right", but that until a listing, no mechanism existed by which anyone could be proved wrong.

The figures in this chapter were current when written in August 2026 and will not be current when read. The valuations are last-round prices; the revenue figures are unaudited run rates; all of it is sourced to the reporting cited, not to filings. If the listing has happened by the time you read this, the prospectus and the subsequent filings supersede every number here — which is, in the end, this chapter's argument.
