# Vendor Financing, Old and New

In April 2001, two days after missing a seventy-five million dollar payment to Lucent Technologies, Winstar Communications filed for bankruptcy protection and sued Lucent for ten billion dollars, per the Deseret News and RCR Wireless News reporting the filing that month. Lucent had agreed to provide Winstar up to two billion dollars of equipment financing, with the amount it was obliged to have outstanding at any one time capped at five hundred million, per the credit agreement described in the companies' own filings of the period. So the supplier had sold the equipment, lent the customer the money to buy it, recognised the sale, and was then sued by its borrower for not lending more.

That single transaction contains everything this chapter is about. It is also, according to a large and confident body of commentary, exactly what is happening now.

The comparison deserves better than it usually gets. It is normally deployed as a rhetorical device — a knowing reference to Lucent, a raised eyebrow, and no numbers. It is worth doing properly, because the mechanism really did destroy an industry, the mechanism really has reappeared in modified form, and the modifications are substantial enough that a serious reader has to weigh them rather than wave at them. This chapter sets out what vendor financing did to telecommunications equipment between roughly 1996 and 2002, what the equivalent structures look like in 2026, and then puts the case for difference and the case against difference side by side, at equal length and with equal seriousness. It reaches no verdict. It is not investment advice, and it alleges nothing against any company named in it.

## The mechanism, stated plainly

Vendor financing is the practice of a seller providing, arranging or guaranteeing the money with which its customer buys the seller's product.

In accounting terms the effect is easy to state and easy to miss. Take an illustrative equipment maker, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It ships one hundred million dollars of equipment to a customer and simultaneously lends that customer one hundred million dollars on five-year terms. Cash out equals cash in; the group's bank balance has not moved. But the income statement records one hundred million of revenue and, on a forty per cent gross margin, forty million of gross profit. The balance sheet records a loan receivable of one hundred million, which is an asset. Nothing in the primary statements says the revenue and the receivable are the same transaction seen twice.

Now let the customer fail. The receivable is written down, and the write-down flows back through the income statement — as a bad debt charge, not as a reversal of revenue, and usually in a different period and a different line from the sale it relates to. The profit was reported in year one. The loss arrives in year three, in a different place, where it is harder to connect to the sale that produced it.

This is not, in itself, improper, and it is very old. Captive finance arms are ordinary industrial practice: aircraft manufacturers, agricultural equipment makers, motor manufacturers and industrial conglomerates have all financed their customers for decades, generally with good results and full disclosure. There are three respectable reasons to do it.

The first is credit knowledge. A manufacturer often understands the collateral better than a bank does, because it built the collateral and knows the resale market. Lending against a machine you can repossess, refurbish and resell is a business with a genuine informational advantage.

The second is market development. Where a product is new, the addressable market is limited by the customer's ability to finance it rather than by the customer's appetite. Removing a financing obstacle converts latent demand into orders, which is a legitimate thing to do.

The third is timing. A creditworthy buyer may want the equipment before its own funding closes. Bridging that gap is a service, priced accordingly.

The line between those and something worse is not about the practice. It is about the borrower. Financing a solvent customer's timing is banking. Financing a customer who has no other source of capital, on terms no independent lender would offer, in order to book a sale, is something else — and the distinction only becomes visible when capital markets close, because that is when the two look different for the first time.

## The old case: how the telecommunications industry did it

The late 1990s produced an unusually pure version. Deregulation in the United States created a class of competitive local exchange carriers — new entrants building networks to compete with incumbents. They had licences, business plans and, in many cases, almost no revenue. They were funded by the capital markets, which in that period would fund almost anything with a network in it.

Their suppliers were the great equipment makers of the era: Lucent, spun out of AT&T in 1996; Nortel Networks of Canada; Cisco; Alcatel; Motorola; Ericsson. Each of them faced the same commercial fact. The customers wanted the equipment and could not yet pay for it. Whoever solved that would take the order.

The scale of what followed is visible in contemporaneous trade reporting of the vendors' own disclosures. TheStreet, covering the practice as it grew, and later compilations of the same disclosures, put Lucent's committed vendor financing at roughly eight point one billion dollars, of which about two point one billion had been drawn; Nortel's at about three point one billion committed with roughly one point four billion outstanding; and Cisco's customer-loan commitments at about two point four billion. Individual agreements were correspondingly large: Lucent entered a credit agreement in October 1996 to provide Sprint Spectrum with one point eight billion dollars of long-term financing for its personal communications services network, and the Winstar facility described above ran to two billion.

Three features of that arrangement matter, because each of them recurs.

The customer's business plan depended on continued access to outside capital, not on its own cash flow. The vendor's revenue therefore depended on the capital markets remaining open to a set of companies the vendor did not control.

The vendor's exposure was to a small number of counterparties, in a single industry, all of whose fortunes moved together. There was no diversification anywhere in the structure. When one CLEC was in trouble, they all were, for the same reason at the same time.

And the financing was competitive. Once one vendor offered terms, the others had to match or lose the order. Nobody chose the aggregate exposure the industry ended up with; each participant chose one deal at a time. This is worth holding on to, because it is the most under-appreciated feature of the episode. There was no single decision to build an eight billion dollar loan book to uncreditworthy start-ups. There was a series of individually defensible decisions, each taken against a competitor who was about to take it instead.

## 2001: what the unwind actually looked like

The sequence, when it came, ran in a specific order, and knowing the order is more useful than knowing the outcome.

First the capital markets closed to the carriers. Second, the carriers could neither draw further nor service what they had drawn. Third, the vendors' receivables impaired, and the impairments ran through the income statement. Fourth — and this is the step that turns a bad year into a structural crisis — the vendors, now capital-constrained themselves, could no longer extend financing, so the marginal order stopped being placed at all. Demand did not merely fall by the amount of the failures. It fell by the amount of financing that was no longer available to support it.

The numbers from that period are still striking a quarter of a century later. Nortel reported a loss of nineteen point four billion US dollars for the second quarter of 2001, which CBC News described at the time as the largest in Canadian history; for the full year the company originally reported a net loss of about twenty-seven point one six billion dollars, later restated to twenty-five point seven two billion, per Computerworld's coverage of the restatement. Cisco recorded an excess inventory charge of two point two five billion dollars in the quarter ended April 2001, reported at the time by The Washington Post and examined ever since as a case study in forecasting through a distribution chain that had been telling it what it wanted to hear.

The quality of the loan books deteriorated at a speed that no model had contemplated. Later analyses of Nortel's 2001 disclosures record impaired customer loans rising from roughly a quarter of the portfolio at the end of 2000 to roughly four-fifths a year later. The Globe and Mail reported in the same period that the company had found it markedly harder to sell down or syndicate its customer financing to third-party lenders as the downturn set in — which is the detail an experienced credit officer would notice first. The exit from these positions was assumed to exist because it had existed. It closed at precisely the moment it was needed.

The litigation ran for years afterwards. Lucent was ordered to pay two hundred and forty-four million dollars to the Winstar estate, a judgment the company estimated would produce a charge of about three hundred million, per Light Reading and CFO.com reporting the ruling. Motorola's exposure to Telsim in Turkey, where it had advanced roughly two billion dollars, ended in a judgment reported by The Washington Post on 1 August 2003 at about four point three billion dollars — comprising some two point one billion of principal and a similar sum in damages. A judgment, as any practitioner knows, is not a recovery.

Separately, and this distinction matters, the United States Securities and Exchange Commission brought an accounting case against Lucent. In May 2004 the company paid a twenty-five million dollar civil penalty in settlement, without admitting or denying wrongdoing and without being required to restate, while nine current and former executives were charged; the regulator's allegation was that roughly one point one billion dollars of revenue had been improperly recognised in fiscal 2000, and that incentives given to customers to induce purchases had not been disclosed. The investigation had begun in late 2000 when Lucent itself disclosed that six hundred and seventy-nine million dollars of revenue had been booked prematurely. This is reported by Computerworld, NBC News, InfoWorld and the Deseret News in May 2004.

Read that paragraph carefully, because it is routinely misquoted. The regulatory case concerned revenue recognition and undisclosed customer incentives. It was not a case about vendor financing as such. Vendor financing was lawful, disclosed in the filings, and practised openly by every major participant. The lesson of the telecommunications episode is not that somebody broke a rule. It is that a perfectly legal structure, disclosed in the notes and understood by everyone who read them, converted a demand shock into a credit shock and parked it on the supplier's balance sheet, where it did far more damage than a demand shock alone would have done.

## The new case: what the 2020s structures actually are

Now set the modern arrangements alongside that, without adjectives.

The core of today's lending is described in chapter twenty-eight and need only be summarised. Accelerator fleets are financed with secured debt: CoreWeave's first facility of two point three billion dollars in August 2023 was led by Magnetar and Blackstone and secured on its Nvidia fleet; a seven point five billion dollar facility followed in May 2024, secured on the fleet and the contracts it served; by March 2026 an eight and a half billion dollar bank-led facility was reported at two and a quarter points over SOFR with an A3 rating from Moody's. Trade analyses of the market in 2026 describe standard terms of fifty to seventy per cent advance rates against appraised value and two-to-four-year tenors matched to an assumed useful life. Lenders are underwriting a contracted revenue stream, with the hardware as backstop.

Layered on top of that are structures that come from the seller's side, and these are the ones that invite the comparison.

There is a residual purchase obligation. A securities filing disclosed, and CNBC reported on 15 September 2025, that Nvidia is obliged to purchase CoreWeave's unsold residual cloud capacity to a value of six point three billion dollars through April 2032, under an agreement originally struck in 2023.

There is equity in the customer base. Chapter fifty-six sets out the record: compilations of Nvidia's Form 13F filed on 14 August 2026 put the disclosed portfolio at roughly sixty-three billion US dollars, per the Motley Fool and 24/7 Wall St. Cloud operators, model developers, suppliers and adjacent businesses all appear in it.

There is an instrument with no real 1990s equivalent, running the other way. On 6 October 2025 AMD and OpenAI announced an agreement to deploy six gigawatts of AMD accelerators, and AMD issued OpenAI a warrant over up to one hundred and sixty million AMD shares at an exercise price of one cent, vesting in tranches against deployment milestones and specified share-price conditions, exercisable through 5 October 2030, per the companies' announcements; CNBC reported AMD shares rising twenty-three per cent on the news. The vendor is not lending the customer money. It is paying the customer, in its own equity, for buying — and the payment is worth more the more the market approves of the purchase.

There is a guarantee question that was live as this was written. Bloomberg reported on 27 July 2026 that Nvidia's roughly seven hundred and fifty billion dollars of announced deals had revived concerns about circular financing, and Axios reported the same day that the company was weighing a financing guarantee in connection with OpenAI.

And then there is the structure that most directly addresses the comparison. On 10 and 11 August 2026, Bloomberg, CNBC, Fortune and CNN reported that Nvidia had assembled a coalition of asset managers and banks — Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR — to mobilise more than five hundred billion dollars of third-party capital for AI infrastructure, creating dedicated pools of capital for Nvidia's customers to fund data centres and hardware without using their own balance sheets. Jensen Huang described AI compute as becoming an investable asset class and said he had approached only those six firms, none of which declined. Bank of America analysts were quoted describing the initiative as a pivot away from the vendor financing that had drawn criticism; CNBC's coverage on 11 August 2026 reported that Wall Street was not uniformly persuaded it would settle the question.

The market-wide context is a debt market that has grown quickly. Forbes reported in July 2026, citing Morgan Stanley, that global AI-related debt issuance was on track for nearly five hundred and seventy billion dollars in 2026, more than double the 2025 figure. The Bank for International Settlements published analysis in its March 2026 Quarterly Review specifically on the on- and off-balance-sheet borrowing behind the build-out — the existence of that paper being itself a signal of where official attention had turned.

## The assumption underneath both eras

Every one of these structures, old and new, rests on the same estimate: what the financed asset is worth after the borrower stops paying for it.

In telecommunications the estimate was made about switches, routers and optical fibre. It proved catastrophic. Capacity had been built ahead of demand on a scale that took most of a decade to absorb; the physical assets were sound but the market price of using them collapsed, and lenders discovered that collateral nobody wants is not collateral.

Today the estimate is made about accelerators, and the evidence points in two directions at once — which is why chapter twenty-eight refused to adjudicate and why this chapter will not either. On one side, the open-market rental price of an H100 fell from above eight dollars per GPU-hour at the 2023 peak of the shortage to a market median around three dollars by mid-2026, per trade price-trackers including Silicon Data and IntuitionLabs, with AWS cutting its own on-demand H100 pricing by forty-four per cent in June 2025. A collateral asset whose rental price more than halved in three years is a demanding thing to lend against. On the other, CoreWeave's chief executive said on the August 2026 earnings call that 2020-vintage A100 capacity had been contracted out to 2029 at full price, per Tom's Hardware and 24/7 Wall St. — because the binding constraint in 2026 was energised, cooled floor space rather than silicon, and an old chip in a powered hall is supply.

The arithmetic that decides it is unglamorous. Take an illustrative fleet, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company: one billion dollars of hardware, financed with seven hundred million of four-year amortising debt. The loan pays down roughly a quarter of principal a year. If the asset's market value decays at twenty per cent a year, the loan stays ahead of the collateral throughout and the lender is never exposed. If it decays at forty per cent, the loan is behind its security in year two and stays behind. Nobody knows which figure is right, and the entire secured-lending edifice of this cycle is an implicit vote on the answer.

## The case that it is different

Stated properly, because it is strong and a reader who cannot make this case is not equipped to assess the other one.

**The lender is not the seller.** This is the largest single difference. In the 1990s the vendors' own balance sheets held the credit risk: eight point one billion of Lucent's own commitments, three point one billion of Nortel's. In this cycle the overwhelming majority of the money comes from banks, private credit funds and insurance capital, and the August 2026 coalition is an explicit arrangement to source other people's capital rather than the seller's. Where credit losses arise, they arise for lenders who were paid to take credit risk, not for a manufacturer whose income statement then transmits them to its own ability to sell.

**The borrowers are creditworthy in a way CLECs were not.** The largest counterparties in today's contracts are among the most cash-generative businesses that have ever existed. Chapter one records Google Cloud's second-quarter 2026 revenue of twenty-four point eight billion dollars at a thirty-five point six per cent operating margin, per Motley Fool and Globe and Mail reporting in August 2026. A start-up carrier with a licence and a plan is not the same counterparty as a trillion-dollar operating company, and the credit spread between them is not a matter of opinion.

**The assets are used, and the capacity is sold.** The telecommunications build-out laid fibre that was not lit for years. In this cycle the recurring complaint from buyers is that they cannot get capacity, not that they cannot fill it. Demand is being turned away rather than manufactured — and an asset earning revenue at high utilisation from the month it is installed is a different credit proposition from an asset waiting for a market to arrive.

**The accounting rules are tighter and the disclosure is contemporaneous.** Chapter fifty-six sets out the ASC 606 treatment of consideration payable to a customer, which did not exist in its present form in 2000; the substance of Lucent's regulatory case was undisclosed customer incentives, and that specific gap has been closed. Today's structures are announced by both parties, filed in commitments notes, and visible in quarterly holdings reports. Whatever else is true, they are not hidden.

**The instruments are more varied, and several are not credit at all.** A warrant paid to a customer, an equity stake taken at fair value alongside other investors in the same round, and a right to invest later are different animals from a loan. They do not create a receivable that can go bad, and several of them transfer value to the customer rather than extending it.

## The case that it is not different

Stated with equal force, because the differences above are real and none of them is decisive.

**The shape is identical.** In both eras, the seller's revenue depends on the buyer's ability to raise money from someone else, and the seller has taken deliberate action to improve that ability. Every argument about who holds the paper is a second-order argument about a first-order fact that has not changed.

**Relocating risk is not removing it.** Moving the credit exposure from the vendor's balance sheet to private credit funds, special purpose vehicles and leases that have not yet commenced makes the total exposure harder to observe, not smaller. In 2001 an analyst could read a vendor's customer-financing note and see the number. The BIS thought the modern equivalent worth a paper in March 2026 precisely because the equivalent number is now distributed across structures that report to nobody in particular. A shock that is difficult to locate is not a shock that has been avoided.

**Substance over form applies to backstops.** A residual purchase obligation, a lease guarantee and a warrant priced off purchase volume are not loans. Economically, each one improves a customer's ability to buy the seller's product, and each one leaves the seller worse off if the customer's business fails. That is what vendor financing does. Calling it something else changes the accounting and not the exposure.

**The correlation is the point.** Chapter fifty-six put it as arithmetic rather than accusation, and it bears repeating here. When the same demand assumption supports the seller's revenue, the value of its equity stakes, the viability of its residual backstop and the credit quality of the fleets financed against its hardware, those are not four independent risks. They are one risk, recorded in four places. That was also true in 1999.

**Some of the buyers file nothing.** The creditworthiness argument is strong for the hyperscalers and much weaker for the private model developers, whose accounts, as chapter five established, no outsider has audited and whose spending commitments are funded from successive rounds of private capital. Where a contract's counterparty is a loss-making private company, the quality of that contract is the quality of its next round — which is exactly the dependence the CLEC financings had, wearing better clothes.

**The maturity schedule and the useful-life test arrive together.** Trade coverage in 2026 described a cluster of neocloud debt maturities falling between 2026 and 2028, arriving as the 2023 and 2024 hardware vintages reach the age at which residual-value assumptions stop being theoretical. Refinancing risk and collateral risk peaking in the same window is the configuration that made 2001 severe.

**Everyone thought it was different then, too.** This is the least quantitative point and the hardest to dismiss. Each 1990s financing was defended by competent, well-informed people with detailed models and genuine reasons. The structures were not stupid. They were rational responses to competitive pressure, and they became dangerous in aggregate rather than individually. Nothing about the intelligence of today's participants distinguishes them on that dimension.

## What to watch, from the filings

None of the following is an accusation, and finding any of it is a reason to ask a question rather than to reach a conclusion.

| What to check | Where it lives | What a change would mean |
|---|---|---|
| Seller's own financing extended to customers, and its trend against revenue | Commitments and contingencies note; receivables note | The 1990s pattern returning to the seller's balance sheet |
| Days sales outstanding, and any lengthening of payment terms | Working-capital disclosure and cash flow statement | Revenue being purchased with credit rather than sold for cash |
| Guarantees, residual purchase obligations and lease backstops | Commitments note; announcements by the counterparty | Vendor financing in a form that never touches the receivables line |
| Advance rates and spreads on new secured facilities against old | Borrower's debt disclosures and rating actions | Lenders repricing the collateral, in either direction |
| Renewal rates on expiring capacity contracts against original rates | Operator results commentary | The residual-value question being answered in public |
| Impairments or useful-life changes on hardware carrying values | Accounting policy and estimates notes | The assumption underneath the whole structure moving |
| Customer concentration crossing ten per cent thresholds | Segment and concentration note | Whose decisions actually determine the revenue |

Of those, the first is the sharpest single indicator, and it deserves the last word in this section. In 1999 the equipment makers were proud of their customer financing and described it in investor presentations as a competitive advantage. In August 2026 the largest supplier in this industry publicly assembled six of the world's biggest asset managers so that its customers could borrow from someone else. Those are opposite postures, and the direction of travel between them is observable. If the third-party capital pools work, seller-provided credit stays small and the comparison weakens with every quarter. If they do not — if the private lenders retreat and the seller's own balance sheet quietly steps in to fill the gap — then the structural difference this chapter has taken seriously will have disappeared, and it will have disappeared in the commitments note rather than in a press release.

## What the rhyme is worth

Two conclusions, and neither is a forecast.

The first is that the historical comparison is legitimate and should not be dismissed as pattern-matching by people who do not understand the technology. The mechanism is genuinely the same mechanism: a seller acting to fund its own demand, in an industry building capacity years ahead of the revenue that must justify it, with counterparties whose survival depends on capital markets staying open. That configuration ended badly once, in living memory, and it ended badly for reasons that were fully disclosed in advance and read by nobody with sufficient attention.

The second is that the differences are substantive and should not be dismissed either. Credit risk sitting with diversified professional lenders is not the same as credit risk sitting with the manufacturer. A counterparty generating tens of billions of dollars of operating cash flow is not a CLEC. Capacity that is sold out is not dark fibre. Anyone who repeats the 2001 comparison without engaging with those three facts is doing history rather than analysis.

Both statements are true at once, and the resolution between them is not available yet. It will arrive as data — in renewal rates, in impairments, in the spreads on refinancings, and in whether the seller's own balance sheet stays out of the credit business. Those are observable quantities, published quarterly, available to any reader willing to look them up.

Every figure in this chapter carries a date because every figure in this chapter will move: the debt issuance totals, the rental rates, the size of the third-party capital pools, the value of the equity stakes, the status of the guarantee reported as under consideration in July 2026. The 2001 figures will not move, which is the one advantage history has over the present. Use them the way an accountant uses a comparative period — not as a prediction, but as the only other observation in the sample.
