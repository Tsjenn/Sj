# Anthropic I: The Founding

At the end of 2020, the vice-president of research at the best-known artificial intelligence laboratory in the world resigned. Within weeks a group of his senior colleagues had followed him out of the door, and in January 2021 they incorporated a new company, per the company histories compiled by Britannica and Contrary Research. The departures were not accompanied by a lawsuit, a leaked memo, or a public argument. By the standards of corporate splits it was remarkably quiet. Five years later the quiet company was worth, on paper, close to a trillion dollars: Anthropic closed a sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, as reported by CNBC in May 2026.

This chapter is about the founding: who left, the reasons they gave, and the two deliberate choices that separated the new company from the old one — a research posture organised around safety, and a commercial strategy aimed at enterprises rather than consumers. The next two chapters deal with the ownership structure and the valuation. This one deals with the decision to exist.

A note before starting. Corporate splits generate competing narratives, and this one has generated more than most. The people involved have been restrained in public; the commentary around them has not. The discipline this book applies elsewhere applies here with extra force: where the record is thin, say so, and read the documents rather than the retellings. As it happens, the documents are unusually eloquent. The clearest statement of why Anthropic was founded is not anything a founder said in an interview. It is the corporate structure the founders built, the investors they took, and the first products they shipped. Companies reveal their beliefs in what they sign.

## The people who left

The departure was led by Dario Amodei, who had been vice-president of research at OpenAI, and his sister Daniela Amodei, who had also been a vice-president there — her Forbes profile records the role as vice-president of safety and policy. He became chief executive of the new company; she became president. That sibling pairing at the top has persisted ever since, and it matters for a reason that will recur in the next chapter: a founding team that stays together and keeps control is in a much stronger position when large investors arrive later.

They did not leave alone. The founding group included Tom Brown, an engineering lead on GPT-3; Jared Kaplan, a physicist whose work on scaling laws — the empirical relationship between compute, data and model capability — underpinned the era's central bet; Chris Olah, one of the most cited researchers in interpretability, the study of what is actually happening inside a trained model; Jack Clark, who had run policy at OpenAI; and the researchers Sam McCandlish and Benjamin Mann, per the founding-team accounts assembled by Contrary Research and Britannica. Published accounts differ on whether the founding group is best counted as seven or eight; the company's own materials have generally said seven co-founders, and this book will not pretend to more precision than the record supports.

Look at the composition rather than the count. This was not a group of product managers leaving to build an application. It was a vertical slice through a research organisation: scaling-law theory, large-model engineering, interpretability, safety policy, operations. A team like that can do exactly one thing, and it is the most expensive thing in the industry — train frontier models from scratch. Whatever the disagreement was about, the people leaving were not walking away from large-scale artificial intelligence. They were taking the capability with them and setting it up under a different roof.

That is worth dwelling on, because it distinguishes this split from the ordinary kind. Most corporate departures are about the next rung: an executive leaves to be a chief executive somewhere smaller. This group left seniority at the field's most famous laboratory to start again at zero, in a business whose entry ticket — training compute — is measured in hundreds of millions of dollars before a single customer exists. Chapter five's warning applies here: a private research laboratory publishes no accounts, so an outsider cannot watch the cost of that decision accrue. But the shape of it is visible from the funding history below. The founders' first two years were spent buying the right to compete in a game whose stakes they already knew precisely, because they had helped set them.

## The reasons given

Why did they leave? The honest answer is that the full story has never been laid out by either side, and this book will not invent one.

What the record supports is this. Contemporary and subsequent coverage — Britannica's company history is representative — reported that the Amodeis left over concerns about OpenAI's direction, in the period after OpenAI had taken its first billion-dollar investment from Microsoft in 2019 and begun commercialising GPT-3 through an API. Accounts of the split published since, including profiles of the founders, describe a difference of view about how much weight to give safety and alignment research — the work of making a model's behaviour predictable and controllable — relative to the race to scale capability. Dario Amodei has described the founding group as people who shared a specific set of beliefs about how safely to build increasingly capable systems, and who wanted an organisation in which those beliefs set the agenda rather than contending for it.

Notice what that framing does and does not claim. It does not claim that the people who stayed at OpenAI were indifferent to safety; OpenAI has maintained safety teams and published safety research throughout its existence, and its own account of its structure — covered in chapter thirty-four — is built on the same vocabulary of benefiting humanity. It does not claim the disagreement was about money, and the record gives no reason to think the founders left for want of compensation. What it claims is narrower: a group of senior researchers concluded that the institution around them would not weight the problem the way they would, and rather than keep arguing, they left to build an institution that would.

This book takes no side on the underlying question — whether the marginal dollar at a frontier laboratory is better spent on capability or on alignment, and in what ratio. That is a genuinely contested technical and philosophical dispute, and an accountant has no standing to settle it. What an accountant can observe is that the dispute was real enough to be priced. People do not abandon senior positions, recruit a founding team, and raise venture capital to escape a disagreement that could have been resolved in a meeting. The split is itself evidence of how deep the difference ran; the direction of the split — toward more emphasis on safety, by the founders' own account — tells you which side of the argument the leavers were on. Everything else is inference, and the reader should treat confident retellings of what happened inside OpenAI in 2020 with the same scepticism this book applies to unaudited revenue figures.

There is one more reading worth naming, because it is the cynical one and it deserves a fair hearing. A sceptic might say that "we left over safety" is a flattering story for a company whose commercial position depends on being trusted, and that every challenger needs a differentiator. That reading cannot be disproved from public information. But it has to contend with an awkward fact of sequence: the founders adopted the safety framing in 2021, years before it had commercial value, at a time when there was no consumer AI boom, no enterprise procurement checklist asking about model safety, and no obvious market reward for the posture. They also encoded it in legal structure, which is the expensive way to make a marketing claim. The structure is the next section.

## A structure as a statement

Anthropic was incorporated in Delaware as a public benefit corporation. The choice sounds technical and is worth a paragraph, because it is one of the few founding decisions that is legible directly from the legal record rather than from anybody's retelling.

An ordinary corporation's directors owe their duties to the shareholders, and the working presumption is that the shareholders want the value of their shares maximised. A public benefit corporation is a Delaware form that adds a stated public purpose to the charter and permits — requires, formally — the directors to balance shareholder interests against that purpose. Anthropic's stated purpose is the responsible development and maintenance of advanced artificial intelligence for the long-term benefit of humanity, per its corporate materials as described in the company's governance publications. The practical force of the form is contested among lawyers, and a benefit corporation is not a charity: nothing in the structure prevents profit-seeking, and Anthropic has sought profit vigorously. What the form does is give directors legal cover to make a decision that costs shareholders money in the name of the stated purpose — cover an ordinary board does not have.

On top of the corporate form sits a stranger and more distinctive mechanism. In 2023 the company described its Long-Term Benefit Trust: an independent body designed to hold a special class of shares — Class T — whose members are meant to be financially disinterested, and which gains, on a schedule, the power to elect a growing portion of the board, ultimately a majority, per the company's 2023 governance announcement. The design intent is explicit: as the company grows and its ordinary shareholders multiply, control over the board shifts toward a body that does not hold an economic stake and is charged with the long-term interest of the mission rather than of the capital.

Treat the trust the way this book treats every governance mechanism: as a claim to be tested against events, not a guarantee. Trusts have members, members are appointed, and any structure can be amended by whoever holds the power to amend it. The point here is narrower and belongs in a founding chapter: this structure was built at the start, when the company was small and the founders could have kept simple, conventional control. A team that had just left one institution over how it was governed sat down and wrote governance before it wrote products. Whatever else the founding was, it was deliberate.

The comparison with the older company's structure is instructive and is developed in chapter thirty-four: OpenAI began as a non-profit and bolted a capped-profit subsidiary underneath it when the fundraising mathematics demanded it, an arrangement that later had to be restructured. Anthropic ran the sequence the other way — a for-profit form from day one, with the mission constraint written into the for-profit's own charter and share classes. Neither arrangement is obviously superior. Both are attempts to solve the same problem: how to raise capital at the scale frontier models require without the capital fully owning the mission. The industry has effectively run two live experiments in mission-constrained corporate design, and the honest position in 2026 is that neither has yet faced the test that matters — a direct, expensive conflict between the mission and the money, resolved in public.

## Paying for a principle

Structure is free to write down. Training runs are not. The funding history of Anthropic's first two years shows what it cost to buy into the frontier — and it contains the founding era's one genuine scandal-adjacent episode, which deserves precise handling.

| Round | Announced | Amount | Led by | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Series A | May 2021 | 124 million US dollars | Jaan Tallinn | Company announcement, May 2021; FinSMEs |
| Series B | April 2022 | 580 million US dollars | Sam Bankman-Fried | Company announcement, April 2022; SiliconANGLE |
| Google investment | Reported February 2023 | About 300 million US dollars, for a stake reported at about ten per cent | — | Financial Times, February 2023; VentureBeat |

The Series A tells you who believed first. It was led not by a conventional venture firm but by Jaan Tallinn, the Skype co-founder who had spent the previous decade funding research into existential risk, with participation from James McClave, Dustin Moskovitz, the Center for Emerging Risk Research and Eric Schmidt, per the company's May 2021 announcement as covered by FinSMEs. That investor list is a signature. This was capital drawn from the small world that had been worrying about advanced AI for years before it was fashionable — money that shared the founders' stated priors rather than money betting on a product roadmap. One hundred and twenty-four million dollars was, by the standards of what came later, almost nothing; by the standards of a company with no product, no revenue and a stated intention to spend it on research, it was a serious opening commitment.

The Series B is the episode that requires care. The five hundred and eighty million dollar round announced in April 2022 was led by Sam Bankman-Fried, then the chief executive of the FTX cryptocurrency exchange, with participation from several of his associates alongside returning backers, per the company's announcement as covered by SiliconANGLE and Private Equity Wire. Some eighteen months later FTX collapsed into bankruptcy and Bankman-Fried was subsequently convicted of fraud; the money he had deployed across his investments became, in substance, an asset pool for defrauded creditors. In March 2024 the FTX bankruptcy estate agreed to sell the majority of its roughly eight per cent Anthropic stake — a holding acquired for about five hundred million dollars — for eight hundred and eighty-four million dollars, with the largest portion going to ATIC Third International Investment Company, an Abu Dhabi buyer aligned with the Mubadala sovereign fund, alongside Jane Street, HOF Capital, the Ford Foundation and funds managed by Fidelity, as reported by CNBC in March 2024.

Read that episode as an accountant rather than as a moralist, because the mechanism is the useful part. A company that sells shares to an investor receives the cash at closing; the cash is then the company's, regardless of what later happens to the investor. When the investor fails, the shares do not evaporate and the company is not liable — the shares simply become property of the estate, to be sold like any other asset for the benefit of creditors. Anthropic's operations were never dependent on FTX's survival, and the estate's sale of the stake changed Anthropic's shareholder register, not its balance sheet. The episode cost Anthropic reputational awkwardness and cost nobody at Anthropic any money. Meanwhile the estate's creditors were the accidental beneficiaries of the stake's appreciation. There is no allegation here against Anthropic, and the public record supports none: taking investment from a fraudster who has not yet been exposed is a misfortune shared by a long and distinguished list of counterparties. The lesson is about diligence limits, not complicity — an investee's ability to vet its investor is, in practice, close to zero once the money clears.

The third row of the table belongs to the founding story's hinge. In February 2023 the Financial Times reported that Google had invested roughly three hundred million dollars in Anthropic for a stake reported at about ten per cent, with Google Cloud becoming a preferred provider of computing capacity; coverage at the time put the implied valuation around five billion dollars, per VentureBeat's February 2023 report. The timing was pointed: the report landed days after Microsoft's expanded investment in OpenAI, reported at ten billion dollars in January 2023, as contemporary coverage noted. The pattern that would define the model layer — each major cloud attaching itself to a frontier laboratory, part investment, part capacity deal — was now fully visible, and Anthropic was inside it. Where that led, including Amazon's arrival and commitments that grew to tens of billions from both clouds, is the subject of the next chapter.

## Two quiet years, then a product

Between January 2021 and March 2023, Anthropic shipped no product. For a venture-funded company that is a long silence, and it was spent in a way consistent with the founding story: on research, much of it published.

The publication that mattered most commercially appeared in December 2022: a paper describing Constitutional AI, a training method in which a model is steered by an explicit written set of principles — a constitution — and by feedback generated by the model itself against those principles, reducing the dependence on armies of human raters. The technique became the basis of the company's models and, as importantly, the basis of its pitch: behaviour shaped by a document you can read, rather than by an undocumented pile of human judgements.

The product arrived in March 2023. On the fourteenth of that month, per TechCrunch's report and the company's own announcement, Anthropic launched Claude — and the shape of the launch is the founding strategy made visible. There was no consumer application on launch day. There were two models, Claude and a cheaper, faster variant called Claude Instant, offered through an API — a programmable interface for businesses to build on — after a closed testing period with named partners: Notion, which used it inside a writing assistant; Quora, which offered it through its chat application Poe; and DuckDuckGo, which used it for answering search queries, per the same March 2023 reports.

Contrast the sequence with the company up the road. OpenAI's ChatGPT, launched in November 2022, was a consumer product first, and its explosive adoption set what chapter one called the industry's clock speed. Anthropic, launching four months into that frenzy, declined to chase it. It shipped infrastructure for other companies' products, with reference customers already installed. A consumer chat interface followed later, and a paid consumer tier later still; but the door the company chose to open first was the business door.

## Enterprise-first, as a strategy rather than a slogan

It would be easy to read the enterprise focus as timidity — the challenger conceding the consumer market to the incumbent. The accounts suggest a more deliberate reading. Consumer and enterprise revenue are different substances, and the choice between them shapes everything downstream.

| Dimension | Consumer-first | Enterprise-first |
|---|---|---|
| How revenue arrives | Millions of small subscriptions, won one at a time | Fewer, larger contracts, won through procurement |
| Cost to serve | Heavy free tier subsidising conversion | Usage mostly paid for from the first token |
| Churn behaviour | Cancelling is one click; loyalty is shallow | Switching means re-engineering workflows; stickier |
| Marketing cost | Brand and virality, expensive at scale | Sales teams and certifications, expensive per deal |
| Customer concentration | Very low | A real risk — a top customer can be material |
| What the buyer asks | Is it good and is it fun | Is it reliable, safe, compliant, supported |

Neither column is simply better. Consumer scale delivers brand, data and a moat of habit; enterprise revenue delivers predictability and margin discipline but concentrates risk in a shorter customer list — the same concentration risk chapter three taught you to look for in a chip company's filings. The point is that the columns reward different companies. A laboratory whose differentiator is that its models are controllable, documented and steered by an explicit constitution has built exactly the attributes the right-hand column buys. An enterprise deploying a model into customer service or legal drafting is not shopping for delight; it is shopping for the absence of surprises. Safety research, whatever its motivations, produces the evidence such a buyer asks for. The founding posture and the commercial strategy are not two separate decisions. They are one decision, seen from the laboratory side and the invoice side.

The strategy is visible in the revenue mix that later reporting described. By 2026, roughly eighty per cent of Anthropic's revenue was coming from its API and enterprise customers rather than consumer subscriptions, per valueaddvc's 2026 breakdown of the business, while 2026 trade coverage consistently put the majority of OpenAI's revenue with consumers — estimates ranged from around sixty to seventy per cent depending on the source and month. None of these figures is audited, all of them move quickly, and chapter five's warnings about private-company numbers apply in full. But the direction of the contrast has been stable across every account: two frontier laboratories, one earning mostly from people, the other mostly from businesses. The scale that mix eventually reached — an annualised revenue run rate reported above sixty-five billion dollars in July 2026, per CNBC in August 2026, with all the caveats about run rates that chapter one set out — belongs to chapter thirty-nine. What belongs here is that the mix was chosen at the founding, in March 2023, before anyone could know whether it would work.

## What the split settled, and what it did not

Step back from the detail and the founding resolves into a clean sequence. A disagreement inside one institution, serious enough that argument gave way to departure. A group with the full capability set walking out together. A new company built with unusual care in its legal structure, funded first by mission-aligned capital, then — through the accident of one investor's fraud — becoming an incidental asset of a bankruptcy estate, and then drawing in the first of the cloud giants. A safety-forward research identity, published before it was profitable. And a commercial launch aimed at enterprises while the incumbent owned the consumer moment.

What the founding did not settle is the disagreement itself. Both institutions still exist; both train frontier models; both say safety is central and both raise capital at a scale that demands commercial success. Whether the industry needed a separate company organised around the safety-first bet, or whether the same work would have happened anyway inside the original one, is unknowable — the experiment has no control group. What can be said with confidence is accounting-shaped: the split created a second buyer of frontier-scale compute, a second seller of frontier models, and a second destination for the tens of billions of dollars the cloud companies were preparing to deploy into the model layer. Competition at the model layer, with everything it later implied for pricing, for cloud strategy and for the valuations in the anchor chapters ahead, dates from those quiet resignations at the end of 2020.

The next chapter follows the money that arrived afterwards: two competing clouds as investors in the same private company, up to thirty-three billion dollars committed by Amazon and up to forty-three billion by Google per 2026 reporting, and the governance puzzle of taking both cheques without being owned by either. The founding built the vessel. The next question was whose capital would fill it.
