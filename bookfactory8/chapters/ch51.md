# What Users Actually Pay For

A renewal notice arrives ninety days before a contract ends, and somewhere in a finance function a person has to decide whether to sign it. The invoice says one line: annual subscription, so many seats, so many dollars. It does not say what the seats did. Multiply that decision by a few hundred thousand companies and you have the revenue on which every capital budget in this book depends.

This chapter is about that decision. Not about whether the technology works — that argument is conducted elsewhere and by other people — but about why a buyer signs, which of those reasons survive a bad quarter, and how a reader can tell the two apart from the outside. It matters more than any other question in the application layer, because the money that pays for the fabs, the data centres and the accelerators enters the system here and nowhere else. Roughly seven hundred billion US dollars of capital expenditure was in prospect from the four largest buyers in 2026, as reported by CNBC in February 2026. Every dollar of it is a bet that renewal notices keep getting signed.

## Five reasons a buyer signs

Purchase reasons in this market fall into five recognisable groups, and they are not equally durable. Some of them appear in the accounts. Some of them appear only in a slide deck. One of them is not a reason at all in the ordinary sense; it is a fear, and it has been paying real invoices for three years.

The first is time: a task that took an hour now takes fifteen minutes. The second is headcount: a role that existed last year does not exist this year. The third is capability: the buyer can now do something that was not previously purchasable at any price, or not at a price they could meet. The fourth is obligation: a regulator, an auditor or a customer contract requires something the buyer must now demonstrate. The fifth is anxiety: a competitor has announced something, a board has asked a question, and a budget has appeared.

Set them out with the test each one has to pass.

| Reason for buying | Where it should appear | How durable | The honest test |
|---|---|---|---|
| A saved hour | Nowhere, unless converted | Durable only if converted into fewer hours paid or more output sold | Did total labour cost fall, or output rise, in a line you can point at? |
| A removed headcount | Employee costs, headcount disclosure | Durable, and the hardest to reverse quietly | Did the role stay gone twelve months later? |
| A capability not previously purchasable | New revenue, or a cost that was previously prohibitive | Most durable of the five | Is there a product or process that did not exist before it? |
| A compliance obligation met | Legal and professional costs, or embedded in product | Durable while the rule stands, and set by statute rather than by return | Is there a dated obligation and a named regulator? |
| Fear of being left behind | Central budget with no operational owner | Cyclical. The first thing cut | Who loses their bonus if the spend stops? |

The rest of this chapter takes them in turn, then asks what the aggregate evidence says about the mix.

## A saved hour is not a saved dollar

Start with the most commonly claimed benefit and the weakest one, because a great deal of published enthusiasm rests on it.

Suppose a tool saves each of a thousand knowledge workers thirty minutes a day. That is five hundred hours a day, and at a fully loaded cost of, say, sixty dollars an hour it looks like thirty thousand dollars a day, or something over seven million a year. The arithmetic is correct and the conclusion is usually wrong. Those figures are illustrative, chosen for round numbers, and belong to no real company.

The saving is not real to the accounts unless one of two things happens. Either the organisation pays for fewer hours — fewer people, less overtime, fewer contractors — or it sells more of something with the hours released. If neither happens, the thirty minutes disperse into the working day and leave no trace in any ledger. Nothing in the profit and loss account has a line called "time". An accountant reading a business case built on saved hours should ask, in the plainest language available, which existing line item is expected to go down, and by how much, and in which period. If the promoter of the business case cannot answer, the saving is notional.

There is a second problem, which is that self-reported time savings are unreliable in a specific and measurable direction. In a randomised controlled trial published in July 2025, the research organisation METR had sixteen experienced open-source developers work through two hundred and forty-six tasks in repositories they knew well, with AI tools permitted on a randomly assigned subset. Measured completion time with the tools available was nineteen per cent longer. Asked afterwards, the same developers estimated on average that the tools had made them twenty per cent faster.

That result deserves careful handling, and METR itself has been careful with it. The sample was small, the developers were experts working in codebases they knew intimately — close to the hardest case for an assistant — and the tools were those available between February and June 2025. METR has since said publicly, in a February 2026 note on redesigning the experiment, that the finding should be read as historical and does not necessarily describe current tools or workflows. It is not evidence that assistants do not help anyone. It is evidence of something narrower and more useful: that the gap between how fast people feel and how fast they are can be large, can run in the unflattering direction, and can survive being asked politely.

Every survey finding built on "employees report saving x hours a week" inherits that gap. Such surveys are not worthless — perception drives renewal, and a tool people believe in gets used — but they are not measurement, and a business case that cites them as measurement is doing something an auditor would not accept from a client.

The honest version of the time argument is narrower and holds up better. Where a task is discrete, high-volume, previously done by people, and now completed to an acceptable standard without them, the saving converts because the work was being bought by the unit in the first place. Transcription, first-draft translation, routine document review, first-line support triage: these have unit costs that were already visible in someone's budget, and when the unit cost falls, a real line falls with it. The further a use case sits from a countable unit of purchased work, the more likely the saving is to evaporate on contact with the accounts.

## The only reason that shows up in the accounts

Removed headcount is different, because payroll is a line item and people notice when it moves.

The most-cited example is also the most instructive, because it has now run long enough to have a second half. In February 2024 Klarna announced that its AI assistant, built with OpenAI, had handled two point three million conversations in its first month — about two-thirds of the company's customer service chats — doing work equivalent to seven hundred full-time agents, with average resolution time falling from eleven minutes to under two, repeat enquiries down twenty-five per cent, and an estimated forty million US dollars of profit improvement expected in 2024. Those are the company's own figures from its own press release of February 2024, not audited disclosure, and they were reported widely at the time including by Forbes in March 2024.

By May 2025 Klarna was rehiring human agents. The chief executive's own account, reported by Forbes that month, was that the company had focused too much on efficiency and cost, that quality had suffered, and that customers wanted the option of a person; the company moved to a model in which a human is always reachable. Note what did and did not reverse. The automation of the high-volume, low-complexity tier stayed. What came back was the complex and premium tier, which is exactly the work least suited to a system that answers from patterns. The first announcement was true and the correction was also true, and reading only one of them gives a badly distorted picture.

A second example, from a company that sells the tools rather than merely buying them: in remarks reported by Fortune on 2 September 2025, Salesforce's chief executive said the company's support organisation had gone from nine thousand people to about five thousand since the start of 2025, that roughly half of customer interactions were then handled by agents rather than people, and that support costs had fallen by about seventeen per cent. He also described redeploying staff into other functions rather than simply removing them. That last detail is the one to hold on to, because redeployment and removal look identical in a press quotation and entirely different in the accounts. A person moved from support to professional services is still on the payroll. Total employee costs, not the headcount of one department, is where the claim gets settled.

For a reader assessing this category from outside, three checks do most of the work. Look at total employee costs rather than a departmental number. Look one full year later, because roles cut in an enthusiastic quarter have a way of reappearing under different titles. And look at whether the company describing the saving is a buyer or a seller of the technology, because a vendor's own deployment is simultaneously an operational decision and a marketing asset, and the second purpose does not make the first one false but does mean the figure was chosen.

Removed headcount is the most durable of the five reasons precisely because it is the most painful to reverse. Nobody rehires four thousand people to unwind a subscription. Where this reason is genuinely present, the spending attached to it is close to permanent, and it will survive a downturn better than almost anything else in a software budget.

## A capability that could not be bought before

The third reason is the strongest and the least discussed, largely because it does not fit neatly into a return-on-investment template.

Some things are now purchasable that were not purchasable at all. A mid-sized company can have every one of its customer conversations transcribed, summarised and searched. A law firm can run a first pass over a document set that would previously have needed a room of contract lawyers and a month. A small software team can maintain interfaces in thirty languages. None of these were expensive before; they were unavailable, in the sense that the price at which they could have been done exceeded what any buyer would pay, so nobody bought them and the demand never appeared in any market statistic.

This category is durable for a structural reason. When the purchase creates a product or a process the buyer did not previously have, removing it means removing the product. That is not a subscription cancellation; it is a strategic retreat. Contracts of this kind renew almost by default, and they are the ones that quietly build the base revenue underneath the noisier growth.

They are also the hardest to measure with the usual tools. There is no "before" to compare against, because the activity did not exist. A business case that says "this year we did something we have never done" cannot compute a percentage improvement, and in organisations where funding is allocated by percentage improvements, that is a disadvantage rather than a recommendation. It is one of the reasons the aggregate return evidence looks worse than the operational reality in places: the most valuable purchases are the ones the measurement framework was not built to see.

Where the new capability produces revenue, of course, it shows up like any other revenue, and then the argument is over. That is the shape to look for in a buyer's accounts: not a cost line falling, but a revenue line existing that did not exist two years ago, with the tooling cost sitting underneath it in cost of sales.

## The obligation nobody chose

The fourth reason is the one least affected by anything happening in this industry, because it is set by statute.

The European Union's AI Act put a schedule of dates into law, and those dates create purchasing that has nothing to do with expected return. In June 2026 the position changed materially: on 16 June 2026 the European Parliament gave final approval to the Digital Omnibus amendments, which deferred the Act's high-risk obligations substantially — standalone high-risk systems under Annex III moving from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and AI embedded in regulated products under Annex I to 2 August 2028 — according to analyses published in 2026 by law firms including Gibson Dunn, Holland & Knight and Travers Smith. What was not deferred, on those same analyses, were the transparency duties in Article 50 and the Commission's enforcement powers over general-purpose models, both of which still landed on 2 August 2026.

Two lessons sit in that paragraph, and the second one is the important one.

The first is that regulatory demand is real demand. An obligation with a date attached produces a buyer who is not comparing the purchase against a hurdle rate. They are comparing it against a penalty. Governance tooling, model documentation, audit trails, disclosure mechanisms and the professional services around them get bought because a lawyer said so, and that spending is unusually insensitive to the technology cycle. It is also, notably, spending that flows to a different set of vendors than the ones the headlines are about: compliance is bought from consultancies, from governance software firms, and from the incumbent enterprise platforms that already hold the data, rather than from the model labs.

The second lesson is that regulatory deadlines move. A great many 2025 business cases in Europe were built around August 2026, and in June 2026 the ground shifted by sixteen months. If a vendor's forecast depends on a compliance date, the forecast depends on a political process, and political processes are not a reliable revenue driver. The correct treatment of regulatory demand in any assessment is: durable in direction, unreliable in timing.

## The reason nobody writes on the purchase order

Then there is fear, and this section is where the chapter has to be blunt.

A material share of current spending is not the result of a measured return. It is the result of a board asking a chief executive what the company is doing about artificial intelligence, and the chief executive needing an answer by the next meeting. That produces a budget with an unusual set of properties: it is central rather than departmental, it is defended by someone senior, it is not required to clear the hurdle rate applied to other capital allocation, and it has no operational owner whose bonus depends on it working.

There is nothing dishonest about that. Option value is a legitimate reason to spend money, and a company that learns how to deploy a technology two years before its competitors has bought something real even if the pilot itself produced nothing. The problem is not that the spending exists. The problem is that it is counted, by the layers below, as though it were the same kind of revenue as a renewed departmental contract with a measured saving behind it. It is not. It is the most cyclical revenue in the industry, and it is the first line cancelled when a quarter goes badly.

The best-known attempt to size the phenomenon is Project NANDA's report at MIT, *The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025*, whose preliminary findings were published in July 2025 and reported very widely that August. Its headline was that around ninety-five per cent of enterprise generative AI pilots had produced no measurable return in the profit and loss account, with roughly five per cent extracting substantial value.

That figure has been repeated so often that it needs its caveats restated. The study drew on a review of more than three hundred publicly disclosed initiatives, interviews with representatives of fifty-two organisations and survey responses from a hundred and fifty-three senior leaders, gathered between January and June 2025. It was not peer-reviewed. It relied in part on self-reported outcomes. And "no measurable return" is a claim about measurement as much as about value — a pilot that produced a capability nobody had a template for measuring counts as a failure by this definition, as the previous section explained. It is one report and not settled evidence.

Its most useful findings were the ones underneath the headline, which travelled far less. About half of generative AI budgets went to sales and marketing, while some of the clearest returns appeared in unglamorous back-office automation: companies spent where the excitement was rather than where the payback was. And initiatives built with external partners reached deployment about two-thirds of the time, against about a third for those built internally. Neither of those findings is about the capability of models. Both are about organisational behaviour, which is what the anxiety category is made of.

The counterweight comes from a source with no interest in the outcome. The United States Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey asks a large sample of firms directly whether they used AI in producing goods or services in the past two weeks. Its published results put use between about seventeen and twenty per cent of businesses from December 2025 through May 2026, with nineteen point eight per cent as at May 2026, and between twenty and twenty-three per cent expecting to use it within six months. Adoption rises sharply with size: around thirty-seven per cent of firms with at least two hundred and fifty employees, thirty-two per cent of those with a hundred to two hundred and forty-nine, and under twenty per cent of firms with four or fewer employees. By sector, as at 3 May 2026, use ran at thirty-nine point seven per cent in Information and thirty-three point nine per cent in Finance and Insurance, against about fourteen per cent in Retail Trade.

Read those two sources together and a more sober picture emerges than either provides alone. Adoption is real, concentrated in large firms and information-handling sectors, and growing but not vertically. Four out of five American businesses were not using the technology in operations in mid-2026. That is not a market in collapse; it is an early market, which is a different thing, and it is one where the loudest spending and the most measurable value are not yet in the same place.

## Where the demand is visible in money

Survey data describes intention. Invoices describe demand. The best public series on what enterprises actually spend on the model layer comes from Menlo Ventures, whose 2025 State of Generative AI in the Enterprise report, published in December 2025, put total enterprise spending on generative AI at thirty-seven billion US dollars in 2025, against one point seven billion in 2023. Within that, spending specifically on large language model APIs rose from three point five billion at the end of 2024 to eight point four billion by August 2025. The same report put Anthropic at about forty per cent of the enterprise API market, OpenAI at twenty-seven per cent and Google at twenty-one per cent, with the three together accounting for the large majority of enterprise usage. These are a venture firm's estimates from its own survey work, not audited figures, and the market-share numbers in particular should be read as one house's measurement rather than an industry census.

Set that alongside what the model companies themselves have told investors. Anthropic said its annualised revenue run rate had climbed above sixty-five billion dollars in July 2026, reported by CNBC in August 2026; OpenAI's annualised run rate was reported at around forty billion dollars as at August 2026 in trade coverage of the two companies. As chapter one insisted, an annualised run rate is a recent period multiplied up. It is not audited, it is not a year's revenue, and it is not comparable with the trailing figures listed companies report. It is nonetheless the number the whole stack below is being built against.

On the consumer side the disclosure is thinner still. Compilations of 2026 reporting describe ChatGPT weekly users rising through roughly nine hundred million in February 2026 toward approximately a billion in July 2026, with something like fifty million paying subscribers plus more than nine million paying business users — a paid conversion rate in the region of five to six per cent. OpenAI is private; none of those figures is audited or filed, and the compilations that carry them do not always agree. Treat the conversion rate as an order of magnitude rather than a measurement. What it suggests, even loosely, is that the free tier is a marketing cost of considerable size.

## What the pricing model reveals

Vendors know which of the five reasons is paying them, and their pricing shows it.

A per-seat price is a bet on the saved hour. It bills for access, not for use, and it works when the buyer believes a category of worker is generally more productive. It is comfortable for the vendor, because revenue is predictable, and dangerous for the same reason: a seat that goes unused is a seat removed at renewal, and the vendor finds out too late.

A consumption price bills for what was done. It is honest in both directions, and it transfers the volatility to the vendor, which is why finance functions on both sides tend to dislike it. An outcome price — per resolved case, per completed action, per closed ticket — is the pricing model of the removed headcount, because it compares directly with what the displaced work used to cost.

Salesforce's Agentforce has now been through all three, which makes it a useful case. It launched in 2024 at two dollars per conversation. In May 2025 the company announced Flex Credits, a consumption model priced at five hundred dollars per hundred thousand credits with an action costing twenty credits — ten cents an action — alongside pay-as-you-go, pre-commitment and pre-purchase options; per-user licensing was subsequently offered as a flat-fee alternative from a hundred and twenty-five dollars a month. Those figures are from Salesforce's May 2025 announcement and 2026 pricing analyses including Constellation Research. The stated reason for the change was granularity and predictability; the two-dollar conversation had drawn criticism for being both expensive and hard to forecast.

The direction of travel across the industry is from seats toward consumption and outcomes, and that direction is itself evidence. Vendors do not move to usage-based pricing when seats are being used heavily; they move when a buyer with a low utilisation rate has started reading their own telemetry. It is also the pricing model that most exposes the vendor to the layer below, because a consumption invoice can only be profitable if the cost of serving the action is below the price charged — which returns the whole question to inference cost, and therefore to the compute and silicon layers, which is where the next chapter begins.

## Telling durable from cyclical, from outside

A reader with no access to a private company's management accounts can still form a defensible view. Five questions do most of the work, and none of them requires a subscription to anything.

Ask who signs the cheque. Spending controlled by a central innovation function is anxiety-funded until proved otherwise. Spending that has migrated into an operational budget — support, legal, marketing production, engineering tooling — has an owner who defends it because it does their job, and that spending behaves like software maintenance rather than like discretionary investment.

Ask what got smaller. Durable purchases usually displace something: a contractor line, an outsourcing contract, a licence for the tool that used to do the job badly. If nothing shrank and nothing new was sold, the purchase is additive spending, and additive spending is reviewed in every downturn.

Ask what the vendor discloses about retention rather than growth. Growth in a young market tells you about sales capacity. Retention tells you about value. Compilations of 2026 reporting suggest one-year retention on ChatGPT's tiers of roughly eighty-eight per cent for Enterprise, sixty-eight per cent for Team and fifty-nine per cent for Plus — figures that are neither audited nor company-disclosed, and are cited here for their shape rather than their precision. That shape is entirely consistent with everything else in this chapter: the more the purchase is embedded in an organisation's work, the more it stays.

Ask whether the pricing moved, and which way. A shift to consumption is a vendor's admission that seats were not being used. A shift toward committed contracts is a customer's admission that usage is now predictable enough to buy in bulk.

Ask what happens at the second renewal, not the first. Almost anything gets bought once. The organisation that has now had eighteen months of experience, has replaced the champion who bought it, and signs again, is telling you something the first order could not.

## Why the whole stack is standing on this

The argument of this book is that value has been moving down the stack, and the evidence for that is in the margins at the bottom. But the money moves in the opposite direction to the value. It comes in at the top, from a buyer signing a renewal, and it works downward through model fees, cloud invoices, accelerator purchases and wafer starts. The layers below are not merely exposed to this layer's revenue. They are exposed to a version of it that has been extrapolated forward several years and cast in concrete.

That is the reason to be careful about the composition of current demand, rather than only its size. Demand founded on removed headcount, on capabilities that did not previously exist, and on statutory obligation is unusually robust: it survives a recession, it survives a disappointing model release, and it survives a change of chief executive. Demand founded on a saved hour that never converted, and on a board's discomfort at being asked what the company is doing, is not robust at all. Both kinds of demand are currently paying the same invoices, and they are indistinguishable in a revenue line.

Nothing in the public record makes it possible to say what the split is, and this book will not invent a number for it. Anyone who tells you the proportion with confidence has estimated it. What can be said with confidence is which observations would settle the question, and they are the ordinary ones: net retention rates as they are disclosed, the migration of budgets from central to operational lines, whether pricing settles on consumption or drifts back to seats, and whether the total employee costs of buyers — not the headcount of one department in one press quotation — begin to bend.

This is not investment advice and contains no view on any security. It is a description of where the industry's cash actually originates, and of the fact that a very large construction programme is being financed against it. The layers below have committed to their capital expenditure. The buyers at the top have committed to nothing beyond the current term. That asymmetry is the single most important structural feature of the industry as it stood in the middle of 2026, and it is why the next chapter asks the question this part has been circling: when the money has finished moving down through all four floors, which of them gets to keep it.
