# Nvidia as an Investor

On 14 August 2026, Nvidia filed a Form 13F. That is a routine document for an asset manager and an unusual one for a company that designs chips. The filing disclosed a portfolio that compilations of it put at roughly sixty-three billion US dollars, with about thirty billion in Intel — a little under half the total — and about twenty-one billion in SpaceX, per the Motley Fool and 24/7 Wall St. writing on the filing in August 2026.

Two years earlier, that sentence would have described a mid-sized investment institution. It now describes a side activity of the company that sells the industry its accelerators.

This chapter is the one in the book that has to be written most carefully, so it is worth stating the rules at the top. Everything described here is disclosed. Everything described here is lawful. Investing in your customers is an ordinary commercial practice with decades of precedent in semiconductors, telecommunications equipment and industrial supply. Nothing in this chapter alleges improper conduct by Nvidia or by any company it has invested in, and nothing here is investment advice. What the chapter does is set out what the public record contains, explain in neutral terms why a dominant supplier might rationally take equity in the businesses that buy from it, show where each holding lands in the accounts, and give the reader a specific list of things to check in the filings. The purpose is to make the reader able to form their own view from primary documents, not to supply one.

## Three windows, none of them complete

The first thing to understand is that there is no single place where this portfolio is disclosed. There are three, they have different scopes, different timings and different rules, and they do not reconcile to each other. Reading one and assuming it is the whole picture is the commonest error in coverage of this subject.

| Window | What it covers | Timing | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual report and quarterly filings | Aggregate carrying values, gains and losses, equity-method holdings, investment commitments, concentration | Quarterly, weeks after period end | Aggregated; individual holdings mostly unnamed |
| Form 13F | Reportable holdings in certain exchange-traded securities | Quarterly, up to 45 days after quarter end | Only 13(f)-eligible securities; a snapshot at one date; says nothing about why |
| Announcements and press reporting | Named transactions, sometimes with amounts and structures | On announcement, then never updated | Announced intentions are not signed obligations, and are rarely reconciled to later filings |

The third window is the one most readers actually use, and it is the least reliable, not because journalists are careless but because an announcement is a statement of intention at a moment in time. Nothing obliges anyone to publish a correction when the intention changes. This chapter's central example of that is below.

The accounts are the authoritative window, but they are aggregated. They will tell you what the whole portfolio is carried at and what it did to reported profit. They will not, in general, name the companies. The 13F names companies, but covers only the securities it is required to cover, arrives with a lag, and is a photograph of one date. Compilations of the August 2026 filing did not even agree with each other on the portfolio total, which is a good reason to read the filing rather than the summaries of it.

## The record, as far as it goes

What follows is the public record as at August 2026, grouped by the kind of counterparty. Every figure carries its date and its source. Every one of them will have moved by the time you read this, and several will have moved a great deal, because marked-to-market positions move daily and private valuations move whenever somebody else transacts.

The OpenAI line is the reason the third disclosure window deserves suspicion. The September 2025 announcement was a letter of intent: non-binding, milestone-linked, expressed as "up to". The figure of one hundred billion dollars entered general circulation immediately and stayed there. By February 2026 Jensen Huang was telling Fortune that the pledge had "never" been "a commitment"; 2026 reporting described the finalised position as an equity stake of roughly thirty billion dollars taken in the round OpenAI closed in the first quarter of that year. This book cites that round elsewhere, from valueaddvc and Forbes in 2026, as one hundred and twenty-two billion dollars at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation in March 2026; other contemporaneous accounts put the round at one hundred and ten billion dollars announced on 27 February 2026. Both cannot be right, and neither company files audited accounts that would settle it. Where the record disagrees with itself, the honest thing is to show the disagreement rather than pick the tidier number.

The size of the correction is the point worth keeping. The difference between an announced hundred billion and a settled thirty billion is seventy billion dollars — larger than the annual revenue of all but a handful of companies on earth. Announced figures in this industry are marketing as much as accounting, and the gap between an intention and an obligation is where most of the confusion in this subject lives.

### Robotics, tools and the wider ecosystem

Below the named transactions sits a venture programme. Trade compilations in 2026 described NVentures, the corporate venture arm, as a two-person team with seventy-nine cumulative investments and twenty portfolio companies at unicorn valuations, having made forty-three new investments in the preceding twelve months and twenty in the first five months of 2026, against thirty deals in 2025. Those are compilations rather than filings and should be read as approximate; the underlying deals are individually small enough that they do not appear separately in the accounts.

Two named examples give the flavour. Nvidia was among the investors in Figure AI's Series C of more than one billion dollars at a thirty-nine billion dollar post-money valuation in September 2025, per the company's own announcement. It participated in a $1.05bn round for the autonomous-driving developer Wayve in May 2024, with further investment reported as expected in 2026 coverage.

These holdings are trivial against a thirty-billion-dollar stake in Intel. They matter for a different reason: they are the layer at which a supplier shapes the software ecosystem that will run on its hardware for the next decade. A robotics company that builds on one vendor's development platform is a customer for a very long time.

## Why a dominant supplier might do this

Set the suspicion aside for a moment and take the strategic logic on its merits, because it is genuinely strong and a reader who cannot state it fairly is not equipped to assess it.

**The binding constraint is not chips.** This is the most important argument and the least understood. A supplier invests in demand when demand is the scarce thing. Through this period demand was not scarce — capacity was. What was scarce on the buyers' side was capital, power and buildings. An equity investment in a data centre operator does not create appetite for accelerators that did not exist; it removes a financing obstacle standing between existing appetite and a delivered order. Chapter thirty-one made the case that power has replaced silicon as the binding constraint. If that is right, then investing in the people who assemble land, power and capital is investing in the removal of the bottleneck, which is a different act from buying demand.

**Complementary assets are worth more together.** An accelerator is useless without a building, a grid connection, cooling, networking and software. A supplier whose product is one input among several has an interest in the others existing in sufficient quantity, and a direct financial interest if it owns part of them. This is the ordinary logic of vertical investment, and it is why Corning, Nokia and Intel appear in the same portfolio as CoreWeave and OpenAI.

**Capital is abundant and internally generated.** A company producing very large operating cash flows must do something with them. The choices are dividends, buybacks, acquisitions, or investment. Acquisitions of significant scale in this industry attract competition review and, in several jurisdictions, have failed on that ground. Minority stakes do not, in general, face the same obstacle. A minority investment programme is partly the residue of an acquisition strategy that regulation has made impractical.

**Standards and ecosystems.** Chapter thirteen argued that the durable asset in this business is the software platform, not the die. Early money in a robotics or tooling company is cheap compared with the cost of losing a generation of developers to a competing platform.

**Return, plainly.** The Intel position was reported at roughly six times its cost within a year. It is not necessary to construct an elaborate strategic rationale for an investment that performed like that, and it would be naive to assume returns played no part.

There is a symmetric argument on the other side, and it should be stated with the same clarity. A supplier that invests in its own customers has correlated its asset values with its revenue. In a downturn the two fall together, because they were never independent risks. The customer whose spending falls is also the holding whose value falls, and possibly the borrower whose debt was raised against equipment the supplier sold. That is not an accusation; it is arithmetic about correlation, and it applies to any supplier who does this, in any industry, in any decade.

## Where each holding lands in the accounts

This is where an accountant earns their keep, because the treatment determines what a reader sees and where.

Under US accounting standards, equity securities with readily determinable fair values are measured at fair value with changes running through net income, under ASC 321. That is the Intel and Nokia treatment: the position is remarked every quarter and the movement lands in the income statement, below the operating line, in other income and expense.

Holdings without readily determinable fair values — private companies, which is most of the model labs and all of the venture portfolio — are commonly carried at cost less impairment, adjusted for observable price changes. In practice that means they are revalued only when somebody else's transaction forces the issue. A private holding can therefore sit unchanged for a year and then jump on a single funding round, which is why the reported gains from private positions are lumpy in a way the underlying businesses are not.

Where an investor has significant influence but not control — commonly, but not mechanically, indicated by a holding around twenty per cent or by board representation — the equity method applies under ASC 323, and the investor picks up its share of the investee's results rather than marking the position.

The consequences of all this are visible in the filings, and the numbers are large. In the Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 April 2026, filed on 20 May 2026, Nvidia attributed the change in other income and expense against the same quarter a year earlier primarily to unrealised gains on publicly held equity securities of thirteen point four billion dollars and on non-marketable equity securities of two point six billion. The same filing disclosed cumulative gross unrealised gains on non-marketable equity securities of five point three billion dollars, against cumulative gross unrealised losses and impairments of one hundred and ninety-nine million; one billion dollars of investments in infrastructure funds accounted for under the equity method; and total investment commitments of about twenty-seven billion dollars, subject to contingencies, expected to be made through the remainder of the fiscal year.

Put the sixteen billion of unrealised gains beside the quarter's revenue of eighty-one point six billion dollars, up eighty-five per cent year on year with GAAP earnings of two dollars and thirty-nine cents a diluted share, per the results announcement of 20 May 2026. The gains are not revenue and are not in operating income. They are, nonetheless, a material contributor to the bottom line for that quarter, and they arose from price movements in securities rather than from selling anything.

Three properties of that number deserve emphasis, and none of them is a criticism.

It is symmetric. Fair-value accounting through profit and loss reports declines exactly as promptly as it reports gains. A reader who is impressed by sixteen billion of gains in a rising quarter has accepted, whether they realise it or not, the same treatment in a falling one.

It is unrealised. No cash moved. The position is still held, still exposed, and worth whatever the market says on the next reporting date.

It is disclosed. The company reported it, in the filing, in the note, in the management discussion. Nothing about this is hidden, and the analyst's task is not detection — it is arithmetic.

### An illustrative separation

Take an illustrative company, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real business. It reports operating income of one hundred, other income of forty of which thirty-five is unrealised marks on equity holdings, and a tax charge that need not concern us. Reported pre-tax profit is one hundred and forty. Growth against a prior year at one hundred and ten looks like twenty-seven per cent.

Now strip the marks from both years. Operating income of one hundred against, say, ninety-five is growth of five per cent. Both calculations are correct. They answer different questions, and only one of them is about the business of selling products.

The point of the exercise is not that marks are illegitimate. They represent real changes in the value of real assets. The point is that they are a separate business — an investment business — reported inside the same set of accounts, and a reader who wants to know how the chip operation performed must separate the two before comparing anything.

## What an analyst should actually watch

Here is the practical section. Each of these can be checked from public documents by a reader with no special access. None of them is an accusation, and finding a change in any of them is a prompt to ask a question, not a conclusion.

**Read operating income before anything else, and compute a version of earnings with the marks removed.** Other income and expense is a note in every quarterly filing. Subtract the unrealised gains and losses from pre-tax profit in each of the last eight quarters and plot the result. If the shape of the adjusted series differs materially from the headline series, the headline series is partly an investment story. This is five minutes of work and it settles more argument than any commentary.

**Watch the investment commitments figure against revenue growth.** Commitments are the forward-looking half of the portfolio: money promised, not yet spent, and in some cases contingent. Twenty-seven billion dollars was the disclosed figure at 26 April 2026. What matters is the trend of that number relative to the trend of revenue. Commitments growing faster than sales, over several quarters, is the pattern that chapter three flagged in the abstract, and it is observable here without inferring anything about intent.

**Establish whether any investee is also a disclosed major customer, and accept that you probably cannot.** The concentration disclosure names no customers — ASC 280 does not require it, and Nvidia's filings identify them only as letters. Chapter fifteen sets out the figures: for fiscal 2026 as a whole, two direct customers at twenty-two and fourteen per cent of revenue; in the third quarter of that year, four at twenty-two, fifteen, thirteen and eleven per cent. Whether any of those letters corresponds to a company in the investment portfolio is not disclosed, and no amount of reasoning from outside will establish it. When the answer to a question is "the filings do not say", that is the answer, and the analyst who supplies a guess instead has stopped doing analysis.

**Know what would trigger related-party disclosure, and check whether it has.** Related-party disclosure under ASC 850 bites where a relationship could affect the terms of transactions — significant influence, common control, board representation. A minority stake with no board seat and no influence over operating decisions frequently does not meet the threshold, in which case revenue from that counterparty is not separately identifiable in the accounts. So watch for the things that would change the answer: a board seat, an increase in holding towards the level where significant influence is presumed, a shift of a holding from cost basis to the equity method, or a new related-party note appearing where none existed. Any of those changes what must be disclosed, and each is visible in the filings.

**Understand the revenue-recognition rule that governs consideration paid to a customer, and watch how transactions are characterised against it.** Under ASC 606, consideration payable to a customer is accounted for as a reduction of the transaction price — that is, of revenue — unless it is in exchange for a distinct good or service transferred to the seller, at no more than fair value. An investment in equity, made at fair value on terms available to other investors in the same round, is ordinarily an investment rather than consideration payable to a customer, and is accounted for as such. The judgements that matter are therefore about facts: was the investment made at the same price and on the same terms as other participants; is the security a genuine equity interest; is there a linkage between the investment and the pricing of a supply agreement. Those judgements are made by management and tested by the auditor. What a reader can watch is the disclosure of the terms, the presence or absence of a critical audit matter on the subject, and any change in the accounting policy note describing how such transactions are treated. Chapter sixty-one takes up what the auditor's side of that work looks like.

**Read the structures that are not investments at all.** The obligation to purchase up to six point three billion dollars of a customer's unsold capacity through April 2032, reported by CNBC in September 2025, is not an equity holding and will not appear in a portfolio table. It is a contractual commitment with a credit character, and it strengthens a borrower's position with lenders precisely because it comes from the party with the deepest interest in the collateral. Similarly, the xAI arrangement was reported as running through a special purpose vehicle that buys hardware and leases it on, which is a financing structure rather than an ownership one. Portfolios of this kind are not fully described by their equity list, and the commitments and contingencies note is where the rest lives.

**Track the exits.** Positions dropped tell you where the strategic interest has ended. The August 2026 filing showing exits from Arm, Applied Digital, Recursion Pharmaceuticals and WeRide is, in its quiet way, as much a statement of direction as any new stake.

**Watch what management says about the direction of the programme.** In March 2026 Jensen Huang indicated that the company might have written its last large cheques to the leading model developers, per TechCrunch on 4 March 2026. Statements of that kind are cheap and non-binding, and they are also the clearest forward-looking information available. Record them, date them, and check them against the following year's filings.

## What none of this will tell you

The accounts do not name the investees behind the aggregate carrying values. They do not separate revenue originating with an entity the company has invested in, and no rule requires them to. They do not disclose the terms of individual supply agreements, or whether the price paid by an investee differs from the price paid by anyone else. They do not tell you what a private holding is worth on any date other than the one on which someone else transacted. The 13F does not cover holdings outside its defined scope, and arrives with a lag of up to forty-five days. And nothing in any of the three windows tells you why a decision was taken, which is the question most readers actually want answered.

That is not a complaint about disclosure. Disclosure rules are designed to make positions and their effects visible, not to explain motives, and it is not the accounts' job to adjudicate strategy. It is a boundary, and knowing where it sits is the difference between analysis and speculation.

Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot with a date attached. The portfolio total, the Intel mark, the SpaceX position, the commitments figure and the unrealised gains will all be different by the time you read this — the marked positions will have moved daily, and the private ones on the next round anyone else raises. The current versions are in the company's most recent quarterly filing and its most recent 13F, both published on its investor relations pages, and in the announcements of the companies at the other end of each transaction. Look them up, write the date beside each one, and redo the arithmetic in the watch list above. It takes an hour, and it is worth more than any summary of it, including this one.

## The honest summary

A supplier with equity across its customer base is a structure that correlates things which would otherwise be independent: reported profit and asset values, revenue growth and investment gains, the health of the buyer and the value of the stake. That correlation is a fact about the structure. It is not evidence of anything improper, and this chapter asserts nothing of the kind.

What the structure does is raise the standard of reading required. Where a supplier sells to unconnected customers, revenue growth and asset values are separate pieces of information. Where it holds equity in the buyers, they are not, and a reader who treats them as separate will double-count good news and be surprised twice in a downturn. The remedy is not suspicion. It is arithmetic, performed on disclosed numbers, with the operating line kept firmly apart from the marks.

Chapter fifty-seven takes the next step, and examines the circular structure directly: what it means in accounting terms when money moves from a supplier to a customer and back again as revenue, what the recognition rules require, why it is not automatically improper, and what would distinguish a healthy version from an unhealthy one. That chapter, like this one, describes mechanisms. The verdicts belong to the reader, and they should be reached with the filings open.
