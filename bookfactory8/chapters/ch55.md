# Amazon, Google and Anthropic

On 30 July 2026 Amazon published a set of quarterly results in which the largest single number was not earned by selling anything. Net income came in at sixty-two point six billion US dollars. Inside it sat fifty-three point four billion of non-operating pre-tax other income, arising principally from the revaluation of the company's investments in Anthropic, per the company's second-quarter 2026 results published in July 2026 and the coverage of them. The entire operating business — the retail network, the advertising machine, the largest cloud on earth — produced twenty-seven point five billion in the same three months. Earnings per share were five dollars seventy-five against analyst expectations of roughly one dollar eighty-one, per CNBC in July 2026.

Three weeks earlier, on 22 July 2026, Alphabet had done something structurally similar. Revenues of one hundred and nineteen point eight billion dollars, up twenty-four per cent; operating income of forty point eight billion, up thirty per cent; and net income of about one hundred and twelve billion dollars, a rise of roughly two hundred and ninety-eight per cent on the twenty-eight point two billion of a year earlier, per Alphabet's second-quarter 2026 results and reporting on them by Yahoo Finance and CNBC in July 2026. The gap between forty point eight and one hundred and twelve was other income: a net gain of about ninety-eight billion dollars, overwhelmingly unrealised marks on equity securities.

Two of the largest companies in the world, direct competitors in cloud computing, reported quarters in which the dominant profit figure came from holding shares in private companies they do not control. In both cases the largest of those holdings was a stake in the same company. This chapter is about how that happened, what each side actually bought, and what it does to the accounts of the buyers.

## How two rivals ended up in the same cap table

The relationships were built in tranches over three years, and the escalation is easiest to see laid out by date.

|---|---|---|---|
| Early 2023 | Google | Roughly $300m for a stake of about 10% | CNBC's later accounts of the relationship |
| Sept 2023 | Amazon | Up to $4bn; initial $1.25bn | CNBC / TechCrunch, Sept 2023 |
| Oct 2023 | Google | Up to $2bn more; $500m up front, $1.5bn over time | CNBC, Oct 2023 |
| Mar 2024 | Amazon | Second tranche of $2.75bn, completing the first $4bn | CNBC, Mar 2024 |
| Nov 2024 | Amazon | A further $4bn, total $8bn; AWS named primary cloud and training partner | CNBC, Nov 2024 |

Read down the right-hand column and notice how rarely more than a few months pass before the other name appears. The last two rows are four days apart. Whether the timing of the April 2026 announcements reflected direct competitive response is not publicly documented, and this book will not guess. The pattern is on the record; the motive is inference.

What matters for a chapter on ownership is that these are not two versions of the same transaction. Google's position was built partly through convertible debt: court filings in the United States government's search case against Google, reported by The New York Times in 2025, revealed that Google's funding had run through convertible notes, with a further seven hundred and fifty million dollars of notes then scheduled, and that its total funding exceeded three billion dollars at that point — more than had previously been public. Amazon's first eight billion dollars also went in as convertible notes, converted in part to nonvoting preferred stock in the first quarters of 2025 and 2026, per the description of Amazon's quarterly filings in reporting by GeekWire in 2025 and StockTitan in 2026.

The instrument is not a detail. A convertible note sits on the investor's balance sheet as a debt security until it converts. It carries no vote, triggers no ownership threshold, and attracts none of the regulatory attention that a straight equity block of the same size would bring. It is the standard answer to an awkward question: how does a strategic investor put in the money without acquiring the position?

## What Amazon was buying

Amazon entered this relationship with an obvious hole in its business and an expensive project that needed a reference customer.

The hole was in the model layer. Amazon Web Services rents more computing than anyone on earth. When the AI wave arrived, the thing customers suddenly wanted to run on rented computing was a frontier model, and Amazon did not have one of comparable standing. The company could build, buy or rent that capability. Building takes years and a research culture it did not have. Buying was not available — a controlling acquisition of a frontier lab would have gone straight into merger control on two continents. Renting exposure through a minority stake, paired with a commercial agreement, was the remaining option.

The expensive project was silicon. Amazon has spent a decade designing its own accelerators, and a cloud that designs its own chips escapes the merchant vendor's gross margin on every unit it does not buy. But an in-house accelerator has a credibility problem money cannot solve on its own: no serious customer bets a flagship training run on a chip nobody demanding has used. Anthropic could confer exactly that. Project Rainier, activated on 29 October 2025, is the physical form of the arrangement — a campus in Indiana reported by CNBC at eleven billion dollars of investment, running roughly half a million Trainium2 chips, dedicated to training and serving Claude, per CNBC and Data Center Dynamics in October 2025.

Third, and least discussed at the time, the money largely comes home. Anthropic's dominant cost is compute — figures shared with investors and reported by PitchBook and Morningstar in 2026 put compute at about seventy-one cents of every revenue dollar in the first quarter of 2026 — and its compute is bought, under these agreements, substantially from its own investors. A dollar invested by Amazon that returns as AWS revenue is capital-expenditure financing wearing equity's clothes.

That sentence describes a mechanism, not a misdemeanour. The arrangements were announced, the accounting is disclosed, and nothing in the public record suggests either side has recognised revenue it should not have. But a reader of Amazon's segment note should hold both ends of the loop in view at once. In the second quarter of 2026, AWS revenue reached forty-two point two billion dollars, up thirty-seven per cent year on year — the segment's fastest growth in eighteen quarters — with segment operating income of sixteen point six billion against ten point two billion a year earlier, per Amazon's second-quarter 2026 results and coverage by Yahoo Finance and KuCoin in July 2026. How much of that acceleration is arm's-length demand and how much is a partner spending money its cloud provider supplied is not separately disclosed, and the honest position is to say so rather than to guess a split.

## What Google was buying

Google's problem was the opposite shape. It already had a frontier model. Gemini competes with Claude about as directly as two products in this industry can. On the face of it, funding Anthropic looks like funding the competition.

Three things resolve the apparent contradiction.

The first is the same silicon argument, in a stronger form. Google's tensor processing units are the only serious alternative to merchant accelerators that has been in production for a decade, and their commercial value outside Google depends on evidence that somebody outside Google will train frontier models on them. The October 2025 agreement — access to up to one million TPUs and more than a gigawatt of capacity coming online in 2026, per CNBC in October 2025 — is that evidence bought at scale. No captive subsidiary could have supplied it. The demonstration works precisely because Anthropic was free to choose otherwise and conspicuously keeps Amazon's chips and Nvidia's in the building alongside.

The second is the cloud line. Google Cloud revenue reached twenty-four point eight billion dollars in the second quarter of 2026, up eighty-two per cent, with segment operating income of eight point eight billion against two point eight billion a year earlier, per Alphabet's second-quarter 2026 results and reporting on them in July 2026. A committed, multi-year, gigawatt-scale customer is a rare thing to put behind a cloud business's growth rate, and it is exactly the sort of contract that turns speculative data-centre capital expenditure into contracted demand.

The third is a hedge. Alphabet wins if Gemini prevails. Through a holding reported at about fourteen per cent, plus a TPU tenancy, it also participates if Claude does. That is not a subtle strategy, but it is a rational one for a company whose principal risk in this cycle is not that AI fails; it is that AI succeeds somewhere other than inside Google.

## What "up to" actually means

Both headline figures — thirty-three billion and forty-three billion — are ceilings, and the distance between a ceiling and a cash flow is the whole subject of this section.

A commitment of "up to" a sum is typically structured in tranches with later tranches conditional. Sometimes the condition is time and the investor's own option, as in Google's October 2023 commitment of five hundred million up front and one and a half billion over time, per CNBC. Sometimes it is explicit performance: CNBC's April 2026 report on Google's forty billion described ten billion invested at announcement and up to thirty billion contingent on milestones. The headline is the top of the escalator, not the first step.

How far up the escalator has anyone actually travelled? For Amazon there is a public answer of sorts. Analysis published by The Motley Fool in August 2026, working from Amazon's quarterly filings, put the total cash Amazon has put into Anthropic at about thirteen billion dollars, against a commitment ceiling of up to thirty-three billion. For Google, no comparable public reconciliation exists; the last authoritative figure for money actually in was the "more than three billion dollars" that emerged through litigation and was reported by The New York Times in 2025, since supplemented by the ten billion described as invested at the April 2026 announcement.

So the four figures a careful reader should keep separate are these:

| Figure | Amazon | Google |
|---|---|---|
| Announced ceiling | Up to $33bn (CNBC, Apr 2026) | Up to $43bn (CNBC, Apr 2026; 2026 reporting) |
| Cash actually invested | About $13bn (Motley Fool analysis of filings, Aug 2026) | Over $3bn as at 2025 litigation disclosure (NYT, 2025), plus $10bn described at the Apr 2026 announcement |
| Carrying value of the holding | $190.4bn at 30 June 2026 (Motley Fool analysis of filings, Aug 2026) | Not separately disclosed; grouped within equity securities |
| Ownership | Minority, no board seat (CNBC / GeekWire, Nov 2024) | About 14%, capped at 15%, non-voting (NYT reporting of court filings, 2025) |

Three of those four rows are commonly reported as though they were the same number. They are not. The first is a negotiating outcome, revisable. The second is cash. The third is an estimate of what the cash is now worth, and it is the one that hits the income statement. The fourth is what the money bought in governance terms, which is close to nothing.

## What a compute commitment means in practice

Alongside the equity sits the part of these deals that will still matter when the valuations have moved: the supply agreements. They deserve their own treatment because they behave differently from the investment in every respect that counts.

An investment is discretionary, one-directional and marked to a valuation. A compute commitment is contractual, mutual and settled in cash on a schedule. When a hyperscaler agrees to make a gigawatt of capacity available, it is agreeing to spend its own capital years ahead of the revenue: to sign for land and power, order accelerators, build shells, and accept the depreciation that follows. When a model company commits to spend at that scale, it is undertaking to find the revenue to pay for it.

For the supplier the accounting consequence is a familiar and slightly uncomfortable one. Capacity built for a named customer is capital expenditure now, revenue later, and depreciation for years in between over a useful life that management estimates. If the customer's demand arrives as contracted, the asset earns out. If it arrives late, the depreciation charge arrives anyway. This is why the segment margin of a cloud business is only partly a statement about pricing, and substantially a statement about timing and about an assumption.

Take an illustrative build, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. A cloud provider agrees to make capacity available to one large customer and spends twelve billion dollars over two years on the buildings, the power connections and the accelerators. Suppose the equipment is depreciated over six years and the buildings over twenty-five. Once the site is in service, the annual depreciation charge might run to something like one and a half billion dollars, and it runs whether or not the customer's workloads arrive on the day the contract assumed. If the customer spends as planned, the site earns its charge and a margin on top. If the customer's own revenue growth slips by two quarters, six months of depreciation lands against six months of thinner revenue, and the segment margin falls without a single thing having gone wrong technically. Shorten the assumed equipment life from six years to four — a live question wherever hardware is being replaced faster than it wears out — and the annual charge rises by roughly a billion on the same twelve billion of spending. No cash changes. The reported profit does.

That is the risk the supplier is carrying on behalf of a relationship the market mostly reads as an investment. It is also why the equity stake and the supply agreement should never be netted against each other in a reader's head. One is an asset whose value floats with a private valuation. The other is a fixed obligation to spend, settled in concrete and electricity.

For the customer, the commitment is the reason multi-sourcing is worth the engineering pain. A frontier lab with one supplier of computing is a lab whose supplier owns the renewal negotiation. Anthropic's workloads have been described across all three of Google's TPUs, Amazon's Trainium and Nvidia's hardware in the coverage of the October 2025 agreements. That is expensive — three software stacks, three sets of optimisation work — and it is what the second bidder is for.

The lines below summarise what each side of these arrangements gave and got, on the public record:

| What the hyperscalers received | What they did not receive |
|---|---|
| Minority equity and convertible positions | Voting control (Google's holding non-voting, per 2025 reporting) |
| An anchor tenant for their own accelerators | Board seats or observer rights (per 2024–25 reporting) |
| Large, contracted, multi-year cloud spending | Exclusivity — the rival cloud is always in the building |
| Exposure to the model layer's valuation | Any say in how the model layer is run |

## How the stakes sit in the investors' accounts

Here is where the chapter earns its place in a part about ownership, because the reason these holdings move headline earnings is a rule most readers have never had cause to learn.

Historically, a minority stake in a private company sat quietly on the balance sheet at cost. It moved only when it was sold, or when it was written down. Under the current United States standard for the recognition and measurement of financial instruments, that changed: equity holdings without a readily determinable fair value are remeasured when an observable transaction in the same or a similar instrument gives a new price, and the change goes through the income statement rather than sitting in reserves. The rule was introduced to stop investors from carrying stale assets at ancient values. Its effect in this cycle has been to turn a funding round into somebody else's earnings event.

The two investors arrive at that outcome by slightly different routes, and the routes are worth distinguishing.

Amazon's position began as convertible notes. Per the description of Amazon's quarterly filings in 2025 and 2026 reporting by GeekWire and StockTitan, the eight billion dollars of notes were classified as available-for-sale debt securities carried at fair value, with unrealised movements initially in other comprehensive income and their valuation treated as a Level 3 measurement — meaning the inputs are not observable market prices but the company's own estimates, built from the rights attached to the instrument, other classes of security outstanding, observable transactions such as new offerings, expectations about timing and type of liquidity event, and a discount for lack of marketability. In the first quarters of 2025 and 2026 a portion of those notes converted into nonvoting preferred stock, recorded at fair value on conversion and thereafter adjusted for observable price changes and impairments.

By 30 June 2026, on the Motley Fool's August 2026 reading of those filings, the holding stood at one hundred and ninety point four billion dollars: ninety-seven point nine billion of convertible notes and ninety-two point five billion of nonvoting preferred stock. Three months earlier, at 31 March 2026, the same analysis put it near seventy-four billion. The difference between the two dates is not a business event at Amazon. It is a funding round at Anthropic.

Alphabet does not break its Anthropic holding out. It reports gains and losses on equity securities in aggregate within other income, and its portfolio contains other large private positions. The Level 3 language is the same in substance; the disclosure is coarser.

Three practical consequences follow, and they apply to any reader looking at any investor in this position:

The gain is non-cash. Nothing has been sold. Free cash flow is unaffected, which is why the cash flow statement is the cleanest place to stand when the income statement is doing this.

The gain is anchored to a negotiated price. The valuation that drives it is the price at which a small number of shares changed hands in a private round, on terms — preferences, ratchets, information rights — that outsiders cannot see. Chapter five of this book sets out why that price is not equivalent to a market capitalisation.

And the gain is symmetrical. A rule that marks a stake up on a good round marks it down on a flat or falling one. Nobody who has enjoyed the upside of this treatment has been given an exemption from the other direction.

## The quarter the investment swallowed the income statement

Anthropic closed a sixty-five billion dollar Series H at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, as reported by CNBC in May 2026. Both investors' second quarters ran through the end of June. The consequences appeared within weeks of each other.

| Measure, Q2 2026 | Amazon | Alphabet |
|---|---|---|
| Operating income | $27.5bn | $40.8bn |
| Net income | $62.6bn | About $112bn |
| Non-operating item | $53.4bn other income, primarily Anthropic revaluation | Net gain of about $98bn on equity securities |
| Source | Company results, July 2026; CNBC, July 2026 | Company results, July 2026; Yahoo Finance and CNBC, July 2026 |

For Amazon, roughly two-thirds of pre-tax income for the quarter was a valuation mark, and the mark was nearly twice what the entire operating business earned. For Alphabet, reporting on the filing put the net gain on equity securities at about ninety-nine billion dollars, adding roughly seventy-seven billion to net income and about six dollars twenty-six to diluted earnings per share, per coverage of the quarter by StockTitan and TechTimes in July 2026. Alphabet's gain reflects more than one holding — its position in SpaceX is in the same line — and the company did not break the components out. One outlet's analysis attributed roughly eighty billion of it to the Anthropic mark, reasoning from a fourteen per cent stake and a valuation that moved from three hundred and eighty billion to nine hundred and sixty-five billion during the quarter. That is an outlet's arithmetic from public inputs, not a company disclosure, and should be read as such.

Both quarters were reported in the ordinary way, under the applicable standards, with the components visible to anyone who read past the headline. The distortion is not in the filings. It is in what happens to the numbers afterwards.

By 3 August 2026, CNBC was reporting that Big Tech's stakes in Anthropic and OpenAI were distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them, naming Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet as having booked sizeable investment gains in the quarter from stakes in Anthropic, OpenAI and, in one case, SpaceX. The article carried the figure that makes the point at index level: earnings growth for the S&P 500 in that quarter topped forty-eight per cent year on year, and excluding those investment gains it was closer to twenty-nine per cent. Gil Luria, head of technology research at D.A. Davidson, told CNBC that the headline earnings numbers "were very much inflated by equity gains in OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX", and that such moves "tend to even out over time, which is why we typically exclude them from a non-GAAP view and from forecasts".

Sit with that for a moment. A private funding round, negotiated between a handful of parties, priced by the most optimistic marginal buyer, moved the reported earnings growth of the largest equity index in the world by nineteen percentage points in a single quarter. Every index-tracking fund, every earnings-growth screen, every mechanical valuation model that takes reported profit as its input absorbed that number without knowing where it came from.

The professional response is unglamorous and takes about ninety seconds per company. Find the operating income line, which excludes the mark by construction. Find the other income line and read the note beneath it. Find cash flow from operations and capital expenditure, neither of which a valuation mark can touch. Then form a view on the business, and treat the stake as what it is: a separate asset of uncertain and volatile value, held alongside the business rather than produced by it.

## What neither of them bought

The last element is the one the numbers keep obscuring. For all the capital, neither investor bought control.

Court filings in the United States search case, reported by The New York Times in 2025, put Google's holding at about fourteen per cent, capped at fifteen, with no voting rights, no board seat and no board observer rights. Reporting of Amazon's November 2024 investment by CNBC and GeekWire recorded the same essentials on the other side: a minority position and no seat on the board. Above both sits Anthropic's own structure — a Delaware public benefit corporation, addressed as "Anthropic PBC" in the Federal Trade Commission's January 2024 orders, with a Long-Term Benefit Trust whose members hold no equity and whose function is to appoint directors; by mid-2026, directors chosen by the Trust held a majority of the seven-member board, with the remaining seats elected by stockholders, per the company's governance announcements and 2026 reporting.

The regulators looked, and looked in public. The Federal Trade Commission issued compulsory orders under section 6(b) of the FTC Act in January 2024 to Alphabet, Amazon, Anthropic, Microsoft and OpenAI, and published a staff report in January 2025 describing the equity, revenue-sharing, consultation and exclusivity rights attached to such partnerships. In the United Kingdom, the Competition and Markets Authority decided in September 2024 that the Amazon–Anthropic partnership did not qualify for investigation under the merger provisions of the Enterprise Act 2002, and concluded in 2024 that Google had not acquired the ability materially to influence Anthropic's commercial policy.

Notice the double duty those clauses perform. The cap, the non-voting stock and the absent board seat protect the investee from its investors, and they are simultaneously the jurisdictional answer to a merger regulator. It would be naive to suppose the drafters were unaware of both functions. It would be equally wrong to read the clearances as settled for all time: each decision was a finding about the arrangements as they then stood, not a licence for whatever the next tranche contains.

## Where to look next, and what none of this is

Four places in the public record will tell you how this arrangement is developing, and none of them requires access anybody lacks.

The other income line in both investors' quarterly reports, with the note beneath it, is where a private company's funding round becomes a public company's earnings volatility — in both directions.

The segment disclosures for AWS and Google Cloud, read alongside each new compute commitment, are where the round-trip question lives. Growth and margin moving together is a different signal from growth bought with the investors' own capital.

The capital expenditure line against the depreciation charge is where the compute commitments eventually land. Capacity contracted today is a depreciation charge for years, and the useful life assumed is management's estimate.

And the merger-control registers on both sides of the Atlantic are where "no material influence" will be tested again if the structure changes.

Every figure in this chapter is a photograph. The commitment ceilings of thirty-three and forty-three billion dollars date to April 2026; the carrying values and earnings effects to the quarter ended 30 June 2026; the ownership percentages to filings reported in 2025. A valuation mark that produced a ninety-eight billion dollar gain in one quarter can produce a charge in another, and by the time this is read the drawn amounts, the marks and possibly the structures will have moved. Check them against the companies' own disclosures and the financial press before repeating any of them.

And nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything, or a prediction about any share price. The purpose of the chapter is narrower and, in the long run, more useful: to make sure that when a headline says a company's profits tripled, the reader's first instinct is to ask which line it happened on.
