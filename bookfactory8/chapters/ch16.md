# AMD: The Long Second Place

In November 2012 a share in Advanced Micro Devices traded at about a dollar eighty, having touched eight dollars twenty in March of the same year. The stock was the worst performer on the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index that year, down close to sixty per cent, as Forbes reported at the end of November 2012. A Nasdaq commentary published on 19 October 2012 carried the headline "Could AMD go bankrupt?" and answered its own question carefully: the company still had a market capitalisation of about 1.55 billion US dollars and was holding about 1.3 billion in cash at the end of September 2012, so an imminent failure was extremely unlikely. That is the tone of the coverage. Not "will it recover", but "how long does the cash last".

Fourteen years later the same company was worth roughly 765 billion US dollars, the nineteenth most valuable company in the world on the companiesmarketcap ranking as at August 2026.

That is the largest turnaround in the modern semiconductor industry, and it is worth studying for two reasons. The first is the mechanics: how a company with a broken balance sheet and a losing product line repaired itself, in what order, and what each step cost. The second is the question the recovery raises and has not yet answered — what second place is actually worth when the leader's advantage is partly written in software.

## What AMD has always been

AMD sells into an instruction set it does not own. For most of its history its commercial position has been the alternative supplier: the same architecture as the market leader, at a lower price, with the promise that a buyer who standardises on one vendor has no bargaining power at renewal. Being the second source is a real business. It is also a structurally awkward one, because the second source is worth most to customers who never buy very much of it.

Everything that follows is a variation on that theme. The 2006 acquisition that nearly killed the company was an attempt to escape it; the 2012 collapse was what happened when the second source stopped being credible; and the question hanging over the accelerator business in 2026 is whether the second-source logic works at all where the switching cost is not the chip but the code.

## The acquisition that broke the balance sheet

AMD completed its acquisition of ATI Technologies on 24 October 2006. The value was approximately 5.4 billion US dollars based on AMD's closing share price that day of 20.32 dollars, per the company's own announcement of the completion. The consideration was roughly 4.3 billion in cash and 57,946,017 AMD shares. The cash came from a 2.5 billion dollar term loan and about 1.8 billion of existing cash and marketable securities.

Read that funding structure before reading anything else about the strategy. A company bought a business worth more than half its own annual revenue, and paid for it mostly in borrowed money, at the top of a cycle, while its main product line was under attack. Whatever the industrial logic — and combining a processor designer with a graphics designer was a defensible idea the company eventually proved right, fifteen years later — the financing left no room for the operating business to disappoint.

It disappointed. On 6 December 2007 AMD filed a report stating that the goodwill recorded on the ATI acquisition was impaired, and on 17 January 2008 it determined the charge would be approximately 1.3 billion dollars, with a further 350 million or so of acquired identifiable intangible assets also impaired.

Goodwill impairment is one of the few places in a set of accounts where management must admit a mistake in figures. Goodwill is not an asset in any ordinary sense. When one company buys another, the accountants allocate the purchase price across the identifiable things acquired — plant, inventory, patents, customer relationships, brands — and whatever is left over is recorded as goodwill. It is the residual: the part of the price that could not be attached to anything you can point at. It is not amortised; it is tested for impairment, and if the business bought is no longer worth what was paid, the difference goes through the income statement.

An impairment charge is non-cash. The money left years earlier. What the charge does is correct the balance sheet's account of what that money bought. When you see a large goodwill write-off, the useful question is not how far profit fell but what the company paid, in cash and in debt, for the thing now being written off, and whether that debt is still outstanding. In AMD's case, in 2008, it was.

## Selling the factories

The next decision was structural. AMD had been an integrated manufacturer — it designed chips and it owned fabs. Fabs are the most capital-hungry assets in industry, and a fab operator that cannot fill its capacity carries an enormous fixed cost against falling revenue.

AMD's answer, announced on 4 March 2009 with Advanced Technology Investment Company of Abu Dhabi, was to spin the manufacturing into a separate business initially called The Foundry Company and soon known as GlobalFoundries. ATIC invested 2.1 billion dollars for about 65.8 per cent of it; AMD held about 34.2 per cent, per the announcement carried by Mubadala.

The accounting consequence is the point. Going fabless takes an enormous fixed asset off the balance sheet, takes the depreciation on it out of cost of sales, and removes the capital expenditure a fab requires every year. What it does not remove is the obligation to buy wafers, because a foundry that has just absorbed your factories will not fill them at its own risk. That obligation went into a Wafer Supply Agreement, and for the next decade the WSA was one of the most consequential documents in AMD's affairs — a purchase commitment fixing how much AMD had to buy, from whom, at what price, at which technology nodes.

Purchase commitments of this kind sit in the notes to the accounts rather than on the face of the balance sheet. They are not debt, and they do not appear in net debt calculations. They are, in economic terms, extremely similar to debt: a fixed obligation to pay cash in future periods regardless of how the business performs. A reader who assesses a fabless company's flexibility from its balance sheet alone will miss them entirely.

## 2012: what near-death actually looks like in the accounts

For the year ended 29 December 2012 AMD reported revenue of 5.42 billion US dollars, down seventeen per cent, an operating loss of 1.06 billion and a net loss of 1.18 billion dollars, or 1.60 dollars per share, with gross margin of twenty-three per cent, per the company's annual results announcement of January 2013. In the fourth quarter it announced restructuring that cut global headcount by approximately fifteen per cent, taking a net restructuring charge of ninety million dollars in that quarter and expecting operational savings of about twenty million in the quarter and about a hundred and ninety million in 2013.

Take those numbers in the order an accountant takes them.

Gross margin first. Twenty-three per cent means that for every dollar of product sold, seventy-seven cents went on making it. In a business that must spend heavily on research and development merely to have something to sell in two years' time, twenty-three per cent is not a bad margin. It is an arithmetic impossibility: there is no plausible level of operating expense at which a semiconductor designer survives on it, because the research bill alone is typically a larger share of revenue than that.

Then the loss, which at 1.06 billion dollars on 5.42 billion of revenue is neither a rounding error nor a one-off. Then the cash: about 1.3 billion at the end of September 2012, against that rate of burn. Then the market capitalisation: about 1.55 billion at the same date. The market was valuing the whole enterprise at not much more than the cash inside it, which is its way of saying it expects the operating business to consume the rest.

The fifteen per cent headcount cut, at a saving of roughly a hundred and ninety million a year, closes about a fifth of the operating loss. That is the arithmetic of a company that cannot cut its way out. It has to sell something, and then it has to sell something better.

## The repair, transaction by transaction

What happened between 2013 and 2017 is usually told as a product story. It is at least as much a treasury story: a series of transactions, each converting a non-core asset into cash or an obligation into flexibility, and each carrying a disclosed cost.

| Date | Transaction | What AMD received | What it gave up |
|---|---|---|---|
| March 2013 | Sale and lease-back of the Lone Star campus in Austin to an affiliate of Spear Street Capital | Approximately 164 million dollars in cash, net of fees | Ownership of the site; a twelve-year sublease obligation |
| April 2016 | Sale of 85 per cent of the assembly, test, mark and pack operations in Penang and Suzhou to Nantong Fujitsu Microelectronics | Approximately 371 million dollars, about 320 million net of expenses | Control of its back-end packaging; retained 15 per cent |
| August 2016 | Multi-year amendment to the Wafer Supply Agreement covering 2016 to 2020 | Flexibility on where wafers could be made | A 100 million dollar cash payment in instalments, plus a warrant over 75 million shares at 5.98 dollars issued to a Mubadala subsidiary, valued at 235 million — 335 million of one-time cost in total |
| January 2019 | Seventh amendment to the Wafer Supply Agreement | Full freedom to use any foundry for 7nm and smaller nodes, with no one-time payments or royalties | Continued purchase targets at 12nm and above through March 2024, with shortfall payments if targets were missed |

Sources, in order: AMD's announcements of the Austin closing, March 2013, of the Nantong Fujitsu closing, April 2016, and of the WSA amendment, 31 August 2016, with contemporaneous coverage by AnandTech and StreetInsider; and the seventh amendment as filed with AMD's quarterly report for the first quarter of 2019.

Two of those four deserve a comment. A sale and lease-back does not create value. It converts an owned building into cash today plus a stream of rent tomorrow, and the rent is priced so the buyer earns a return. What it buys is time, and in March 2013 time was the scarce commodity. Read it as financing secured on property, and note that a reader looking only at the balance sheet sees cash go up and must reach the lease note to see what was promised in exchange.

The 2016 WSA amendment is the more interesting one. AMD paid a hundred million in cash and issued a warrant valued at two hundred and thirty-five million — that is, it paid partly in dilution — for flexibility about where its chips could be manufactured. Total accounting cost of 335 million dollars, for a company that had reported revenue of 3.99 billion in 2015, down twenty-eight per cent, with a full-year net loss of 660 million, an operating loss of 481 million, cash and cash equivalents of 785 million and total debt of 2.26 billion at year end, per its annual results announcement of January 2016.

A company with 785 million of cash and 2.26 billion of debt spent the equivalent of nearly half its cash balance to change the terms on which it could buy manufacturing. That is how central the manufacturing constraint was believed to be. It is also why the seventh amendment in January 2019 mattered: it gave AMD unrestricted access to any foundry at 7nm and below, without payments or royalties, at precisely the moment when the leading edge was about to decide the competitive outcome.

## The architecture decision

Lisa Su became president and chief executive on 8 October 2014, succeeding Rory Read, per AMD's announcement. She had been chief operating officer.

The decision associated with that period was to concentrate research spending on one new processor core architecture, Zen, and to accept a period of weakness elsewhere while it was built. The first Zen-based desktop processors reached the market in early March 2017 under the Ryzen brand; the server version, EPYC, launched on 20 June 2017 with up to thirty-two cores, per AMD's launch announcement.

The financial character of that decision is the part a company in distress usually cannot execute. Research and development is expensed as incurred. It does not sit on the balance sheet as an asset earning a return; it reduces reported profit in the year it is spent, and produces revenue, if it produces any, three or four years later. A loss-making company with a stretched balance sheet is under permanent pressure to cut precisely this line, because cutting it improves the reported result immediately and does no visible damage for two years. Every transaction in the table above bought room to avoid that cut.

There is a second half to the architecture decision that is often left out. A good design is worth nothing manufactured on an uncompetitive process. The freedom obtained in 2019 to use any foundry at 7nm and below let AMD put its designs on the leading edge while its principal competitor was manufacturing its own and struggling to advance. The design decision and the manufacturing decision were one decision.

## The proof: server processors

Whether a second source can take share from an entrenched leader is not a theoretical question for AMD's processor business. It has been answered.

According to Mercury Research data reported by Tom's Hardware and others, AMD reached a record 46.2 per cent of x86 server processor revenue in the first quarter of 2026, on a unit shipment share of 33.2 per cent. Across the whole x86 market, AMD's share reached 34.1 per cent in the second quarter of 2026, up from 32.6 per cent the prior quarter, with Intel at 65.9 per cent, down from 70.6 per cent a year earlier.

Two things in that paragraph repay attention.

The first is the gap between revenue share and unit share. Selling a third of the servers and taking nearly half the money means AMD is winning the expensive sockets — the high-core-count parts that go into data centres — rather than the cheap ones. Revenue share above unit share is the signature of a company competing on capability rather than on price. In 2012 the position was the reverse, and the twenty-three per cent gross margin said so.

The second is the timescale. Zen launched in 2017. Revenue share in servers crossed into the forties in 2026. Nine years, against a competitor that was itself struggling with its manufacturing. That is how long it takes to break an entrenched position in a market where the buyer has to qualify, validate and support every part it deploys.

## What the accounts look like now

AMD reported record revenue of 34.6 billion US dollars for the full year 2025, with GAAP gross margin of fifty per cent, operating income of 3.7 billion, net income of 4.3 billion and diluted earnings per share of 2.65 dollars. On a non-GAAP basis the same year produced gross margin of fifty-two per cent, operating income of 7.8 billion, net income of 6.8 billion and diluted earnings per share of 4.17 dollars. The fourth quarter alone produced record revenue of 10.3 billion at a fifty-four per cent GAAP gross margin. All of this is from the company's results announcement of 3 February 2026.

For the second quarter of 2026, announced on 4 August 2026, revenue was a record 11.5 billion dollars, gross margin fifty-four per cent GAAP and fifty-six per cent non-GAAP, operating income 2.0 billion and net income 2.3 billion. Data centre segment revenue was 6.7 billion, up a hundred and seven per cent year on year and fifty-eight per cent of company revenue. Guidance for the third quarter was approximately 13 billion dollars plus or minus 300 million, roughly forty-one per cent year-on-year growth at the midpoint.

Now put that beside the company one floor up in the same market.

| Full year GAAP gross margin | 50 per cent | 71.1 per cent |

That table is what second place costs, expressed as a line item. Roughly twenty points of gross margin. On AMD's 2025 revenue, twenty points is about seven billion dollars a year of gross profit the second source does not collect — because a second source cannot price like a monopolist, and because it carries the cost of persuading each customer to make the change.

One further feature of AMD's accounts recurs across this industry. In 2025 GAAP operating income was 3.7 billion and non-GAAP operating income was 7.8 billion. That gap is not a presentational quibble; more than half the operating profit on the adjusted measure is absent from the audited one. The usual constituents are amortisation of intangible assets recognised on acquisitions, and share-based compensation. Both are real costs: the first is the unwinding of a purchase price that was actually paid, the second is remuneration that was actually delivered, in a currency that dilutes existing owners. When a company that has made large acquisitions asks you to look at its non-GAAP figures, look at both, and note which one the management incentive plan is measured against.

## The acquisitions, second time round

The Xilinx transaction was announced on 27 October 2020, valued at about 35 billion dollars, and completed on 14 February 2022 with total purchase consideration of 48.8 billion dollars, or 46.4 billion net of 2.4 billion of cash acquired.

The gap between those two numbers is the lesson. The deal was all-stock. Between announcement and completion both companies' shares rose — AMD's from around seventy-five dollars to about a hundred and fourteen, Xilinx's from just over a hundred and fifteen to a hundred and ninety-four, per contemporaneous coverage of the closing — so the price paid, measured at completion, was some forty per cent above the price announced.

Compare the two acquisitions. In 2006 AMD bought ATI with borrowed cash and impaired the goodwill fifteen months later. In 2020 it agreed to buy Xilinx with its own shares, when those shares were highly valued, and the obligation adjusted upward with the market rather than sitting fixed on the balance sheet as debt. All-stock consideration transfers risk to the seller's shareholders and leaves the buyer's balance sheet intact. It also dilutes. Which matters more depends on whether the buyer's shares were expensive at the time, and nobody knows that until afterwards.

The third acquisition completes the pattern. AMD agreed in August 2024 to buy ZT Systems, a builder of hyperscale server systems, for approximately 4.9 billion dollars in cash and stock, saying at the time it would seek a partner for the manufacturing operations. Sanmina completed the purchase of that manufacturing business on 27 October 2025 for three billion dollars in cash and stock including a contingent payment of up to 450 million, becoming a preferred manufacturing partner. AMD bought the engineering capability to design rack-scale systems and sold the factories that build them: the doctrine of 2009, applied to a different part of the chain.

## The accelerator business, and the moat made of software

The Instinct MI300 series launched on 6 December 2023. It crossed a billion dollars of revenue in a single quarter within nine months of launch, and AMD reported more than five billion dollars of data centre GPU revenue for 2024. That is the fastest ramp of any product in the company's history and, on any reasonable reading, the first AMD accelerator treated by customers as a genuine alternative rather than a specialist part.

It also ran straight into the export control regime. In mid-April 2025 the US Commerce Department imposed licensing requirements that made the China-specific MI308 ineligible for export without a licence. AMD said it expected an 800 million dollar charge, reported by CNBC on 16 April 2025; the charge was taken against inventory and related items and reduced non-GAAP gross margin by ten percentage points, to forty-three per cent, in the second quarter of 2025, with the company indicating 1.5 to 1.8 billion dollars of lost revenue for the year. Licences were subsequently granted for certain customers, and in the fourth quarter of 2025 AMD began shipping again and reversed approximately 360 million dollars of those charges, per the company's 2025 annual reporting.

That sequence is a small tutorial in how inventory works. Inventory is carried at the lower of cost and net realisable value. When a political decision makes a product unsellable, its realisable value collapses and the carrying amount is written down through cost of sales. Gross margin in that quarter is crushed — ten points, here. If the product later becomes sellable again, the inventory that survived is carried at its written-down value, so the eventual sale carries little or no cost against it and the margin in the quarter of sale is flattered. Nothing improper is happening. But a reader comparing the two quarters without knowing about the charge will conclude that the business deteriorated violently and then recovered violently, and neither is true.

Now the strategic question. In server processors AMD proved a second source can take an entrenched position, because the switching cost was mostly qualification and support. In accelerators the switching cost is different in kind. The leader's advantage is not only the chip. It is the accumulated body of software — libraries, kernels, tooling, the collective experience of every engineer who has debugged a training run — written against one vendor's programming model over more than fifteen years. AMD's equivalent stack, ROCm, has to be good enough that a customer's existing code runs at competitive speed without a team spending six months on the port. Frameworks abstract some of this away. They do not abstract away performance, and performance is what the customer is buying.

You do not have to take a view on whether that gap has closed. You can read what the parties have agreed to do about it.

On 5 and 6 October 2025 AMD and OpenAI announced a partnership to deploy six gigawatts of AMD accelerators, beginning with one gigawatt of MI450 series parts in the second half of 2026. Alongside it, AMD issued OpenAI a warrant over up to 160 million shares at an exercise price of one cent — approximately 9.9 per cent of AMD's outstanding shares if fully exercised — vesting in tranches tied to purchase milestones running to the full six gigawatts, and additionally subject to share price thresholds escalating to six hundred dollars for the final tranche.

On 22 July 2026 AMD and Anthropic announced a partnership to deploy up to two gigawatts of MI450 series accelerators in AMD's rack-scale Helios systems, the first gigawatt from the first half of 2027, together with an AMD commitment to a strategic equity investment of up to five billion dollars in Anthropic and a multi-year engineering collaboration in which Anthropic's models are used to accelerate AMD's own software development, as reported by CNBC on 22 July 2026 and in the companies' announcements.

Read those two agreements as an accountant reads them, not as a press office writes them.

A warrant issued to a customer is not marketing. Under revenue accounting rules, consideration given by a seller to its customer is generally treated as a reduction of the transaction price, recognised as the related revenue is recognised, rather than as an ordinary expense. When a vendor gives equity to a buyer to secure a purchase commitment, the accounting is designed to show that the vendor is, in substance, selling at a discount. The exact treatment and amounts are set out in AMD's filings and should be read there rather than assumed here. The principle is what matters for judging the strategy: an agreement of this shape means the second source is paying, in dilution, for the right to be adopted. That is what a challenger facing a software-based moat has to do, and what a company with pricing power never needs to do.

The Anthropic agreement says the same in a different currency. An equity investment in the customer, plus an engineering collaboration in which the customer's technology is used to improve the vendor's software stack, is an acknowledgement that the software stack is what stands between the hardware and the sale.

None of this is an allegation. These are disclosed, negotiated arrangements between sophisticated parties, described openly in the announcements. The point is narrower: the structure of a deal tells you where the bargaining power sits, and six gigawatts bought with a warrant over a tenth of the company is not the deal a market leader signs.

A warning about ranking tables while we are here. Compilations published earlier in 2026 put AMD's market capitalisation at roughly 260 billion dollars, per statista and alpha-sense rankings, and that figure and the August one cannot be reconciled by any year-to-date move. Ranking tables are assembled at different moments, from different price sources, and are sometimes republished long after the prices inside them were current. Take a market capitalisation from a tracker that puts a date on it, and check it yourself; every figure in this section will be wrong by the time you read it.

The valuation attached to all this was not modest. Commentary published on 3 August 2026, ahead of the second-quarter results, described AMD as trading at fifty-four times forward earnings. This book does not tell you whether that is too much. It has no price target for AMD, no recommendation on the shares and no view on where they go. What it will do is name the published numbers that decide the question.

Watch the data centre segment against its guidance rather than against last year, because a hundred and seven per cent growth rate cannot persist and the interesting moment is the one when it stops. Watch gross margin: AMD does not break out margin on Instinct accelerators separately, so mix is inferred rather than read, and any commentary claiming to know accelerator margins precisely is guessing. Watch the customer concentration disclosures, since a business growing on a handful of gigawatt-scale commitments is by construction concentrated. Watch how the warrants and equity investments are presented, and where they land in the income statement. And watch inventory and purchase commitments, since a fabless company's obligations to its foundry and its memory suppliers are in the notes, not on the balance sheet.

## The lesson

AMD's recovery is often told as a story about a chip design, which it partly is. Read through the accounts, it is a story about sequence. The balance sheet was repaired first — buildings, packaging plants and foundry terms converted into cash and flexibility, at a disclosed cost of hundreds of millions — because only a company that has stopped worrying about next year's cash can spend four years developing a product for the year after next. The architecture came second. The manufacturing freedom came third, and without it the architecture would not have mattered. The market share came last, a full nine years after the product launched.

That sequence is the transferable part. The rest is a warning about what second place buys. Being the credible alternative is a genuine and profitable position: thirty-four and a half billion dollars of revenue in 2025 and a record quarter in the middle of 2026 are not consolation prizes. It is also a position earning roughly twenty points less gross margin than the leader, obliged to give away equity to be adopted in the newest market, and slow — the better part of a decade to convert a good product into a share of the money.

Whether the same can be done again, in accelerators, against a moat written in software rather than silicon, is the open question at the bottom of the stack. The chapters that follow take up the other companies asking it.
