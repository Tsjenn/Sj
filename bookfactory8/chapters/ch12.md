# Nvidia I: The Graphics Years, 1993 to 2006

Three engineers met in a Denny's on the eastern side of San Jose in 1993 and agreed to start a chip company. Between them they had about forty thousand US dollars of starting capital, according to Quartr's 2024 account of the founding, no product, no customer, and — a detail that would matter more than any other — no factory.

The company was incorporated in April 1993. Jensen Huang, then working at LSI Logic, became chief executive and has never held any other job at the company. Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem came from Sun Microsystems, where they had built graphics hardware for workstations. The plan was narrow and, at the time, unfashionable: build a chip that would make three-dimensional graphics cheap enough for a personal computer that a household would actually buy.

What follows is that company's first thirteen years, told as far as possible without the benefit of knowing what happened next. This is difficult and worth the effort. Read backwards from 2026, every decision Nvidia made between 1993 and 2006 looks like foresight. Read forwards, most of them look like a small company doing the only thing available to it, and getting several important calls wrong before it got one right.

## Who actually wrote the first cheques

Start with a correction, because this book will use it again.

Nvidia's early venture backers were Sequoia Capital and Sutter Hill Ventures. They were not SoftBank. This is worth stating in plain language because the error is common enough in popular accounts of the AI boom that it has become a kind of folk fact, and it garbles the corporate history of two separate companies.

The introduction came through the industry rather than through a pitch process. Sequoia's own history of the company, published in its 2023 Crucible Moments series, describes Wilfred Corrigan — the head of LSI Logic, where Huang was then working — telephoning Don Valentine at Sequoia on Huang's behalf. Mark Stevens, then a Sequoia partner, joined the board in 1993 and would sit on it for decades.

The size of that first round is one of the places where a careful reader should notice that the record is thin. Widely repeated accounts put the initial venture funding at around twenty million US dollars, led by Sequoia with Sutter Hill alongside; other accounts, closer to Sequoia's own telling, describe a first cheque nearer two million dollars. Both cannot be describing the same event. Nvidia was a private company in 1993, the terms were not filed with any regulator at the time, and nobody with first-hand knowledge has published the round documents. The honest position is that the identity of the backers is well established and the amount is not. This book will therefore use the names and leave the number alone.

The SoftBank confusion has a real origin, and it is worth untangling once so the later chapters do not have to. SoftBank acquired Arm in 2016 and returned it to the public markets in 2023. SoftBank's Vision Fund built a stake of roughly 4.9 per cent in Nvidia during 2017 and sold it in January and February 2019 for about 3.3 billion US dollars, a profit of roughly 2.6 billion, as reported by CNBC in February 2019. SoftBank sold its entire remaining Nvidia holding in November 2025 for 5.83 billion US dollars, to fund investment in OpenAI and the Stargate programme, again per CNBC. Those are three genuine and consequential transactions. None of them is a founding investment, and the earliest of them came twenty-four years after the Denny's.

The distinction is not pedantry. A founding investor buys into a company that has nothing, holds through everything, and is compensated for that risk with ownership. A public-market buyer in 2017 bought a business that was already listed, already profitable and already the leader in its niche, and was compensated for a much smaller risk with a much smaller multiple. Confusing the two makes the returns of venture capital look ordinary and the returns of a hedge position look miraculous. It is the same error as confusing a subscription with a signing bonus, and it recurs throughout this industry's popular history.

## The first product was wrong

Nvidia's first chip, the NV1, was launched in May 1995 and sold at retail as the Diamond Edge 3D by Diamond Multimedia. It was manufactured by SGS-Thomson. Nvidia has never owned a fabrication plant — a structural fact rather than a figure, and one that shapes every paragraph of this chapter.

The NV1 was an ambitious part. It combined two-dimensional graphics, three-dimensional acceleration, video and audio on a single chip, which was a sensible response to a market where a consumer PC had few expansion slots and a household had a fixed budget.

Its three-dimensional geometry was built around quadrilaterals rather than triangles. The reasoning was defensible. Curved surfaces can be approximated with fewer quadrilaterals than triangles, and the Sega Saturn console, which Nvidia hoped to draw games from, also worked in quads. Then Microsoft announced that DirectX would support triangles as the primitive, and the argument ended. Not because triangles were better in some abstract sense, but because the standard decides, and Microsoft owned the standard.

The commercial consequence was total. Game developers had no reason to write for an architecture that one card supported and the platform did not. Nvidia, Diamond and SGS-Thomson could not persuade them otherwise. The NV1 sold poorly, and much of what shipped came back through the retail channel. Nvidia has never disclosed a unit number for the NV1 that this book can verify; figures circulating in later write-ups vary widely and none carries an auditable source, so none is repeated here.

| The NV1's design choice | Why it looked reasonable in 1994 | Why it was fatal by 1996 |
|---|---|---|
| Quadrilaterals as the geometry primitive | Fewer primitives for curved surfaces; matched the Sega Saturn | DirectX standardised on triangles; developers followed the standard |
| Graphics, video and audio on one chip | One slot, one purchase, one bill of materials | Tied the graphics business to unrelated features nobody demanded |
| Ports from a console as the software supply | Instant catalogue of titles | Consoles and PCs diverged; the catalogue never arrived |

There is a general lesson in this that applies to every layer of the stack, and it is the reason the story belongs in a book about accounts rather than a book about engineering. A component maker does not sell to a market. It sells into a standard. When the standard moves, the inventory on the balance sheet does not become slightly less valuable. It becomes scrap, and the write-down lands in a single quarter.

Consider an illustrative fabless start-up, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. It commits to a mask set and a wafer start, capitalises the resulting finished goods at ten million dollars, and ships eight million of that into distributors. If it recognises revenue on shipment into the channel, its first year looks like a business. If the product does not sell through, the distributor returns it, the eight million reverses, and the ten million of inventory is written to whatever the scrap value is. The company's reported revenue in year one was never real. It was a loan from the channel, repaid in year two with interest in the form of a write-down. This is why a reader of a hardware company's accounts checks the revenue recognition policy and the inventory provisions before admiring the growth rate.

## The year the company nearly ended

By 1996 Nvidia was three years old, its first product had failed, and its remaining commercial hope was a contract with Sega to design the graphics chip for the console that would eventually become the Dreamcast.

Huang's account of what happened next, given in interviews and reported by CNBC in March 2024, is unusually clean. Finishing the Sega contract would have brought in money. It would also have consumed the year in which the rest of the industry was moving to triangles, leaving Nvidia with cash and an obsolete architecture. He told Sega the company could not do the work, and asked to be paid anyway.

Two things then happened that are hard to fit into any theory of corporate strategy. Sega's president, Shoichiro Irimajiri, secured a five million dollar investment in Nvidia, as reported by Tom's Hardware in 2024 — a payment to a supplier that had just declined the job. And Huang cut the company from about a hundred employees to fewer than forty, a reduction of more than sixty per cent, and pointed what remained at a single triangle-based chip.

It is worth pausing on the arithmetic that a decision like that rests on, because it is the same arithmetic every capital-hungry company in this book performs, only with more zeros. Take an illustrative company, again with invented round numbers belonging to nobody: a hundred employees at a fully loaded cost of ten thousand dollars a month each is twelve million dollars a year of payroll. Cut to forty and the annual burn falls to under five million. Add five million of unexpected cash and a runway of a few months becomes a runway of a year. A year is not a strategy. It is just enough time for exactly one more product to be right.

That is the position Nvidia occupied when it began the chip that would be called the RIVA 128. Every remaining resource went into it. There was no second programme, no hedge, and no plan for the case where it did not work.

## Betting the company on a simulator

The RIVA 128 shipped in August 1997. What makes it worth a section rather than a sentence is how it was committed to production.

Fabless semiconductor economics impose a specific and unpleasant order of operations. You finalise a design. You pay for a mask set. You buy wafer starts. Only then, months later, do you hold a chip and find out whether it works. If it does not, you pay for the masks again and lose two quarters. A company with a year of cash cannot afford a second attempt.

Nvidia therefore verified the design in emulation and ordered volume production of a chip nobody had physically held. Huang has described the company as having had roughly six months of cash when that decision was made, and accounts of the period — reported by Yahoo Finance and Moneywise in retrospectives published in 2024 and 2025 — put Nvidia at about one month of payroll by the time the part shipped.

It worked. The RIVA 128 sold more than a million units in its first four months, per those same accounts. In accounting terms, what a result like that does to a small company is not primarily a profit event. It is a working-capital event. Revenue on that scale converts a business that must beg for cash into a business that can pre-pay for wafers, which secures allocation at the foundry, which secures the next product. Scale in this industry is not a reward for winning. It is the mechanism by which winning is repeated.

## What the first accounts looked like

Nvidia's early published results are short enough to read in a single sitting, and they show a shape that recurs at every scale the company has since operated at.

| Financial year ended | Revenue | Net income | Source as cited |
|---|---|---|---|
| 31 January 1999 | 158.2 million US dollars | 4.1 million US dollars | The Register, reporting the results, February 1999 |
| 30 January 2000 | 374.5 million US dollars | about 41 million US dollars | Company annual results for the year, as compiled in published company histories |
| 28 January 2001 | 735.3 million US dollars | 98.5 million US dollars | Company annual results for the year, as compiled in published company histories |
| 27 January 2002 | about 1.37 billion US dollars | not verified for this book | Company annual report for the year |
| 28 January 2007 | 3.07 billion US dollars | not verified for this book | Comparative figure in the company's results announcement of February 2008 |

Three points about that table, in ascending order of importance.

First, the comparatives. The Register's February 1999 report gives the prior year against that 158.2 million as 13.3 million of revenue and 1.3 million of net income. Other reference works describing the same period present the early years differently, and this book does not have a source it can rely on to reconcile them. Where a figure cannot be reconciled, the correct treatment is to say so rather than to pick the more impressive version.

Second, the fiscal-year trap, which will cost a careless reader more accuracy than any other single habit. Nvidia's financial year ends in late January. The year ended 30 January 2000 therefore covers almost all of calendar 1999. Secondary sources routinely relabel these years by the calendar year they mostly cover, so the same 374.5 million dollars appears in print as "1999" in one place and "fiscal 2000" in another. Both are defensible; using them in the same paragraph is not. Whenever this book gives a Nvidia annual figure, it gives the date the year ended.

Third, the shape. Revenue roughly doubled, then doubled again, then nearly doubled once more, while net income grew from a rounding error to something an investor could value. A company that goes from four million dollars of profit to ninety-eight million in two years has not become more efficient. It has found a product with a fixed development cost and a variable cost that falls with volume, and then sold a great many of them. That is the whole economics of a fabless chip company in one sentence, and the reason chapter three treats gross margin as the first number to look at rather than the last.

## The IPO, and what forty-two million dollars used to buy

Nvidia listed on Nasdaq on 22 January 1999 at twelve dollars a share, offering 3.5 million shares and raising about forty-two million US dollars, according to retrospectives published by the Motley Fool and Nasdaq between 2024 and 2026.

Set that against the company's position as at mid-August 2026, when Nvidia was the world's most valuable company at roughly 5.3 to 5.4 trillion US dollars on the companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings. The comparison is arresting and almost entirely useless as analysis, so treat it carefully. A market capitalisation twenty-seven years apart compares two different companies in two different currencies of expectation, and the intervening period contains stock splits, dilution, buybacks and a change in what the business sells. What the 1999 figure is genuinely good for is calibration of scale: the entire proceeds of Nvidia's initial public offering would today not pay for a single one of the most advanced lithography machines described in chapter ten.

The other thing worth noticing about a forty-two million dollar raise is what it says about the graphics industry in 1999. This was a crowded, low-margin, fast-cycling business with perhaps a dozen serious competitors, in which chips became worthless in eighteen months and the dominant platform owner could change the rules by press release. Nobody was paying a scarcity premium for a graphics chip designer, because graphics chip designers were not scarce.

## Naming the thing

Nvidia announced the GeForce 256 on 31 August 1999 and released it on 11 October 1999, per the twenty-fifth anniversary accounts published by VideoCardz and TweakTown in 2024. It was marketed as the world's first GPU — graphics processing unit.

The technical basis of the claim was the integration of hardware transform and lighting: the geometry stage of the graphics pipeline, previously computed on the main processor, moved onto the graphics chip. That is a genuine change of kind rather than degree. It means the graphics part is no longer an accelerator that the processor feeds. It is a processor that does its own work.

The claim to have invented the category was less clean, and the anniversary coverage says so: the phrase "graphics processing unit" appears in the literature well before 1999. What Nvidia did was attach the term to a specific consumer product, in a marketing campaign large enough that the word stuck. This is a marketing achievement, and this book has no interest in either inflating or sneering at it. It is simply worth recognising as a distinct kind of corporate asset. A company that names a category is quoted every time anyone discusses the category. When the same three letters later turn up on the invoices of every artificial-intelligence data centre on earth, the naming looks like foresight; in 1999 it was closer to a good afternoon in the marketing department.

The strategic content of the GeForce era was elsewhere, in an unglamorous decision about release cadence. Nvidia set out to ship a new architecture on an annual rhythm, in a business where competitors typically took longer. A faster cadence is brutal on engineering and brutal on the balance sheet, since each generation must earn back its development cost before the next one obsoletes it. It also compounds. A competitor who is six months behind on one generation is a year behind after two, and the market notices the gap long before the engineering catches up.

## Microsoft's two hundred million dollars

In March 2000 Nvidia signed an agreement with Microsoft to supply the graphics chip for the first Xbox console. The detail that belongs in a book about accounts is what happened in April 2000: Microsoft paid Nvidia a two hundred million dollar advance against future chip purchases, per the disclosure in Nvidia's annual report for the financial year ended 28 January 2001.

Put that next to the revenue table. This single customer prepayment was larger than the company's entire net income for that year and a substantial fraction of the revenue line. For a business that had been a month from missing payroll three years earlier, it was transformative — and it is exactly the kind of item that a reader skimming the income statement will not see at all, because a customer advance is not revenue. It is cash received with an obligation attached, and it sits on the liabilities side of the balance sheet until the chips are delivered.

The same disclosure records what was attached to it. Microsoft could terminate the arrangement, and if it did so before the advance had been fully worked off, Nvidia would have to return up to a hundred million dollars and convert the remainder into preferred stock at a thirty per cent premium. That clause tells you the true balance of power in the relationship more precisely than any press release did.

Here is the mechanism, using an illustrative company and round numbers belonging to nobody:

| Event | Cash | Balance sheet | Income statement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer prepays 200 for future chips | +200 | Deferred revenue or customer advance +200 | Nothing |
| Chips delivered, 50 of the advance worked off | No movement | Liability falls to 150 | Revenue +50, with cost of sales against it |
| Programme cancelled with 150 unworked | Possible repayment | Liability settled in cash and equity | Nothing, unless terms change |

Three consequences follow, and they generalise to every large supply relationship in this book, including the ones signed in 2026.

Cash flow and profit tell different stories, and for a while they tell opposite ones. A company can be flush with a customer's money and unprofitable at the same time. It can also be highly profitable and short of cash. Chapter thirty-two returns to this at data-centre scale.

Customer concentration is a real risk even when the customer is paying early. Especially then. A supplier whose largest customer has prepaid has a customer with a claim, not merely an order.

And the console business itself was a specific bargain: high volume, a long design cycle, a fixed price that falls over the life of the platform, and a single buyer with all of the negotiating power. It is a good business to have and a dangerous one to depend on.

## Enron's seat

Two events in the following eighteen months mark the end of Nvidia's period as a small company.

In December 2000 Nvidia agreed to buy the principal assets of 3dfx Interactive — the intellectual property, the chip inventory, and the Voodoo and 3dfx trademarks — for seventy million dollars in cash and one million Nvidia shares, with a fifteen million dollar working-capital loan advanced on signing and credited against the price, as reported by CNN.com in December 2000. 3dfx had been the dominant name in consumer three-dimensional graphics only a few years earlier. It had made an expensive strategic move into manufacturing its own boards, competing with the customers who had been distributing its chips, and it had not survived the consequences.

Then, after the close of trading on 29 November 2001, Nvidia joined the S&P 500, replacing Enron. The Register reported the change the following day and put Nvidia's market capitalisation at that point at about 7.8 billion dollars.

There is no moral to be drawn from which company was leaving the index, and this book will not attempt one. What is worth taking from the pair of events is that the graphics industry had consolidated hard and fast. A dozen credible competitors in the mid-nineties had become, by the early 2000s, essentially two: Nvidia and ATI, with Intel supplying integrated graphics that were adequate for people who did not care. Consolidation of that kind is the normal end state of a business with high fixed development costs and low marginal costs, and it is the same force that later leaves one company printing the finest features on a wafer and one company making the machines that print them.

## The middle years

The period from 2002 to 2005 does not lend itself to a clean narrative, which is itself informative.

Nvidia and ATI traded the performance lead roughly every generation. Nvidia entered and then exited the chipset business. There were disputes about benchmark behaviour, a graphics-industry pastime of the era. The GeForce FX generation was widely judged to have lost a round to ATI on the features that mattered for the then-current version of DirectX, and Nvidia recovered with the generation after it. The company won the graphics contract for one console and lost another to its competitor, then won one back.

Revenue for the year ended 28 January 2007 was 3.07 billion US dollars, per the comparative given in the company's results announcement of February 2008. Compare that with the 1.37 billion of the year ended 27 January 2002. Over five years spanning the end of one console cycle and the start of another, the business roughly doubled. That is a good outcome for a component supplier and an ordinary one for a technology company of the period.

The point of stating it flatly is that a reader who arrives at Nvidia's story from 2026 expects the graphs to bend upward continuously. They did not. This was a competent, cyclical, mid-sized semiconductor company with a strong position in a market of finite size, competing hard against one peer, and dependent on consumers replacing graphics cards to play games. On any measure available at the time, that is what it was.

## A chip that was also a computer

In November 2006 Nvidia launched the GeForce 8800 GTX, built on the architecture known internally as G80.

The change beneath the product name was structural. Earlier graphics chips had separate, specialised hardware for the different stages of the pipeline — one set of units for geometry, another for shading pixels. G80 replaced them with a single pool of a hundred and twenty-eight programmable processors that could be assigned to whichever stage needed them, as described in contemporaneous reviews including PC Perspective's of November 2006 and in Jon Peddie Research's later histories of the period.

For a graphics customer, the benefit was efficiency. Work no longer piled up behind whichever fixed-function unit happened to be the bottleneck.

For anyone else, something stranger had happened. A pool of a hundred and twenty-eight identical programmable processors, all able to run the same instruction over different data at the same time, is not really a graphics device. It is a small parallel computer that happens to be sold in a box with a picture of a spaceship on it. The only obstacle to using it as one was that programming it required expressing your problem as a graphics operation — pretending your data was a texture, your calculation a shading operation, and your answer an image.

Alongside G80, Nvidia released CUDA, which removed that obstacle. It let a programmer write something close to ordinary C, targeted at the parallel processors directly, without any pretence about graphics.

At the time, this was a cost. Supporting general-purpose programming consumed die area, which is to say it consumed gross margin on every chip sold, in service of capability that no paying customer had asked for. It required a permanent software organisation — compilers, libraries, drivers, documentation, developer support — funded out of the research and development line of a company whose revenue came from selling graphics cards to people who played games. In a business where a competitor could spend the same money making the next generation faster, that is not an obviously wise allocation.

Nobody at the launch described CUDA as the most important product decision of the era. The reviews of the day covered it briefly and returned to frame rates, which was a reasonable editorial judgement about what readers had paid to find out. The graphics business in 2006 was contested, cyclical and roughly two-horse, and the year's important question was which company had the faster part for the coming version of DirectX.

## What the first act was actually about

Thirteen years, on the evidence available at the end of 2006, produced a company that had been wrong about its first product, nearly out of business at three years old, rescued by a customer it had just disappointed, saved by a chip verified in simulation because it could not afford to be wrong twice, listed for forty-two million dollars, given a category name by its own marketing, prepaid two hundred million dollars by a customer that could have demanded half of it back, and consolidated into one of two survivors in a market that had been crowded a decade earlier.

None of that is a story about artificial intelligence, and this chapter has deliberately not made it one. The behaviours worth carrying forward are narrower and more boring than foresight: a willingness to abandon a funded contract when the architecture underneath it was wrong, a release cadence maintained through the years when it was expensive, and a habit of spending money on capability before there was a customer for it.

That last habit is the subject of the next chapter, and it is the one that turns out to matter. The hardware in 2006 was a parallel computer with no parallel-computing customers. The software released alongside it made that computer usable by anyone who could write C. What happened over the following decade is that a generation of researchers, looking for cheap parallel computation for reasons entirely unconnected to graphics, found the tooling already built, already documented and already sitting in a machine they could buy at retail.
