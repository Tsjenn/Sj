# Stargate and the Megaproject Era

On the twenty-first of January 2025, the day after a presidential inauguration, three executives stood at the White House and announced a company. It would be called Stargate, it would build computing infrastructure for OpenAI in the United States, and it intended to spend five hundred billion US dollars over four years, with one hundred billion described as deploying immediately, per the joint announcement by OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle and MGX in January 2025. The initial equity funders were those four names. SoftBank took financial responsibility and its founder Masayoshi Son took the chairmanship; OpenAI took operational responsibility; Arm, Microsoft, Nvidia and Oracle were named as key initial technology partners, all per the same announcement.

Five hundred billion dollars is not a figure that belongs to the vocabulary of corporate capital spending. It is roughly ten years of TSMC's capital expenditure at the pace the foundry guided for 2026 — fifty-two to fifty-six billion dollars, per its January 2026 results. It is comfortably more than the annual capital spending of the four largest cloud buyers combined, which was approaching seven hundred billion dollars for 2026 as a group, as reported by CNBC in February 2026 — and that seven hundred billion was itself a record that had roughly doubled in two years. One project, announced from a podium, claiming a sum in that class: that is the moment the old vocabulary broke. Data centres used to be measured in megawatts and disclosed in footnotes. From 2025 they were measured in gigawatts — a gigawatt being of the order of the output of a large power station — and announced beside heads of state.

This chapter is about what such an announcement actually is. Not the theatre, but the structure: what kind of entity a Stargate is, who signs which contract, who bears which risk, why the money comes from a consortium rather than from any single balance sheet, and — the discipline this book keeps returning to — how much of the announced number had become physical reality by the time of writing, as opposed to remaining a press release. The distance between those two states is where every judgement about this era will be settled.

## What was actually announced

Strip the ceremony away and the January 2025 announcement described a joint venture. Stargate was presented as a new company with four equity funders — SoftBank, OpenAI, Oracle and MGX, the Abu Dhabi investment firm — and a division of labour: SoftBank responsible for the finance, OpenAI responsible for operating the capacity, per the announcement of January 2025. The five hundred billion was an intention, not a contract. The one hundred billion was a commitment described as immediate. Neither figure was a sum of money sitting in an account; both were targets for what the venture, its partners and its lenders would assemble over time.

That distinction matters more here than almost anywhere else in this book, because the gap between an announced intention and a funded, contracted, energised data centre is measured in years and in tens of billions of dollars. So it is worth being precise about the vocabulary this chapter will use. A project is *announced* when executives say it will happen. It is *financed* when lenders and equity investors have signed. It is *contracted* when a customer is legally obliged to pay for the output. It is *under construction* when earth is moving. It is *energised* when the utility delivers power and chips draw it. Press releases blur these five states on purpose. Accounts cannot: financing shows up as debt and equity, contracts show up as obligations and backlog, construction shows up as capital work in progress, and energisation shows up, at last, as revenue and depreciation. One useful habit for reading this whole era: every gigawatt you see in a headline is in one of those five states, and the headline will rarely tell you which.

## The structure: nobody owns the whole thing

The first Stargate site makes the structure concrete. It sits on 875 acres outside Abilene, Texas — a campus planned at eight buildings and 1.2 gigawatts of power, developed by a company called Crusoe, per 2025 trade reporting on the project. The first phase was up and running on Oracle Cloud Infrastructure by September 2025, as reported by CNBC in September 2025.

Now look at who actually owns and owes what at Abilene, because it is nothing like one company building a factory.

The buildings belong to a joint venture. Crusoe, the developer, together with funds managed by Blue Owl Capital's real-assets platform and Primary Digital Infrastructure, jointly sponsored the construction, in a venture that reached fifteen billion dollars across two phases, per Crusoe's announcements in 2025. An earlier phase of that financing — 11.6 billion dollars in debt and equity — was reported by Data Center Dynamics in 2025, with most of the debt coming from JPMorgan through two loans totalling about 9.6 billion dollars, per the same reporting.

The joint venture does not sell computing. It is a landlord. Oracle leases the site for fifteen years and supplies the hardware inside it, per 2025 reporting on the transaction, and the Financial Times reported in 2025 that Oracle was to spend as much as forty billion dollars on Nvidia GB200 systems for the site. Oracle, in turn, sells the computing to OpenAI as cloud capacity. And OpenAI's obligation to Oracle is very large indeed: the Wall Street Journal reported in September 2025 that OpenAI had agreed to purchase around three hundred billion dollars of computing power from Oracle over roughly five years, with purchases beginning in 2027. Oracle's own disclosure gestures at the same scale from the other side: the company reported remaining performance obligations of 455 billion dollars at the end of its first quarter of fiscal 2026, per its September 2025 results, without itemising which customers stand behind the total.

So a single campus in Texas involves, at minimum: a developer who found the land and the power; an infrastructure fund and a bank who financed the shell; a cloud company that leases the shell for fifteen years and fills it with chips; a chip supplier that allocated the hardware; and a model company that has promised to rent the result for years into the future. Each party holds one slice. Nobody holds the whole. The project exists only as the sum of the contracts between them.

Once you see that pattern, you see it everywhere in the megaproject era, because it is not an accident. It is the design. The question is why.

## Who bears which risk

An accountant's way into any structure is to ask where each risk comes to rest, and in which set of accounts it will surface when it goes wrong. Run that exercise on the Abilene pattern and the logic of the whole arrangement becomes visible.

| Risk | Who carries it | Where it would surface |
|---|---|---|
| Construction cost and delay | The developer joint venture and its lenders | Capitalised project costs; loan covenants; completion dates slipping |
| Residual value of the buildings | The landlord venture, cushioned by a fifteen-year lease | Impairment of the asset if the tenant fails or the lease is not renewed |
| Obsolescence of the chips | Whoever owns the hardware — Oracle, per the 2025 reporting on Abilene | The depreciation life assumed for the equipment, and any later shortening of it |
| The customer's ability to pay | Oracle, on OpenAI's multi-year purchase commitments | Backlog that converts into revenue on schedule — or does not |
| Demand for AI compute itself | OpenAI and its equity investors | The terms of the next funding round of a company with no audited public accounts |
| Power price and availability | The developer and operator, over decades | Cost of revenue, long after the opening ceremony |

Three things stand out from that table.

First, the risks have been sorted by duration and placed with the parties best shaped to hold them. Buildings and power contracts are long-lived, boring assets; they sit with infrastructure capital that wants exactly that — a fifteen-year lease from a large listed tenant is the kind of cash flow a real-assets fund exists to own. Chips are short-lived, volatile assets; they sit with the cloud operator, whose entire business is sweating hardware inside a depreciation window. Demand risk — the possibility that the world wants less of this than everyone hopes — sits at the top, with the venture equity in the model company, which is the capital that is actually paid to take that kind of risk.

Second, the credit chain transforms one risk into another as it moves. OpenAI is a private company; its accounts are not audited or filed, a point chapter five made at length. A lender would struggle to finance a building against OpenAI's promise alone. But route the promise through Oracle — a listed company with decades of filings — and the same underlying demand becomes an investment-grade tenant's lease obligation, which a bank will finance at scale. The structure launders nothing; every party can see the chain. What it does is translate a risk that infrastructure lenders cannot price into one they can. The translation has a cost, and the cost has a name: Oracle now stands in the middle, and capacity built against contracted future revenue is a bet on the customer's survival as much as on demand. Oracle's shareholders, not JPMorgan's depositors, are the ones underwriting the difference between OpenAI's promises and OpenAI's payments.

Third, notice which line has no natural owner: the chips. Buildings hold value for decades; leases run fifteen years; but the accelerators inside are on nobody's fifteen-year plan. Whoever owns them takes the risk that the next hardware generation strands the current one, and the size of that risk is set by a single accounting estimate — the useful life over which they are depreciated. Chapter thirty-two deals with that estimate on its own, because in the megaproject era it may be the most consequential judgement in the industry's accounts.

## Why a consortium and not a balance sheet

The obvious question about Stargate is why it needed to exist at all. Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet build enormous data centres from their own resources; nobody announces a joint venture with a bank to build a warehouse. Why does this project need four equity funders, an infrastructure fund, a syndicate of developers and a supplier making its own investment?

The first answer is arithmetic. Five hundred billion dollars over four years is beyond what any single balance sheet — including the largest in corporate history — would commit to a single programme. The four biggest cloud builders were guiding to something approaching seven hundred billion dollars of 2026 capital expenditure between them, per CNBC's February 2026 reporting, and that total was spread across every business each of them runs, in every country, and was already being described as a strain. A single company signing up for five hundred billion against the demand of, substantially, one customer would concentrate risk in a way no board and no auditor could bless. The scale itself forces syndication.

The second answer is more interesting: different pools of capital charge different prices, and the structure is built to let each dollar sit where it is cheapest. Infrastructure and private-credit money — the Blue Owl and JPMorgan layer — is abundant and cheap, but only for assets with long contracts and low variance. Corporate balance-sheet money — the Oracle layer — is more expensive, but can absorb technology and customer risk that lenders will not touch. Venture equity — the money invested in OpenAI itself, such as the 122 billion dollar round the company closed in March 2026 at an 852 billion dollar valuation, with Amazon reported as investing fifty billion dollars, per 2026 reporting by valueaddvc and Forbes — is the most expensive of all, and is the only kind that will finance raw demand risk. A megaproject stacks these three kinds of money in layers, each insulated from the risks above it by a contract. Finance a gigawatt entirely with venture equity and it is ruinously expensive; entirely with bank debt and no bank will sign. The consortium is not a diplomatic arrangement. It is a machine for matching each slice of risk to the cheapest capital that will hold it.

The third answer is allocation. Chips were the binding constraint through this period — high-bandwidth memory capacity, for one input, was reported as sold out through 2026 across the major suppliers, per industry analysis in 2026 — and a project of this size does not simply purchase its hardware; it negotiates for a share of what exists. That pulls the supplier inside the tent. In September 2025, Nvidia and OpenAI announced a letter of intent under which at least ten gigawatts of Nvidia systems would be deployed for OpenAI, with Nvidia intending to invest up to one hundred billion dollars in OpenAI progressively as each gigawatt is deployed, the first gigawatt on the Vera Rubin platform in the second half of 2026, per the companies' announcements of September 2025. The letter was non-binding, and both figures — the ten gigawatts and the hundred billion — described intentions, not signed obligations.

Pause on that arrangement, because it is a mechanism this book has flagged before and will flag again: the supplier investing in the customer whose purchases drive the supplier's revenue. There is nothing improper in it, and both companies disclosed it openly and prominently. But an accountant reads it with a particular checklist. When a vendor's capital helps fund a customer's purchases from that vendor, reported demand and financed demand become harder to tell apart, and the reader of the vendor's accounts should want to know how much of its revenue growth is, at one remove, funded by its own investments. The same caution applies up the stack: CNBC reported in August 2026 that Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic had begun distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them, as gains and losses on those stakes moved through their results. None of this is an allegation. It is a description of circularity, and circularity is precisely the condition under which everyone's numbers can look strong at the same time for the same underlying reason. Watch for disclosure of it; prefer companies that make the circle easy to trace.

## SoftBank's part, paid for in Nvidia shares

SoftBank's role deserves its own section, partly because the company holds the finance chair in the venture and partly because how it funded that position says a great deal about what megaproject capital actually is.

First, a correction this book has made before and will keep making, because popular accounts keep getting it wrong: SoftBank was not an early backer of Nvidia. Nvidia's backers at its founding in 1993 were Sequoia Capital and Sutter Hill Ventures. SoftBank's involvement came much later and was twice-ended. Its Vision Fund built a stake of roughly 4.9 per cent in Nvidia during 2017 and sold it in early 2019 for about 3.3 billion dollars, booking a profit of roughly 2.6 billion, as reported by CNBC in February 2019 — an exit made years before the AI boom repriced the company. SoftBank then rebuilt a position, and in November 2025 disclosed that it had sold its entire remaining Nvidia stake for 5.83 billion US dollars, explicitly to fund its investment in OpenAI and Stargate, as reported by CNBC in November 2025. CNBC's reporting that month, citing a person familiar with the matter, said the Nvidia proceeds — together with a partial sale of T-Mobile shares and a margin loan secured against SoftBank's holding in Arm, the chip designer it acquired in 2016 and returned to public markets in 2023 — would fund a 22.5 billion dollar investment in OpenAI.

Read that sequence as a balance-sheet story rather than a drama. SoftBank did not conjure new money for the megaproject era; it rotated out of one layer of the stack and into another. It sold its position in the silicon layer's most valuable company — twice, as it happens — to buy exposure to the model layer and to the compute buildout that connects them. Whether that rotation proves wise is exactly the kind of question this book does not answer and the reader should not expect anyone to answer honestly in advance; this book is not investment advice, and a portfolio shift of that size is a judgement, not a fact. What the sequence does establish, as fact, is the sheer hunger of the megaproject era for cash. When the partner holding financial responsibility for a five-hundred-billion-dollar intention needs to liquidate a Nvidia stake, trim T-Mobile and borrow against Arm to fund a twenty-odd-billion-dollar slice of it, you learn something about how far announced numbers run ahead of committed ones — and about why the structure needs so many other pockets.

## Announced versus built

Now the discipline. What, of all this, physically existed?

By the autumn of 2025 the project had multiplied on paper. In September 2025, OpenAI, Oracle and SoftBank announced five new US sites: Shackelford County and Milam County in Texas, Doña Ana County in New Mexico, Lordstown in Ohio, and a Midwest site later named, in an October 2025 update, as Wisconsin, to be developed by Oracle with Vantage, per the companies' announcements of September and October 2025. Three of the five were Oracle-led and two SoftBank-led, with the two SoftBank sites described as able to scale to 1.5 gigawatts within eighteen months, per the September 2025 announcement. Together with Abilene and projects with CoreWeave, the companies described Stargate as reaching nearly seven gigawatts of planned capacity and over four hundred billion dollars of investment over three years, per the same announcement. In October 2025 a further campus of more than a gigawatt was announced at Saline Township, Michigan, developed by Related Digital with Oracle, taking the stated totals to over eight gigawatts planned and more than 450 billion dollars, per the OpenAI and Related Digital announcements of October 2025. The brand had also gone abroad: in May 2025, G42, OpenAI, Oracle, Nvidia, SoftBank and Cisco announced Stargate UAE, a one-gigawatt cluster to be built by G42 and operated by OpenAI and Oracle within a planned five-gigawatt UAE–US AI campus in Abu Dhabi, with the first 200 megawatts expected live in 2026, per the partners' May 2025 announcements. By late 2025, 2025 reporting described Stargate as the umbrella for essentially all of OpenAI's data-centre efforts, which means the name had become a brand as much as a company — another reason to read each announcement's structure separately rather than assuming they share one.

Set against that paper, the physical record at the same date was one campus partly running and earth moving elsewhere. Abilene's first phase was operational on Oracle Cloud Infrastructure in September 2025, per CNBC that month, with the campus planned at eight buildings and 1.2 gigawatts. Construction at the Michigan site was subsequently reported begun by Data Center Dynamics. The rest of the roster was, at announcement, exactly that: announced.

| Site | Announced | Led / developed by | Stated scale | State at announcement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Abilene, Texas (Site 1) | January 2025 | Crusoe; Oracle as lessee and operator | 1.2 GW, eight buildings | First phase running by September 2025 (CNBC) |
| Shackelford County, Texas | September 2025 | Oracle-led | Part of ~7 GW total | Announced |
| Doña Ana County, New Mexico | September 2025 | Oracle-led | Part of ~7 GW total | Announced |
| Wisconsin (Midwest site) | September 2025; named October 2025 | Oracle with Vantage | Part of ~7 GW total | Announced |
| Lordstown, Ohio | September 2025 | SoftBank-led | With Milam Co., scalable to 1.5 GW in 18 months (per announcement) | Announced |
| Milam County, Texas | September 2025 | SoftBank-led | As above | Announced |
| Saline Township, Michigan | October 2025 | Related Digital, with Oracle | More than 1 GW | Construction later reported begun (Data Center Dynamics) |
| Stargate UAE, Abu Dhabi | May 2025 | G42 building; OpenAI and Oracle operating | 1 GW, within a 5 GW campus | First 200 MW expected live in 2026 (per the announcement) |

Every figure in that table is a snapshot from the announcement record of 2025, and all of it will have moved by the time you read this. The states will have advanced — or some of them will not have, which would itself be the story. The way to bring the table up to date is not to search for newer press releases, which measure ambition, but to look where completion cannot be faked: the utility interconnection queues of the relevant grid operators, the capital work in progress and lease commitments in Oracle's filings, the backlog disclosures and — bluntly — satellite photographs, which trade analysts now use for exactly this purpose. Megawatts drawing power are a fact. Megawatts in a press release are a plan. The announced gigawatt total will always run ahead of the energised one; what matters is whether the gap is closing at the promised pace.

It is worth being fair in both directions here. The sceptical reading of the announcement roster — that headline numbers compound faster than concrete — is true, and this chapter has pressed it. But the credulous-sounding claim also happens to be partly true: unlike many famous megaproject eras, this one produced real, operating capacity within its first year. Abilene went from ranch land to a partly energised campus inside 2025, financed, leased and billing. The vocabulary broke because something genuinely without a peacetime parallel was being attempted; the announcements outran the buildings because announcements always do. Both facts belong in the record.

## What the megaproject era changes

Step back from Stargate the project to the megaproject era it named, because the structural shift outlasts any one venture.

The compute layer used to be an internal function of large companies. A cloud provider decided its own capacity, spent its own cash flow, and disclosed the result in aggregate. The megaproject era unbundles that. Demand is contracted years ahead by model companies that own nothing. Buildings are owned by infrastructure funds that compute nothing. Chips are allocated by suppliers that increasingly invest in their own customers. The operating middle — the Oracles, and the neoclouds of the previous chapter — signs long obligations in both directions and lives on the spread. Computing capacity, in short, is being financed the way pipelines, ports and power stations are financed: through special-purpose entities, layered capital, long offtake contracts and consortium risk-sharing. That is what the joint ventures *are*, structurally — project finance, applied to arithmetic.

For a reader of accounts, the practical consequence is that no single set of financial statements shows you the whole of any megaproject any more. The demand sits in an unaudited private company; the obligation sits in a listed cloud vendor's backlog; the asset sits in a fund's portfolio; the hardware risk sits in a depreciation footnote; the supplier's stake sits in an investments line. The project is real, but it is legible only in pieces, and each piece is presented by a party with its own incentive. The habit this book keeps recommending — every figure with a date, every figure with a source, every announcement sorted into announced, financed, contracted, under construction or energised — is not pedantry. In the megaproject era it is the only way to know what has actually been built.

And the direction of travel fits the argument of the whole book. The five-hundred-billion-dollar intentions are not being announced at the application layer, where the twenty-dollar subscriptions live, nor even principally at the model layer. They are announcements about land, power, buildings and silicon — the bottom half of the stack. When the largest sums in the industry's history are being assembled to secure the layers where things are physical, that tells you where the participants themselves believe the scarce thing is. Value has been moving down. Stargate is what it looks like when the money follows.
