# Custom Silicon Everywhere

Nvidia's gross margin for its fiscal year 2026, the year ended 25 January 2026, was 71.1 per cent on a GAAP basis, per the company's results announcement of February 2026 and its Form 10-K. Read that number the way its customers read it. For every dollar the world's data-centre builders handed to Nvidia that year, roughly seventy-one cents was margin over the cost of making the product. The customers writing those cheques were, for the most part, four of the largest and most technically capable companies on earth — companies that design software, run global infrastructure, and employ chip engineers by the thousand.

This chapter is about what those customers decided to do about it. Every large buyer of AI accelerators is now, to some degree, trying to become a maker. Google has been at it for a decade. Amazon bought its way in. Microsoft announced early and shipped late. Meta builds chips for a workload nobody else has at its scale. OpenAI, a company that owns no factories and publishes no audited accounts, has commissioned its own silicon through Broadcom. The question the chapter tries to answer honestly is the one that matters for the whole compute layer: what have these programmes actually achieved, as against what they have announced — and what would it really take to displace an incumbent whose deepest moat is not the chip at all, but the software wrapped around it?

## The arithmetic of escape

Start with why. The popular framing is a performance contest — each new in-house chip is presented, in its launch material, alongside claims about speed and efficiency against the reigning GPU. That framing misleads. None of these programmes exists primarily to beat Nvidia on performance. A custom chip that merely matches the merchant product, or even falls usefully short of it, can still be worth building, because the point is not the performance. The point is the margin.

The logic is old. Any business that buys a critical input from a supplier with pricing power faces the same choice a supermarket faces with branded goods: keep paying the brand's margin, or develop an own-label substitute and keep the margin yourself. The supermarket does not expect its own-label cola to win taste tests. It expects the cola to be good enough, and cheaper, and to discipline the price of the branded product sitting next to it on the shelf. AI accelerators are the most expensive own-label programme in commercial history, but the shape of the decision is the same.

An accountant would put it in terms of where the money goes. When a cloud company buys a merchant accelerator, the purchase price — including the supplier's seventy-odd per cent gross margin — is capitalised and then depreciated against the cloud's revenue over the years that follow. The supplier's margin becomes the customer's cost, permanently, spread across future periods. When the same company deploys its own chip, it pays the foundry, the memory makers, the packaging house and a design partner their costs and margins — but the fattest margin in the chain, the merchant designer's, drops out. Nothing about this appears as a revenue line anywhere. The saving shows up, if it shows up, as lower capital expenditure per unit of computing, and therefore as lower depreciation in future years. It is one of the largest cost decisions in modern business, and it is almost invisible in the accounts.

It is worth being precise about how the sums work, so take an illustrative programme, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Suppose a merchant accelerator sells for 25,000 dollars, and the physical cost of making it — wafer, memory, packaging, test — is 8,000. A hyperscaler running an in-house programme pays roughly that same 8,000 to the same supply chain. Add the cost of the programme itself: say a billion dollars a year for the design team, the design-services partner, the tape-outs and the masks. If the company deploys 400,000 chips a year, that overhead adds 2,500 dollars per chip. Total: about 10,500 dollars against the merchant's 25,000. On the face of it, the buyer has escaped more than half the cost.

Now apply the honest corrections, still inside the illustration. Suppose the in-house chip delivers only sixty per cent of the useful work of the merchant part — not because the silicon is bad, but because the software that feeds it is younger, the schedulers are less refined, and fewer engineers know how to squeeze it. Cost per unit of useful work is now 17,500 against 25,000. Still a saving, but a third rather than a half, and the gap narrows further if immature software leaves the chips idle more of the time. And the billion a year is committed before a single chip works; if the volume never comes, the overhead per chip balloons. That is the real shape of the decision: a large, probable saving, bought with a large, certain fixed cost and a schedule risk that sits entirely with the buyer. It explains both why everyone with the scale to try is trying, and why the results have been so uneven.

There are two further motives beneath the arithmetic. The first is bargaining power: a credible second source changes the price of the first source, even if the second source never reaches half the volume. The second is supply itself. In a period when the merchant supplier's products have been allocated rather than simply sold — chapter twenty-eight described businesses whose entire model is access to that allocation — an in-house chip is a queue you own.

What can be said from the record is narrower and more useful: the custom-accelerator business grew fast enough in that quarter — up triple digits year on year, per the March 2026 results coverage — that the named programmes alone struggle to account for it. The movement is wider than its press releases.

## Google: the decade head start

Google is the proof that the escape can work, and also the measure of how long it takes. The company deployed its first tensor processing unit in its own data centres in 2015 and only told the world in May 2016, at its I/O conference, mentioning that the chip had already been in production use for over a year. That origin matters. The TPU was not announced and then built; it was built, used, and then announced. Every other programme on the list has run the sequence the other way around.

Ten years and six generations later, Google unveiled Ironwood, its seventh-generation TPU, in November 2025, with general availability on Google Cloud late that month, as reported by CNBC and announced by Google Cloud. Each Ironwood chip carries 192 gigabytes of high-bandwidth memory, and the chips are sold in pods of 256 or 9,216, per Google's launch material of November 2025. The specification details will age quickly; the structural point will not. Google is the only hyperscaler whose custom silicon has passed the hardest test available — a large, sophisticated external customer choosing it at scale, with its own money.

That customer is Anthropic. In October 2025, Google and Anthropic announced a deal, reported by CNBC on 23 October 2025 as worth tens of billions of dollars, giving Anthropic access to up to one million TPUs and well over a gigawatt of capacity coming online in 2026. Anthropic's stated reasons were price-performance and its existing experience running its models on the chips. A caveat belongs in the same sentence, and it is one this book will keep making: Google is also an investor in Anthropic, with commitments reported in 2026 at up to 43 billion dollars including ten billion in April 2026. A customer partly funded by its supplier is still a real customer, but the relationship is not the same evidence as an arm's-length win, and the reader should hold both facts together.

Why did Google's programme work when others stalled? Three reasons, none of them secret. It started earliest, when the workloads were smaller and the incumbent's software advantage thinner. It controlled both sides of the interface — Google wrote the machine-learning frameworks its own researchers used, so it could make its chip a first-class citizen of its own software world rather than a guest in someone else's. And it had, from the first day, a massive captive workload — search, advertising, translation — that guaranteed the volumes over which the fixed costs of chip design could be spread. Every element of that formula is hard to copy a decade later, which is precisely what the rest of this chapter is about.

## Amazon: buying the customer that proves the chip

Amazon's route in was acquisition. In January 2015 it bought Annapurna Labs, a small Israeli chip firm, for a reported 350 million dollars, per GeekWire's reporting at the time. Out of that purchase came a family of silicon — the Nitro infrastructure chips, the Graviton processors, and eventually the Inferentia and Trainium accelerators. As a piece of capital allocation, the Annapurna purchase has a fair claim to being one of the better ones of its decade, and it cost less than a fifth of what a single leading-edge tape-out programme now consumes each year in the illustrative arithmetic above.

The scale arrived in 2025. Project Rainier — a training complex built for Anthropic across multiple US data centres — was activated with roughly half a million Trainium2 chips, per AWS statements and trade reporting in late 2025, with Amazon projecting that Anthropic would be using more than a million of the chips by the end of that year. In December 2025, at its re:Invent conference, AWS announced general availability of Trainium3 systems, built on a three-nanometre TSMC process and assembled into servers of up to 144 chips, per the company's announcement.

Those are real numbers, and they make Trainium, by deployed volume, the most consequential challenger programme in the industry. But the honest assessment has to note how the volume was achieved. Amazon has committed up to 33 billion dollars to Anthropic, per 2026 reporting, and Anthropic is overwhelmingly the flagship workload for Trainium at scale. The chip's proving customer is a company its maker part-owns and finances. The mechanism deserves a paragraph rather than an insinuation, because there is nothing improper in it and something genuinely circular about it. Amazon invests in Anthropic; Anthropic spends heavily on Amazon's compute, some of it on Amazon's own chips; the spending demonstrates the chips at scale, which strengthens the case for the next generation. Each step is commercially rational. The thing to watch, as ever, is what the arrangement proves and what it does not: it proves the chips work at scale, because Anthropic's models visibly run on them; it does not prove that an unrelated customer, paying its own money and free to choose, would have made the same choice. The Google–Anthropic TPU deal and the Amazon–Anthropic Trainium build are, between them, the strongest external validation any custom silicon has ever received — and both come from a customer in which the chipmakers hold large financial stakes. Both things are true.

One detail from the December 2025 announcements repays attention. AWS said its future Trainium4 would be designed to support NVLink Fusion, an Nvidia interconnect technology, per the re:Invent material and trade coverage. Read that carefully: the escape vehicle is being designed to dock with the incumbent's infrastructure. It is a small, telling admission of how gravity works in this industry.

## Microsoft: the cost of a slipped year

Microsoft's programme is the cautionary entry, and it deserves a sympathetic reading precisely because it went the way these programmes usually go. The company unveiled its first accelerator, Maia 100, in November 2023, with the stated aim of reducing reliance on Nvidia hardware in its data centres. The announcement was polished. What followed was harder. In 2025, reporting originating with The Information and carried by Reuters and the data-centre trade press said that mass production of the second-generation chip, codenamed Braga, had slipped into 2026 — at least six months late — citing design changes, staffing shortages and turnover on the team.

The chip eventually arrived: Microsoft announced Maia 200 in January 2026, positioned as an inference chip aimed at cutting the cost of serving AI models, per trade coverage in January and February 2026, with reporting later in 2026, again originating with The Information, saying a Maia 300 reveal was planned for September. So the programme lives. But measure it honestly, announcement against achievement: two years and two months passed between the November 2023 unveiling and a shipping second generation, during which Microsoft remained one of the largest buyers of merchant accelerators on earth, with capital expenditure guidance for 2026 near 190 billion dollars per the compilations of company guidance cited in chapter one. Against an incumbent that ships a new architecture on a roughly annual rhythm, a six-month slip is not a six-month problem. By the time the delayed chip arrives, the merchant part it was benchmarked against has been replaced, and the comparison that justified the programme internally has to be rebuilt against a faster target.

The deeper lesson is about scarcity of a particular kind of team. Chip design at this level is done by a few thousand people in the world, most of whom already work for the incumbent, the foundries' big customers, or Broadcom. A software company deciding to field a silicon team is bidding for people who have their pick of employers, on a project whose cancellation risk they can read in the press. Google solved this by starting before the talent war; Amazon solved it by buying a functioning team outright and leaving it in Israel; Microsoft has been assembling one under deadline, in public, against the market. The delay was not bad luck. It was the price of the third route.

## Meta: the maker with one customer

Meta's programme is the purest expression of the escape logic, because Meta has no cloud to sell and therefore no pretence that its chips are products. The Meta Training and Inference Accelerator exists to serve exactly one customer — Meta — and began not with the glamorous workload but with the profitable one: the ranking and recommendation models that decide what several billion people see in their feeds, which are the company's actual money machine.

In March 2026, Meta set out a roadmap of four further MTIA generations — numbered 300, 400, 450 and 500 — per the company's announcement and coverage in Tom's Hardware. On the company's own account, MTIA 300 was in production for ranking-and-recommendation training; MTIA 400 was in lab testing ahead of deployment; MTIA 450 was scheduled for mass deployment in early 2027 and MTIA 500, aimed at generative-AI inference, later in 2027. Meta said it had built the capability to ship a new chip roughly every six months. Alongside this, Broadcom announced an extended partnership to support multiple gigawatts of MTIA deployment, per Broadcom's announcement and the coverage of its early-2026 results.

Note what should be believed now and what should be filed as intention. Chips described as "in production" for a workload the company actually runs are achievement. A cadence promise and 2027 deployment dates are announcement, and this chapter's whole discipline is to keep those categories separate. Still, Meta's structural position is unusually favourable for the sceptical reader to accept: its dominant inference workloads are stable, enormous, and entirely under its own control, which is as close to Google's 2015 starting conditions as anyone can now get. Where Meta's savings land is also worth stating precisely, because no revenue line will ever show them: success appears only as lower capital expenditure per unit of inference served, inside a capex budget that guidance compilations in 2026 put at 125 to 145 billion dollars for the year, per the reporting cited in chapter one. A programme that shaved even a modest percentage off the accelerator portion of that number would repay its design costs many times over. Whether it has done so is not disclosed, and this book will not guess.

## OpenAI and Broadcom: a design without a factory

The newest programme belongs to the company with the least conventional balance sheet. On 13 October 2025, OpenAI and Broadcom jointly announced a collaboration to deploy ten gigawatts of OpenAI-designed accelerators, with OpenAI doing the design, Broadcom doing the development and deployment, the racks connected with Broadcom Ethernet, first deployments targeted for the second half of 2026 and completion by the end of 2029, per the joint announcement and Reuters coverage. The stated focus was inference — serving models, not training them.

Two features distinguish this from everything above it in the chapter. The first is the unit of announcement. The deal was denominated not in chips or dollars but in gigawatts — the vocabulary of chapter twenty-nine's megaprojects — which tells you the constraint its architects were designing around. The second is what OpenAI is. Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta fund their silicon from some of the largest operating cash flows in corporate history. OpenAI is a private company with no audited public accounts, funding an ambition of this scale alongside every other commitment it has announced; its March 2026 round of 122 billion dollars at an 852 billion dollar valuation, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes, is the measure of the capital it can summon, and chapter five's warnings about private-company figures apply in full. As at its announcement, the programme had deployed nothing. That is not a criticism — every programme starts at zero — but the reader should hold the ten-gigawatt figure as a plan with a 2029 completion date, not a fact about the present.

The unambiguous winner of the announcement was the partner. Broadcom has become the quiet beneficiary of nearly every escape attempt in the industry: it is the design-services and connectivity house through which hyperscalers turn architectural intentions into working silicon, and its results now measure the whole custom-silicon movement in one line. In its first quarter of fiscal 2026, Broadcom reported AI revenue of 8.4 billion dollars, up 106 per cent year on year and more than forty per cent of its total revenue, and guided the next quarter's AI revenue to 10.7 billion, per its results as reported in March 2026, with its chief executive describing a line of sight to 100 billion dollars of annual AI revenue by late 2027 or early 2028. Guidance is intention, not outturn. But the direction of the line explains the market capitalisation of roughly 1.61 trillion dollars that early-2026 rankings gave the company, per statista and alpha-sense compilations: investors are pricing Broadcom as the tollbooth on the road away from Nvidia. Whether every announced gigawatt is built or not, the design fees and the networking sockets come first.

## The moat is software

Now the harder half of the brief. If the economics of escape are this compelling, and the escapers this rich, why does the incumbent still command the volumes and the 71.1 per cent gross margin reported for fiscal 2026? The answer is mostly not in the silicon. It is in twenty years of software.

Nvidia announced CUDA, its platform for general-purpose programming on graphics processors, in 2006 and shipped it in 2007. For most of the following decade it looked like an expensive indulgence. What it actually was, in hindsight, was the moat being dug before the castle was worth attacking. Nearly every machine-learning framework, every optimised kernel, every debugging tool, every university course and every experienced practitioner grew up on that platform. A company can tape out a chip in two years. It cannot tape out an ecosystem: the libraries, the compilers, the tribal knowledge of a million engineers, and the certainty that whatever obscure thing a research team tries next will already run.

This is why the internal workload is the only realistic beachhead, and why every successful programme in this chapter started there. When you own both the chip and the software that runs on it, the ecosystem problem shrinks to your own payroll: Google could make its frameworks treat the TPU as a native target; Meta tunes chips for ranking models whose code it alone maintains; Anthropic's engineers, per the October 2025 announcements, work closely with the silicon they run on. Selling a custom chip to strangers is a different business entirely, because the software burden transfers to people who owe you nothing and who can always fall back on the platform everything already runs on. That — far more than any transistor-level shortfall — is what it takes to displace the incumbent, and why nobody fully has.

The scorecard, then, reads like this. After a decade of headlines announcing challengers, Nvidia's data-centre revenue for fiscal 2026 was 197.3 billion dollars, per its Form 10-K for the year ended 25 January 2026, and its market capitalisation of roughly 5.3 to 5.4 trillion dollars as at mid-August 2026, per companiesmarketcap and Motley Fool rankings, made it the most valuable company in the world. Custom silicon did not displace the incumbent. It grew beside it — Broadcom's 8.4 billion dollar AI quarter against Nvidia's roughly ten times larger data-centre run — because the demand for computing grew faster than either could supply alone. The truthful summary of the whole movement, as at 2026, is that the escapers have not beaten the merchant supplier and mostly no longer claim they will. They have built the second source, captured part of their own spending, and put a permanent question under the incumbent's pricing. In a market this large, that is not failure. It is the point.

## What the accounts will and will not tell you

Close with the practical problem, because it is unusually acute here: almost none of this is disclosed. No hyperscaler publishes the cost of its silicon programme, the number of chips it deploys, the cost per chip, or the savings achieved against merchant alternatives. The chips generate no revenue line; their success or failure dissolves into capital expenditure and, later, depreciation, inside companies whose capex now runs to twelve figures. A reader who wants to track this story from the filings must therefore read it sideways, and three instruments serve.

First, Broadcom's AI revenue line, reported quarterly, is the nearest thing to a published index of custom-accelerator volume across the industry, since so many of the programmes route through it — 8.4 billion dollars in the quarter reported in March 2026, with the growth rate mattering more than the level. Second, Nvidia's data-centre gross margin, disclosed in its results each quarter, is the instrument that will register pricing pressure if the second sources ever truly bite; through fiscal 2026 it had not registered much. Third, the customer-concentration and related-party disclosures in everyone's filings — chapter three explained where to find them — are where the circular relationships in this chapter, investor-funding-customer-funding-supplier, leave their only audited traces.

All of the figures above are photographs, taken mostly between October 2025 and August 2026, of an industry that re-prices weekly; by the time you read this, every specification will have a successor and every quarterly number a fresher one, and the current versions will be in the companies' own results pages and filings. Nothing in this chapter is a view on any company's shares, and it should go without saying — but this book says it anyway — that none of it is investment advice. The durable content is the structure: a seventy-per-cent margin at the bottleneck, a queue of the world's richest companies digging under it, and a moat made of software that has held, so far, against them all.
