# How a Foundry Actually Makes Money

A three-hundred-millimetre silicon wafer at the leading edge is a disc about the size of a dinner plate. TSMC's two-nanometre wafers were reported in October 2025 by TrendForce and TechNode to have been priced at around thirty thousand US dollars each, ten to twenty per cent above the three-nanometre average of twenty-five to twenty-seven thousand, and well below the fifty per cent increase the market had been speculating about. Nobody outside the negotiation knows the real number, because foundry pricing is not disclosed. But the order of magnitude is right, and it is the number this chapter is built on.

Thirty thousand dollars for a plate of silicon. The question that decides the capacity of the entire artificial intelligence industry is what fraction of that thirty thousand the foundry keeps, and how that fraction changes over the eight or ten years a process node is alive.

## What a foundry actually sells

Start with the invoice, because the invoice settles several arguments at once.

A foundry does not sell chips. It sells processed wafers. The customer supplies the design, pays for the mask set, books a slot, and takes delivery of a wafer with several hundred — or several dozen — copies of its design printed on it. Some of those copies work and some do not. Under the standard arrangement, the customer pays for the wafer either way.

That single commercial fact does more work than it looks. It means the foundry's revenue is a physical quantity multiplied by a price: wafers shipped times average selling price, sliced by process node. It means the risk of a design that yields badly sits mostly with the designer, not the manufacturer. And it means the foundry has one overwhelming operational objective, which is to put as many wafers through the line as the line will take, because the line costs almost the same whether it is full or empty.

The second thing a foundry sells is time — specifically, future time. Capacity at the leading edge is booked years before it exists. Trade reporting through 2026, including summaries from Dataconomy in March 2026 and subsequent supply-chain coverage, described TSMC's advanced capacity as booked out through 2028, with two-nanometre lines allocated and lead times measured in the high double digits of weeks. Those reports come from the supply chain rather than from the company, and they should be read as trade estimates rather than disclosure. But they describe a market in which the buyer's problem is allocation, not price.

So the foundry's revenue line is really an answer to three questions. How much capacity exists? How full is it? And what is the mix of nodes running through it? TSMC reports the third of those every quarter, and it is the most informative single table the company publishes.

| Node | Share of TSMC wafer revenue, second quarter 2026 |
|---|---|
| 2-nanometre | 3 per cent |
| 3-nanometre | 30 per cent |
| 5-nanometre | 33 per cent |
| 7-nanometre | 11 per cent |
| Advanced technologies (7-nanometre and below) | 77 per cent |

Those figures are from the company's second-quarter 2026 results, announced on 16 July 2026. Read the table as a picture of a business in which three-quarters of the money comes from processes that did not exist a decade ago, and in which the newest process — the one that costs the most to build — is still only three per cent of revenue in the quarter it begins to matter.

## The cost of a fab is settled before the first wafer

Nearly everything a fab will ever cost is decided before it produces anything sellable.

International Business Strategies estimated, in analysis reported by Tom's Hardware and TechSpot in 2024, that a two-nanometre-capable fab with capacity of roughly fifty thousand wafer starts a month would cost about twenty-eight billion US dollars to build and equip, against roughly twenty billion for a three-nanometre fab of similar capacity, with the difference driven mainly by the number of extreme ultraviolet lithography tools required. The same analysis put the processed cost of an N2 wafer at around thirty thousand dollars against around twenty thousand at N3 — note that this disagrees with the TrendForce figure quoted above, which put N3 at twenty-five to twenty-seven thousand. Two respectable sources, two different numbers, because one is modelling cost and the other is reporting price, and neither has seen a contract. That is the ordinary condition of this industry's public data, and a reader who does not check what a number is measuring will cheerfully average two incompatible things.

What is not in dispute is the shape. The overwhelming majority of a fab's cost is spent before revenue begins, sits on the balance sheet as property, plant and equipment, and comes back through the income statement as depreciation over the following five years or so. Published fab construction analyses in 2026 put the span from groundbreaking to volume production at roughly three to five years.

You can see the consequence in TSMC's own guidance. The company guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion US dollars in its January 2026 results, above analyst expectations, and then raised it in July 2026 to sixty to sixty-four billion, with seventy to eighty per cent directed at advanced process technologies, as reported by TrendForce and wire coverage of the results call. Against 2025 capital expenditure of about forty point nine billion dollars, cited in 2026 analysis of that guidance, capital intensity was expected to rise to around thirty-six per cent of sales in 2026 from about thirty-three and a half per cent in 2025. And the company guided depreciation expense to rise by a high-teens percentage in 2026.

That last sentence is the one to sit with. Depreciation is not a forecast of anything. It is the mechanical consequence of money already spent. When a foundry raises capital expenditure by ten billion dollars in a single revision, it is simultaneously committing itself to several billion dollars a year of additional fixed charge, beginning roughly when the tools are installed and continuing whether or not the demand arrives. Capital expenditure is optimism. Depreciation is the invoice for it.

## An illustrative fab

The mechanism is easier to see in numbers small enough to hold. What follows is an illustrative fab, with round numbers chosen for clarity. It is not any real company's fab and none of these figures should be attributed to one. Real fabs have messier cost structures, longer ramps and confidential prices.

Take a fab that cost twenty billion to build and equip, with capacity of four hundred thousand wafers a year — about thirty-three thousand wafer starts a month. Depreciate it straight-line over five years and the annual charge is four billion. Add one point two billion a year of other fixed cost: the operating staff, the maintenance contracts, the facility, the abatement, the baseline power that keeps a cleanroom a cleanroom whether or not it is producing. Total fixed cost, five point two billion a year. Then add variable cost — the blank wafer, the chemicals and gases, the incremental electricity, the consumables — at three thousand dollars a wafer.

At full capacity, the arithmetic is friendly. Fixed cost spread across four hundred thousand wafers is thirteen thousand dollars each; add three thousand variable and the fab produces wafers at sixteen thousand dollars. Sell them at twenty-five thousand and the gross margin is thirty-six per cent.

Now run the same fab at sixty per cent. Two hundred and forty thousand wafers must carry the same five point two billion, which is nearly twenty-two thousand dollars of fixed cost each. Add the three thousand variable and the cost per wafer is close to twenty-five thousand. At the same price, the fab has stopped making money.

| Illustrative fab | Cost per wafer | Gross margin at 25,000 price |
|---|---|---|
| 100 per cent utilisation (400,000 wafers) | 16,000 | 36 per cent |
| 80 per cent utilisation (320,000 wafers) | 19,250 | 23 per cent |
| 60 per cent utilisation (240,000 wafers) | 24,667 | 1 per cent |
| 50 per cent utilisation (200,000 wafers) | 29,000 | negative |

The breakeven point falls out of the same algebra. Each wafer contributes twenty-two thousand dollars above its variable cost, and five point two billion of fixed cost has to be covered, so the fab must ship about two hundred and thirty-six thousand wafers — roughly fifty-nine per cent of capacity — before it earns its first dollar of gross profit. Below three-fifths full, this fab loses money on every wafer it makes and would lose more by making none.

That is why a fab must run full, and it is not a preference or a matter of management discipline. It is the shape of the cost curve. A business with eighty per cent fixed costs has enormous operating gearing in both directions: the last ten per cent of utilisation is almost pure profit, and the first ten per cent of idleness is almost pure loss. Every commercial instinct in a foundry — long-term agreements, capacity reservation fees, prepayments from customers, price concessions to fill a hole in the schedule, the willingness to take low-margin work that keeps the line moving — follows from this one arithmetic fact.

It also explains why foundries and their customers sign contracts that look strange from outside. A customer who books capacity three years ahead is not being generous; it is buying insurance against a queue. A foundry that accepts prepayment is not desperate; it is converting a fixed-cost gamble into a partly funded one. The fab gets built with borrowed money, retained profits and, at the margin, customer commitments, and every one of those funding sources wants the line full.

## Yield: the number nobody publishes

The illustrative fab above priced wafers. Customers do not buy wafers because they want silicon discs; they buy them because they want working die. Between the two sits yield, which is the single most commercially sensitive number in the industry and is disclosed by nobody. Foundries do not publish it. Their customers do not publish it. Figures that appear in the trade press are estimates, often supplied by people with an interest in the impression they create, and this book treats them as such.

What can be described honestly is the shape of the curve, because the physics is not secret even when the numbers are.

A new process starts badly. Defect densities are high, the process window is narrow, and a large fraction of the die on each wafer fails electrical test. Over the following two or three years, engineers find and eliminate defect mechanisms one at a time, and yield climbs — quickly at first, then in smaller increments as the easy problems are used up. The cost of a processed wafer barely changes through all of this. What changes is the number of good die that come off it.

Return to the illustrative fab and add an illustrative die: a large processor, of which sixty candidate copies fit on a wafer. Again, round numbers chosen to teach the mechanism, belonging to no real product.

| Illustrative year | Utilisation | Cost per wafer | Yield | Good die per wafer | Cost per good die |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 (ramp) | 60 per cent | 24,700 | 50 per cent | 30 | 823 |
| Year 2 | 90 per cent | 17,400 | 70 per cent | 42 | 414 |
| Year 3 | 100 per cent | 16,000 | 85 per cent | 51 | 314 |

The cost of a working chip falls by more than half in two years, and not one dollar of that improvement comes from the price of anything the fab buys. It comes from two sources only: the fab filling up, which spreads the fixed cost, and the yield rising, which increases the output per unit of the same input. Both are permanent in a way that a favourable input price is not. This is the reason process engineering is treated inside these companies as a profit centre rather than a cost centre, and the reason a foundry with a two-year head start on a node has an advantage that money cannot immediately buy.

## Why price falls more slowly than cost

Now put the price alongside the cost, because the gap between the two curves is the whole business.

Wafer prices at a given node do fall over its life. They do not fall as fast as cost falls during the ramp. In the illustrative fab, suppose the price is twenty-five thousand in year one, twenty-four thousand in year two and twenty-three thousand in year three — a decline of about four per cent a year, against a cost per wafer that falls by a third over the same period.

| Illustrative year | Price per wafer | Cost per wafer | Gross profit per wafer | Gross margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 25,000 | 24,700 | 300 | 1 per cent |
| Year 2 | 24,000 | 17,400 | 6,600 | 28 per cent |
| Year 3 | 23,000 | 16,000 | 7,000 | 30 per cent |

The node earns almost nothing in its first year and then produces most of its lifetime profit in the years when it is full, yielding well and still the best process available. That window is the entire economic reason to be first.

Why does price not fall as fast as cost? Because at the leading edge the customer has no alternative. There is only one process capable of the density and power characteristics a flagship accelerator needs, and the queue for it is long. Price at the leading edge is not set by cost plus a margin; it is set by what the scarcest input in the world can command from customers whose own products sell at extraordinary margins. TrendForce reported global foundry market share for the first quarter of 2026 at seventy-two point three per cent for TSMC, six point five for Samsung, five point one for SMIC and three point nine for UMC, with the top ten foundries' combined revenue at a record forty-seven point nine five billion US dollars, up three point seven per cent on the quarter. A seventy-two per cent share of a market is unusual. A seventy-two per cent share concentrated in the part of the market where there is no second source is something else.

The published results say the same thing without the adjectives. TSMC's second quarter of 2026, announced on 16 July 2026, produced revenue of forty point two zero billion US dollars, up thirty-three point seven per cent year on year and twelve per cent on the quarter, with a gross margin of sixty-seven point seven per cent, an operating margin of sixty point three per cent and a net margin of fifty-five point six per cent. Net income was NT$706.56 billion, up seventy-seven point four per cent year on year, and the company raised its full-year 2026 revenue growth outlook to slightly above forty per cent in US dollar terms. CNBC reported the same day that the company would invest an additional hundred billion dollars in Arizona.

Notice the distance between that consolidated sixty-seven point seven per cent and the thirty-six per cent the illustrative fab earned at full capacity. The gap is not an error in the illustration. A consolidated foundry margin blends new fabs carrying full depreciation with older fabs whose capital cost was written off years ago, mature nodes running at high utilisation on long-paid-for equipment, advanced packaging, and a mix in which the highest-priced wafers are also the ones in shortest supply. No single new fab earns the company margin in its first years. Understanding that is most of what separates a reader who can interpret a foundry's accounts from one who cannot.

## When the node matures, the price falls hard

The pleasant part of the curve ends. A node stops being the leading edge the moment the next one qualifies, and from then on its price is set by competition rather than scarcity. Other foundries have caught up. The customers who needed the very best have moved on, and what remains is high-volume, price-sensitive work: microcontrollers, power management, image sensors, connectivity, the analogue and mixed-signal parts that go in everything and command nothing.

The published pricing history at mature nodes is a study in what happens when supply catches demand. Trade price trackers put twenty-eight-nanometre wafers at around two thousand nine hundred to three thousand two hundred dollars in early 2024, drifting down through the year. In January 2025, trade reporting described SMIC cutting its twenty-eight-nanometre price from about two thousand five hundred dollars a wafer to about one thousand five hundred, a decline of roughly forty per cent. TrendForce reported in October 2025 that UMC was preparing to cut its supplier prices by fifteen per cent for 2026. Those are trade reports of private commercial terms, not company disclosure, and should be weighted accordingly. But the direction is corroborated by the reported margins, which is the test that matters.

Compare three foundries in the same quarter.

| Company | Quarter | Gross margin | Utilisation | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TSMC | Second quarter 2026 | 67.7 per cent | Not disclosed as a rate; advanced nodes described as highly utilised | Company results, July 2026 |
| UMC | Second quarter 2026 | 32.5 per cent | 85 per cent | Company results, July 2026 |
| SMIC | Second quarter 2026 | 25.3 per cent | 93.7 per cent | Company results, reported by TrendForce, August 2026 |

Read that table slowly, because it contains the chapter's central claim in three rows. SMIC ran its fabs at nearly ninety-four per cent — fuller than either of the others disclosed — and earned roughly a quarter of each revenue dollar in gross profit. TSMC did not disclose a utilisation rate and earned more than two-thirds. Running full is necessary. It is nowhere near sufficient. What decides the margin is whether anyone else can make the same thing.

The trailing edge is not a bad business in every year. UMC's gross margin rose from twenty-nine point two per cent in the first quarter of 2026 to thirty-two point five in the second as utilisation climbed from seventy-nine per cent to eighty-five, and the company guided the third quarter to a gross margin in the mid-thirties with utilisation above ninety per cent, alongside a wafer price adjustment in the second half of the year. SMIC's average selling price rose five point seven per cent on the quarter, and it guided third-quarter gross margin to twenty-six to twenty-eight per cent. Trade reporting in 2026 described mature eight-inch foundry prices rising broadly by five to fifteen per cent between the first and second quarters as demand from artificial intelligence spread outward from the leading edge into power management and supporting components. After two years of falling prices, the mature end found a floor.

But notice what the recovery is made of. It is a cyclical upturn in utilisation and a modest price rise, in a segment where the long-run price trend is down and the competitive set keeps growing. That is the difference between a cycle and a franchise.

There is one genuinely attractive feature of an old fab, and it is worth stating precisely because it is so often stated loosely. Once equipment is fully depreciated, the largest single line in its cost structure disappears. Return to the illustrative fab in a year after the five-year depreciation life has run out: fixed cost falls from five point two billion to one point two, cost per wafer at full utilisation falls from sixteen thousand to six thousand, and even at a mature-node price of twelve thousand the fab earns a gross margin of about fifty per cent. That is why companies keep old fabs running long after the technology is unfashionable.

The catch is that this happy state exists only for a company that has stopped building. A foundry that keeps adding capacity — as every serious foundry must, because standing still at the trailing edge means being undercut by someone with newer, cheaper equipment — never lets depreciation run off in aggregate. The old fab's cash flow funds the new fab's depreciation. That is the treadmill, and it is the reason the sector's reported margins are so much lower than the arithmetic of any individual paid-for asset would suggest.

## Why capacity decided now sets the ceiling three years out

Everything above adds up to a conclusion about supply that most coverage of artificial intelligence skips.

The quantity of leading-edge silicon available in 2029 is very close to being fixed already. It is a function of fabs whose ground is being broken now, tools ordered from a supplier with a multi-year backlog, and cleanrooms that will take three to five years to reach volume production. No amount of demand appearing in 2028 can add capacity in 2028. The only lever is price, which allocates the existing queue rather than lengthening it.

This is what makes a capital expenditure number a forecast rather than a fact about the present. When TSMC raised 2026 capital expenditure from fifty-two to fifty-six billion dollars to sixty to sixty-four billion in July 2026, with seventy to eighty per cent going to advanced process, it was not describing 2026 demand. It was making a statement, in concrete and lithography tools, about how much artificial intelligence silicon the world will be able to consume at the end of the decade. The same applies to the additional hundred billion dollars committed to Arizona and reported by CNBC in July 2026.

Two refinements matter for anyone reading the capacity story properly.

The first is that wafer capacity is no longer the only binding constraint. An accelerator is not finished when the logic wafer is. It must be cut, stacked with high-bandwidth memory and assembled on an interposer, and that advanced packaging step has its own capacity, its own tools and its own queue. TrendForce reported in June 2026 that the supply-demand gap in TSMC's CoWoS advanced packaging was expected to narrow from around twenty per cent to around ten per cent by the end of 2026 as capacity expanded, with monthly capacity variously reported as ramping toward roughly a hundred and twenty to a hundred and forty thousand wafers a month by late 2026. Those are trade estimates rather than company disclosure and the figures differ between sources. The point that survives the uncertainty is structural: adding logic capacity without adding packaging capacity moves the bottleneck rather than removing it.

The second is that capacity built in a politically preferred location costs more to run. TSMC has said on its 2026 earnings calls that the ramp of overseas fabs dilutes consolidated gross margin by two to three percentage points in 2026, widening to three to four points as full-scale production begins at those sites. TrendForce reported in August 2026 that TSMC's Arizona operation, while sharply more profitable than a year earlier in the first half of 2026, saw its second-quarter earnings pull back on the quarter as depreciation from the ramp began to bite. Geographic diversification is bought with margin. That is a defensible price for reducing concentration risk, and it is a price, and the accounts show it.

Put the three together — a three-to-five-year construction lag, a packaging step with its own queue, and a deliberate shift of capacity to higher-cost locations — and the supply of leading-edge computing power for the rest of this decade looks less like a market that clears and more like a schedule that is already written. The ceiling on how much artificial intelligence the world can run in 2029 was set by capital allocation committees meeting in 2026.

## Reading a foundry's accounts

Six things tell you nearly everything about a foundry, and five of them are published.

Revenue by node tells you where the company sits on the price curve, and how much of its revenue depends on processes that will be commoditised within a few years. A rising share from the newest node is the leading indicator of the next two years of margin.

Utilisation tells you where the company sits on its fixed-cost curve, and it is the number that moves gross margin fastest in both directions. UMC and SMIC disclose it. TSMC does not disclose a consolidated rate, which means its margin must be read through the mix instead.

Capital expenditure tells you what management believes about demand three years out, expressed in cash it cannot get back. Watch for mid-year revisions, which is where conviction shows.

Depreciation, and its growth rate, tells you what that belief will cost in reported profit. A company guiding depreciation up by a high-teens percentage has already decided to absorb that charge; the question is whether revenue arrives to meet it.

Capital intensity — capital expenditure as a share of sales — tells you how much of each revenue dollar is being recycled into the next generation rather than returned. Around thirty-six per cent in 2026 against thirty-three and a half in 2025, for TSMC, on 2026 analysis of the company's guidance.

The sixth is yield, and it is not published by anyone. It has to be inferred: a gross margin rising while price is flat or falling, and utilisation unchanged, is a yield story. That inference is the most useful thing an outside reader can do with a foundry's accounts, and it takes about ten minutes a quarter.

One caution to close on. Every figure in this chapter is a snapshot with a date attached, and several of the most interesting ones — wafer prices, yields, booking horizons, packaging capacity — are trade estimates rather than audited disclosure, because the companies involved treat them as commercial secrets. They will have moved by the time this is read. The quarterly results releases and the Form 20-F filings are where the current numbers live, and they are free. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell any security, and nothing here is a forecast of any share price. The purpose is narrower: to make the next foundry results announcement legible, so that when a company raises its capital expenditure by ten billion dollars in a single quarter, a reader knows exactly which three years of the industry's future have just been decided, and what it will cost in depreciation to find out whether the decision was right.
