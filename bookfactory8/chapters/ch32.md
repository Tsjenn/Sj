# Depreciation: The Number That Decides Everything

Somewhere in the notes to every cloud operator's accounts, usually between the summary of accounting policies and the tax note, sits a sentence like this: "Servers and network equipment are depreciated on a straight-line basis over estimated useful lives of up to six years." One sentence, no numbers in bold, no chart. In this industry, that sentence decides more reported profit than any pricing decision, any product launch, and any contract announcement you will ever read about.

This chapter is about that sentence. Chapter four introduced it as one tool among several for reading a cloud company's accounts. Here it gets the full treatment, because it has earned it. The four largest buyers of computing hardware — Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta — were on course to spend approaching seven hundred billion US dollars between them on capital expenditure in 2026, as reported by CNBC in February 2026. Nearly every dollar of that spending reaches reported profit through one mechanism: an estimate of how many years the assets will be useful. Estimate long, and this year's profit is higher. Estimate short, and it is lower. The cash is identical either way.

No prediction is coming in this chapter. What is coming is a method: how the estimate works, what the industry has actually done with it and when, why the machines being bought now may not behave like the machines the estimates were built on, how to calculate for yourself what a change would do, and exactly which lines in the disclosures will tell you the answer first. Nothing here is investment advice; it is instruction in reading.

## An estimate, not a measurement

Start with what depreciation is not. It is not a payment. It is not a decline in a market price. It is not a measurement of anything.

Depreciation is an allocation. An asset is bought for cash, the cash leaves in year one, and accounting's job is to spread that cost across the years the asset earns revenue, so that each year's profit reflects the cost of the capacity that produced it. The spreading requires three inputs: the cost, which is a fact; the residual value at the end, which in this industry is usually assumed to be small or nil, because there is no deep, observable second-hand market for used AI accelerators; and the useful life, which is an estimate made by management.

The word "estimate" is doing precise legal work there. Under both of the major accounting frameworks, a useful life is a change-able judgement, reviewed at least annually. An auditor does not certify that six years is correct. An auditor concludes that six years is reasonable — that it sits within the range a competent management could defend with the evidence available. Several different numbers can all be reasonable at once. That is not a scandal; it is the nature of estimating the future. But it means the profit figure built on the estimate inherits the estimate's character. It is a view, dressed as a result.

Two consequences follow, and both matter for everything else in this chapter.

First, "useful life" means economic life, not physical life. The test is not whether the machine still switches on. It is how long the machine will earn its keep — its power, its cooling, its floor space, its share of the network — against alternatives. A server can be physically perfect and economically finished, because the machine beside it does three times the work per watt. Equally, a machine can outlive every forecast because demand for its class of work refuses to die. The estimate is a forecast about markets as much as about metal.

Second, the estimate is applied prospectively. This is the piece of mechanics that most coverage misses, and it changes how every announcement should be read. A revision to a useful life is a change in accounting estimate, not a change in policy and not the correction of an error. Prior years are not restated. There is no catch-up charge and no catch-up credit. The asset's remaining book value is simply spread over the new remaining life from the date of the change onwards. The past stays as printed; only the future charge moves. Which means the comparative figures sitting beside the current year in the same table were prepared on a different basis, and the growth rate between them is not like-for-like unless you adjust it yourself. The accounts will tell you this, once, in a note. The headline will not.

## The mechanics, done properly

The arithmetic deserves to be shown exactly, because the prospective rule produces effects that are not obvious. Take an illustrative operator, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company.

The operator buys a server fleet for twelve billion dollars, in service from the start of year one, straight-line, nil residual value. Consider four different lives it could have assumed:

| Assumed useful life | Annual depreciation charge | Charge as a share of cost, each year |
|---|---|---|
| Three years | 4.0 billion | 33 per cent |
| Four years | 3.0 billion | 25 per cent |
| Five years | 2.4 billion | 20 per cent |
| Six years | 2.0 billion | 17 per cent |

Between the shortest and longest assumption sits two billion dollars of annual reported profit, on identical machines doing identical work for identical customers. Nothing outside the accounting department is different across those four rows.

Now the prospective mechanics. Suppose the operator assumed six years, and two years in — with eight billion of book value remaining — concludes that the honest remaining life is two years, not four. From the date of that decision, the charge becomes four billion a year instead of two. The change doubles the depreciation on that fleet without touching a single prior-year figure and without any separate charge for the two "missing" billions of earlier years. The correction arrives entirely as heavier future expense.

Run it the other way. Suppose the operator assumed four years and, two years in, with six billion of book value left, extends the total life to six years. The remaining six billion now spreads over four years instead of two: the charge falls from three billion a year to one and a half. Reported profit rises by one and a half billion a year, immediately, quietly, inside a line item that also contains buildings, chillers, fibre and office fit-out. There is no transaction to announce. There is a sentence in a note.

One more feature of the machinery, because it becomes important later: shortening a life is not the only way the estimate can meet reality. If specific equipment is retired before its assumed life is up, the remaining book value is written off at once — an early-retirement charge that lands in a single period. And if a whole class of assets is judged unable to earn back its book value, an impairment does the same at larger scale. A life estimate that proves too long does not produce a restatement of the past. It produces heavier charges in the future, arriving either gradually (a shortened life) or abruptly (retirements and impairments). Watch for both shapes.

## A short history of lengthening

What has the industry actually done with this estimate? For most of the past several years, one thing: lengthened it. The record below is drawn from the companies' own filings and disclosures, and every figure is as disclosed at the time.

| Company | What changed | Effective | Disclosed effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microsoft | Server and network equipment life extended from four years to six | Fiscal 2023, following an assessment in July 2022 | Operating income higher by 885 million US dollars in the three months to 31 March 2023 and by 2.9 billion in the nine months to that date (Microsoft Form 10-Q, filed April 2023) |
| Alphabet | Servers from four years to six; certain network equipment from five to six | Fiscal 2023, announced February 2023 | Expected reduction in depreciation of about 3.4 billion US dollars; the outturn was a 3.9 billion reduction in depreciation and a 3.0 billion increase in net income for the year to 31 December 2023 (Alphabet Form 10-K for 2023) |
| Meta | Certain servers and network assets extended to 5.5 years, after earlier extensions in 2022 | Fiscal 2025, announced January 2025 | Expected to reduce full-year 2025 depreciation by approximately 2.9 billion US dollars (Meta disclosure, January 2025) |
| Amazon | Servers from five years to six | February 2024 | Disclosed as an extension of useful life (Amazon disclosure, 2024) |
| Amazon | A subset of servers and network equipment shortened from six years back to five, citing the increased pace of technology development, particularly in artificial intelligence and machine learning | 1 January 2025 | Expected to decrease 2025 operating income by approximately 700 million US dollars; separately, early retirement of a subset of equipment produced a charge of about 920 million US dollars (Amazon disclosures, 2025) |
| CoreWeave | Technology equipment — the computing, networking and storage that fills its data centres — depreciated straight-line over six years, raised from five | Start of 2023, before the company listed | The category stood at just over 13 billion US dollars of assets at the end of June 2025 (reported by the Motley Fool, November 2025) |

Read the table as a sequence and a shape emerges. Through 2022 and 2023, the largest operators lengthened server lives, and the disclosed benefits ran to billions of dollars of reported profit per company per year. The stated reasons were consistent and, on the evidence then available, defensible: software and operational improvements were genuinely keeping general-purpose servers in productive service longer than the old four-year assumption implied. A web server's workload does not change much from year to year. If the machine still serves pages economically in year six, a six-year life is not aggressive; it is accurate.

Then the direction wobbled. Amazon is the company that went both ways: it lengthened server lives to six years in February 2024, and less than a year later shortened a subset back to five — naming AI and machine learning as the reason — while taking a charge of about 920 million dollars for equipment it retired early. Two adjacent changes, opposite directions, both disclosed with a stated reason and a money amount. Whatever else that is, it is a management revisiting an estimate rather than defending one, and it is the first large, public admission that AI-era hardware might not sit comfortably on schedules built for the web era.

The lengthening has not stopped elsewhere, though it has changed asset class. In July 2026 Microsoft said that from the start of its fiscal 2027 it would extend the estimated useful life of its data centre and office buildings from fifteen years to twenty-five (company commentary, reported July 2026). The company was explicit that this concerns concrete, not servers, and that the larger effect is on reported capital expenditure rather than profit — a longer building life changes how future data centre leases are classified, and finance leases count in headline capex while operating leases do not. File that detail away: an estimate about buildings altered the capital expenditure number the entire market was watching. The useful-life lever reaches further than the depreciation line.

And note who else appears in the table. CoreWeave — a company whose entire business is renting accelerators by the hour — adopted a six-year life for the equipment class containing its GPUs, lengthened from five at the start of 2023. Nebius, a competitor in the same trade, was reported through 2025 to depreciate similar equipment over four years, a contrast drawn repeatedly in trade coverage that year (wccftech; bizety, September 2025). Same machines, same rental market, two-year difference in assumed life. Both assumptions were signed off as reasonable. That pair, more neatly than any hypothetical, makes the chapter's point: the reported margins of the two companies are not comparable until you have put their fleets on a common life — which is a calculation you can do yourself, and which this chapter will show.

## Why AI hardware may not age like a web server

The extensions of 2022 and 2023 rested on an observation about general-purpose servers. The open question — and it is genuinely open — is whether that observation transfers to accelerators. The honest way to present it is as a ledger with entries on both sides, because that is what it is.

On the side of shorter lives, three arguments.

The first is cadence. Nvidia's chief executive told the Computex audience in June 2024 that the company was on a "one-year rhythm": a new flagship data-centre platform every year, where the previous rhythm had been closer to two, with the Rubin generation announced for 2026 to succeed Blackwell (company keynote, June 2024, widely reported at the time). A machine assumed to earn for six years will face five newer generations before its schedule runs out. Each successor does materially more work per watt, and power — as the previous chapter's territory makes clear — is the scarce input. When electricity and floor space are the binding constraints, the economic question is not "does the old machine work?" but "does the old machine deserve its share of the megawatts?"

The second is intensity of use. Training clusters run their accelerators hot, dense and near-continuously, in a way that ordinary web serving never approached. Physical wear is not the accounting test, but it feeds the economic one: a machine pushed at high utilisation for years arrives at the back half of its assumed life with more failures, more maintenance and less headroom.

The third is revealed behaviour. A company that retires equipment early and says so — as Amazon did, with its roughly 920-million-dollar charge disclosed in 2025 — is providing data about actual turnover in an actual fleet. Disclosed retirements are worth more than any argument, in either direction, because they are the estimate colliding with the machines in public.

On the side of longer lives, three arguments of real substance.

First, the counterexample. In November 2025, responding to criticism of the industry's assumptions, Nvidia's chief executive pointed to the A100 — an accelerator launched in 2020 — as still running at full utilisation across customer fleets, and argued that the company's software platform keeps successive generations productive far beyond their headline moment (the exchange was reported in November 2025 by Yahoo Finance, CNBC and others). Set aside that the argument came from the seller of the machines, which you should weigh but not dismiss; the underlying observation was not disputed. Old accelerators were still earning.

Second, the cascade. An accelerator's life is not one job; it is a sequence of jobs. Frontier training migrates to the newest hardware, and the previous generation cascades down to fine-tuning, then to inference, then to internal and batch workloads that are less sensitive to speed. A hyperscaler running enormous internal workloads has a deep reservoir of such second and third jobs. This is also why the same assumed life can be honest at a hyperscaler and optimistic at a pure rental operator: a machine that no external customer will rent earns nothing at CoreWeave or Nebius, while at Amazon or Alphabet it may still be usefully grinding through internal work. Identical hardware, different economic life, because economic life is a property of the owner's demand, not of the silicon.

Third, scarcity itself. Through the period this book covers, computing capacity has been reported as sold out well ahead of supply across much of the industry — the same condition visible in memory, where capacity was reported sold out through 2026 across major suppliers (industry analysis, 2026). In a shortage, old machines keep earning simply because there is nothing to replace them with. Note the shape of that argument, though: it makes long lives true for as long as the shortage lasts, and no longer. An estimate defended by scarcity has borrowed its truth from the market cycle, and market cycles in this industry are the subject of an entire earlier part of this book.

Both sides of the ledger were argued loudly in late 2025. The investor Michael Burry criticised the large operators for what he described as understating depreciation by extending useful lives, put the effect at about 176 billion US dollars across 2026 to 2028, and estimated that the profits of Oracle and Meta specifically could be overstated by roughly 27 and 21 per cent respectively by 2028 on his assumptions (reported by CNBC, 11 November 2025). His premise: hardware on a roughly annual product cycle should not sit on five- and six-year schedules. The rebuttal, described above, came within days. Neither side of that argument is a fact. Both are forecasts about machines, and the machines have not finished voting. The reasonable reader holds the question open and reads the number as an estimate — then does the arithmetic below, which requires no opinion at all.

## What a shortening would do: the arithmetic

The brief for this section is precision without prediction. Here is what a change in assumed life does to reported profit, shown on an illustrative fleet with round numbers belonging to no real company, followed by the recipe for applying the same arithmetic to any real company you choose.

Take an illustrative operator that has been growing hard. It placed ten billion dollars of servers in service at the start of year one, fifteen billion at the start of year two, and twenty-two billion at the start of year three — a fleet of forty-seven billion at cost. It assumes six-year lives, straight line, nil residual.

In year three, its depreciation charge on that fleet is one sixth of forty-seven billion: about 7.8 billion dollars.

Now suppose that at the start of year four management concludes the honest total life is four years, not six. The rule is prospective: each cohort's remaining book value spreads over its new remaining life.

| Cohort | Cost | Book value at start of year four (six-year basis) | Old remaining life | New remaining life | Old annual charge | New annual charge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year one | 10.0 | 5.0 | 3 years | 1 year | 1.67 | 5.0 |
| Year two | 15.0 | 10.0 | 4 years | 2 years | 2.5 | 5.0 |
| Year three | 22.0 | 18.3 | 5 years | 3 years | 3.67 | 6.1 |
| Total | 47.0 | 33.3 | — | — | 7.8 | 16.1 |

The year-four charge more than doubles, from about 7.8 billion to about 16.1 billion. Reported operating profit falls by roughly 8.3 billion dollars in a single year — with no change in cash, revenue, customers or machines. The oldest cohort is hit hardest, because a shortening compresses whatever book value remains into whatever life remains. And notice what the change does not do: it books no loss for the past. The three years already reported stand. The entire correction arrives as future expense, which is why a company can be right to change and still look suddenly, dramatically worse.

Reverse the exercise for completeness. The same fleet moved from a four-year assumption to six in year four would see its charge fall by a similar order — which is, mechanically, what the 2022–2023 extensions did across the industry, cohort by cohort, in the billions per company disclosed in the table earlier.

Now the recipe, for real companies. Everything it needs is in the annual filing.

First, find the property and equipment note. It gives gross cost, accumulated depreciation and net book value, usually split by class — buildings, land, and the class variously called servers, technology infrastructure or technology equipment. Second, find the stated useful life range for that class in the accounting policies note. Third, compute the implied annual charge at the stated life: gross cost of the class divided by the assumed life is a serviceable straight-line approximation. Fourth, recompute at the alternative life you want to test — four years instead of six, three instead of five — and take the difference. Fifth, set that difference against the company's pre-tax profit, and you have the sensitivity: what the alternative assumption would do to the earnings everyone quotes.

Two refinements sharpen it. Accumulated depreciation divided by the annual charge approximates the fleet's average age in years — useful, because a shortening hits an old fleet far harder than a young one, as the cohort table shows. And capital expenditure divided by depreciation tells you how much of the eventual charge is still ahead: a company spending at three or four times its current depreciation has, embedded in its future income statements, a charge several times today's, on whatever life is assumed. That ratio is the single fastest way to see that today's reported margin was earned on yesterday's much smaller asset base.

Then the caveats, which are the difference between a method and a mistake. The approximation ignores residual values (usually small here), mid-year in-service dates (real fleets arrive continuously, not on the first of January), and the blending of asset classes with different lives inside one note. It also ignores leased capacity: a company renting its computing from others carries the equivalent economics in lease liabilities and rent, not in depreciation, and a sector-wide comparison must add that back or it will flatter the renters. Your answer will be approximate. It will also be honest, which the headline number, resting silently on the estimate you are testing, is not quite.

What would a shortening do across the sector rather than one company? The method is the same, summed: apply the recipe to each large operator's technology-equipment class and add. The one published attempt at that sum with a named author and date — the roughly 176 billion dollars across 2026 to 2028 described above (CNBC, November 2025) — should be treated exactly as this book treats any unaudited estimate: as one practitioner's arithmetic on stated assumptions, reported by a named outlet on a stated date, checkable and disputable. The point of this chapter is that you do not need to borrow his answer. The inputs are public.

## What to watch in the disclosures

A change in this estimate announces itself, but quietly, and usually in this order.

| What you want to know | Where it is disclosed |
|---|---|
| The current assumed lives, by asset class | Accounting policies note: property and equipment |
| Whether a life changed this year, and its effect in money | Notes on changes in estimates; management commentary — material effects are disclosed with amounts, as every entry in this chapter's history table was |
| How old the fleet is | Property and equipment note: accumulated depreciation against gross cost, and the implied-age arithmetic above |
| How much charge is still ahead | Capital expenditure (cash flow statement, investing activities) against the depreciation charge |
| Whether the estimate is meeting reality early | Charges for early retirement, abandonment or impairment of equipment — the abrupt shape of the same correction |
| Commitments not yet on the balance sheet | The commitments note: contracted future purchases and leases that today's depreciation says nothing about |
| The discipline underneath it all | Free cash flow: operating cash flow less capital expenditure — the figure no life assumption can touch |

Beyond the table, three softer signals reward attention.

Language first. Filings that say management "assessed the useful lives" of equipment this year are telling you the estimate was actively revisited, whatever the conclusion. The sentence appears before the change does.

Retirements second. Early-retirement charges are the estimate failing in real time, machine by machine. A company disclosing them while lengthening or holding its assumed life is showing you both hands at once, and the tension between them is information.

Divergence third. When companies in the same trade hold visibly different assumptions — six years against four for near-identical rented fleets, as reported of CoreWeave and Nebius in 2025 — at most one of those margins is on the honest side of the range. You do not need to know which. You need to compare them only after restating to a common life, which the recipe above lets you do in minutes.

And throughout, keep cash in its privileged place. Free cash flow is unmoved by any of this: the money left when the machines were bought, and no allocation schedule brings it back or takes more of it. Reported profit and free cash flow can diverge for entirely honest reasons during a build-out of this size — depreciation lags spending on the way up — but the divergence has a size and a direction, and you should always know both before trusting either figure alone.

## The number and the machines

Strip the chapter to its frame and it is this. One assumption — the useful life — sets the annual depreciation charge. The charge is among the largest costs in the sector, so it sets reported operating margin. And because the same assumption governs how quickly the asset base is carried at cost on the balance sheet, it shapes every measure of return on the capital invested. One number, chosen by management, reviewed by auditors as reasonable rather than correct, propagating through everything the market quotes.

The industry lengthened that number when the evidence supported it, and disclosed billions of resulting profit, on the record, with dates. One large operator has already shortened it for part of its fleet, named AI as the reason, and paid for the correction in charges. The hardware now being installed is on an annual product cadence, running harder than anything the old assumptions were built on, defended by arguments — software support, cascading workloads, scarcity — that are real but not permanent. Whether six years proves right, or four, or something nobody has assumed, is not knowable from outside, and this book does not guess.

What is knowable is the method. The lives are stated. The costs are stated. The changes, when they come, are disclosed with amounts. The arithmetic of a shortening is five minutes with a filing and a division. A reader who does that arithmetic holds something better than a forecast: the knowledge of exactly how much of the sector's reported profit rests on a forecast about machines — and machines, as this book has had occasion to say before, do not negotiate. They will run for as long as they earn their power and their floor space, and not one quarter longer. The accounts will find out in due course which estimate was closer. Your advantage is knowing, before the accounts find out, precisely where the answer will appear.
