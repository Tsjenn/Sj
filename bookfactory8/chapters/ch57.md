# Circular Revenue

At the end of June 2026 the Bank for International Settlements published its annual economic report and named the three largest threats it could see to global financial stability. One was sovereign debt. One was an artificial-intelligence capital-expenditure bust. The third was the circular financing that had grown up around it, as reported by Fortune, Business Standard and American Banker at the end of June 2026.

That is a striking place for a structure to end up. Circular arrangements between suppliers and customers are old, common and, in most of their forms, entirely lawful. The central bank of central banks does not usually take an interest in commercial credit terms. It took an interest here because of scale: the same reporting put the combined capital expenditure of the five largest hyperscale operators at more than one trillion US dollars across 2025 and 2026, and private credit lending to artificial-intelligence-related companies at over forty billion dollars in 2025 against roughly three billion in 2010. When a structure gets large enough, its ordinary properties become systemic ones.

This chapter explains the structure. It does not accuse anybody of anything. That needs saying at the top rather than the bottom, because circular revenue is the subject on which commentary in this industry is least careful, and because the accounting here is genuinely interesting and gets buried under the insinuation. What follows sets out the mechanism, what the recognition and disclosure rules actually require of it, why it is not automatically improper, what a historical precedent from the telecommunications equipment industry did and did not demonstrate, and a specific list of things a reader can check in public documents. Chapter fifty-six examined one company's disclosed investment portfolio on the same terms. This chapter is about the general shape.

## The mechanism, stated plainly

A supplier provides capital to a customer. The customer uses capital — some of it, not necessarily that capital, because money is fungible and no accounting rule pretends otherwise — to buy the supplier's product. The supplier delivers the product and recognises revenue. Both companies report growth. Measured across the pair, cash has gone out and come back.

Every step in that sequence can be entirely proper. The goods are real. They are delivered, installed, powered and used. The revenue is earned in the ordinary sense: a performance obligation was satisfied, control of an asset passed to the buyer, and the buyer has an enforceable obligation to pay. Nothing in the standards prohibits selling to a company you own part of, and nothing in commercial law requires your customers to be strangers.

The problem the structure creates is not falsity. It is that one number has been counted in two places at once, in two different companies' accounts, and neither of them is wrong to count it.

Consider what an outside reader learns from the supplier's revenue line in the ordinary case. Revenue growth is evidence that somebody, somewhere, wanted the product enough to pay for it out of money they obtained independently. That is the whole informational content of a sale to an unconnected party: it is a signal about demand that originated outside the seller. Where the buyer's money came from the seller, the signal is weaker — not absent, but weaker, and weaker by an amount that is nowhere disclosed. Revenue remains a fact. It has stopped being the same kind of evidence.

That is the entire analytical content of circularity, and it is worth stating in one sentence before the technicalities crowd it out: a circle does not make revenue fake, it makes revenue less informative about demand.

### Three things people call circular

Public discussion runs three quite different structures together. They have different accounting, different risks and different tells.

| Structure | What moves | Where it sits in the supplier's accounts | Principal risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equity in a customer | Cash out as investment; cash back as revenue | Investment asset (fair value or cost); revenue in the income statement | Correlation: the holding and the revenue fall together |
| Vendor financing | Credit, guarantee or backstop extended to a buyer | Receivable, note receivable, or a guarantee in the commitments note | Credit: the sale was made, the cash may never arrive |
| Reciprocal dealing | Each party buys from the other, often of similar value and similar timing | Revenue one way, cost or asset the other | Substance: whether anything of economic value changed hands at all |

The first is an ownership question and was the subject of the previous chapter. The second is a credit question and is the subject of the next one. The third is the one that has historically produced enforcement action, because it is the only one of the three where the underlying transaction can be hollow.

They also differ in how visible they are. An equity stake in a listed customer eventually shows up in a fair-value note. A guarantee sits in the commitments and contingencies note at the back of the report, where it is disclosed and almost never read. A reciprocal arrangement may not be separately identifiable anywhere at all, because the sale is in revenue, the purchase is in cost of sales or in property, plant and equipment, and nothing in the primary statements links them.

## Why it is not automatically improper

The instinct that a circle must be a trick is wrong, and a reader who cannot argue the other side is not equipped to assess the structure.

Suppliers have financed customers for as long as there have been suppliers. Trade credit — goods now, payment in sixty days — is vendor financing in its most ordinary form, and the entire industrial economy runs on it. Equipment manufacturers have owned finance arms for a century. Franchisors lend to franchisees. Film distributors fund exhibitors. Brewers financed public houses so thoroughly that the arrangement acquired its own name. In none of these cases does the financing make the subsequent sales fictitious.

The economic justification is usually the same and usually sound. A supplier sells into a market where the constraint on the customer is capital rather than appetite. Removing the constraint converts existing demand into a delivered order. It does not conjure demand that was absent. That distinction — financing a bottleneck versus manufacturing an appetite — is the one that separates the defensible version of this structure from the indefensible one, and it is a question about the customer's underlying business, not about the supplier's paperwork.

There is a second, weaker but genuine justification. Complementary assets are worth more when they exist together. An accelerator without a building, a grid connection and cooling is inventory. A supplier with an interest in the buildings existing is not behaving strangely by investing in the people who put them up.

And there is a third, which is simply that new markets need somebody to go first. Capital-intensive infrastructure that has never been built before is difficult to finance from conventional lenders precisely because it has never been built before. The party with the best information about whether it will work is frequently the supplier. That is an argument for the supplier bearing some of the risk, not against it.

So the honest position is that the structure is neutral. What it does is transfer risk from the customer to the supplier while transferring revenue in the other direction, and both of those effects are real, disclosed in outline, and legitimate. Whether a particular circle is healthy depends on facts that are mostly not disclosed.

### The question that actually decides it

There is one property that separates a durable circle from a fragile one, and it has nothing to do with accounting.

Every chain of transactions must terminate in a party that pays from money it earned outside the chain. Call it terminal demand. A supplier finances a data centre operator; the operator buys accelerators; the operator rents capacity to a model developer; the model developer sells subscriptions and enterprise contracts to people who are not in the industry at all. That last step is where money enters the system from outside. Everything upstream of it is a claim on it.

If terminal demand grows faster than the financing, the circle is a bridge: it pulled forward construction that end-user revenue subsequently justified. If terminal demand stalls while the financing continues, the circle is a treadmill, and the supplier ends up holding both an impaired investment and the revenue it produced.

Nothing in any set of accounts tells you which of those is happening, because no company is required to disclose what share of its customers' purchasing power originated with itself. The nearest available proxy is the growth of end-user revenue in the layer furthest from the silicon, examined over several years, which is why this book spends as long as it does on the application layer's economics.

## What the recognition rules require

Now the technical part, which is more restrictive than most commentary assumes.

**Consideration payable to a customer.** Under ASC 606, cash, credit, coupons, vouchers or equity instruments given by a seller to its customer are accounted for as a reduction of the transaction price — that is, of revenue — unless the payment is in exchange for a distinct good or service transferred to the seller, and then only up to the fair value of that distinct good or service. This is the rule that governs the whole subject, and it is a default against the seller: the presumption is discount, and it must be rebutted.

Buying shares in a customer is ordinarily a rebuttal. Shares are a distinct asset. Where they are acquired at fair value on the same terms as other participants in the same round, the investor has bought something for what it is worth, and no part of the payment is a discount on the sales. Where equity is granted to a customer for nothing, or at a price nobody else was offered, the excess is consideration payable to a customer and reduces revenue as the related sales are recognised.

The clean disclosed illustration of the second case is the warrant. On 6 October 2025 AMD and OpenAI announced a partnership to deploy six gigawatts of AMD Instinct accelerators, beginning with one gigawatt of the MI450 in the second half of 2026, alongside a warrant for OpenAI to purchase up to one hundred and sixty million AMD shares at an exercise price of one cent, vesting in tranches against purchase milestones and share-price conditions; AMD's shares rose about twenty-three per cent on the announcement, per CNBC on 6 October 2025. AMD's own filing described the warrant as a material inducement for OpenAI to enter the supply agreement. In its annual report for the year ended 27 December 2025 the company stated that none of the warrant shares had met the vesting or exercise conditions and that the warrant had no impact on its financial statements for that year.

Note what that example demonstrates, because it is the opposite of a scandal. A structure that would obviously be consideration payable to a customer was announced publicly, documented in a filed agreement, described as an inducement in the company's own words, and reported as having no profit-and-loss effect until it vests. The rule worked. The reader was told. Nothing here suggests anything improper by either party, and this book asserts nothing of the kind. What it shows is where in a filing this question is answered when it arises.

**Collectability.** Before any revenue is recognised at all, ASC 606 requires that a contract exist, and one of the criteria for a contract to exist is that it be probable the seller will collect the consideration to which it is entitled. That is a gate, applied at inception, and it is the specific gate that vendor financing pushes against. Selling to a customer who cannot pay unless you lend them the money is precisely the situation the criterion contemplates. Where collectability is not probable, revenue is deferred until cash is received or the contract is terminated, regardless of whether the goods have shipped.

**Non-monetary and reciprocal transactions.** Where a company sells to a party from which it simultaneously buys, the standards on non-monetary exchanges ask whether the exchange has commercial substance and whether fair value is reliably measurable. An exchange that lacks commercial substance — where the entity's future cash flows are not expected to change significantly as a result — is measured at the carrying amount of the asset given up, and no gain is recognised. This is the rule that reciprocal capacity dealing in the telecommunications industry was found to have breached, and it is discussed below.

**Gross versus net.** Where a supplier finances a purchase that flows through an intermediate vehicle, the question of who is principal and who is agent decides whether the full amount or only a margin appears in revenue. Presentation changes here move headline revenue by large amounts without changing profit by a cent, and a change in presentation policy is always worth reading.

**Related parties.** ASC 850 requires disclosure of transactions with related parties, including the amounts, the balances and the terms. Significant influence generally creates the relationship; a passive minority stake with no board seat frequently does not. The practical consequence is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: the structures most likely to produce a large circle are frequently the ones that fall below the disclosure threshold, because the supplier deliberately took a stake small enough not to acquire influence.

**Guarantees and commitments.** An obligation to buy a customer's unsold output, a residual-value guarantee on equipment, a backstop to a lender, or a take-or-pay commitment is not revenue, not an investment, and not on the face of any primary statement. It is disclosed in the commitments and contingencies note. It also frequently matters more than the equity, because it is the thing that let the customer raise debt from somebody else.

## An illustrative circle

Take an illustrative supplier and an illustrative customer, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real business.

The supplier invests one thousand in the customer, at fair value, alongside other investors in the same round. The customer raises four thousand in total from all sources. Over three years the customer spends three thousand with the supplier on equipment. The supplier's gross margin is sixty per cent.

| Line | Amount | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| Investment made | (1,000) | Investing cash flow; investment asset on the balance sheet |
| Revenue recognised over three years | 3,000 | Income statement, revenue |
| Cost of that revenue | (1,200) | Income statement, cost of sales |
| Gross profit | 1,800 | Income statement |
| Net cash across the whole relationship | +800 | Operating inflow of 3,000, less 1,200 of costs, less 1,000 invested |

The supplier is cash-positive on the relationship before the investment is worth anything, which is the first thing to notice: the caricature in which a company simply hands money to a customer and books it back as revenue does not survive contact with a gross margin. Sixty per cent of the sale is profit; forty per cent is real cost incurred with third parties. On these illustrative figures, one thousand of investment supported three thousand of sales and eighteen hundred of gross profit.

Now change one fact and nothing else. Suppose the other three thousand the customer raised came from two other suppliers doing exactly the same thing, and that the customer's own revenue from parties outside the industry is one hundred a year. The accounting is identical. Every entry above is still correct. The revenue is still earned, the goods still delivered, the audit still clean.

What has changed is the quality of the eighteen hundred. In the first version, the supplier's thousand was a minority of the customer's funding and the rest came from investors making an independent judgement. In the second, essentially all of the customer's purchasing power came from its own suppliers, and the terminal demand supporting a four-thousand build is one hundred a year. The first is a bridge. The second is a treadmill.

No line item distinguishes them. That is the honest conclusion of the illustration, and it is why the watch list later in this chapter is mostly about things outside the income statement.

## The telecommunications precedent

The historical rhyme is real, and it repays being told accurately rather than as a slogan. Chapter fifty-eight examines the comparison properly; what follows is the part that bears on revenue recognition specifically.

In the late 1990s, equipment makers financed the carriers building out fibre and wireless networks. TheStreet's contemporaneous reporting on the practice put Lucent's vendor-financing commitments at about seven billion US dollars, more than double Nortel's three point one billion, with Cisco at about two point four billion, and noted Nortel carrying forty-five separate vendor-financing arrangements; other compilations of the same period put Lucent's commitments higher still. The mechanism was straightforward. A loan or a loan commitment allowed a carrier to order equipment it could not otherwise buy; the equipment maker shipped, recognised revenue, and carried the exposure as a receivable.

Three things then happened, and they are worth separating because they are usually merged.

The first was ordinary credit loss. Carriers that had been financed could not pay. Receivables that had converted into revenue converted back into provisions. The scale of those write-downs is best read in the companies' own annual reports for 2001 and 2002; contemporaneous compilations disagree with one another, and this book does not repeat a figure it cannot source. Nothing about this first category was improper. The accounting was correct at the time it was done, the disclosures existed, and the losses arrived exactly as a credit exposure is supposed to arrive: late, and all at once.

The second was reciprocal dealing. Carriers sold each other indefeasible rights of use — long-term rights over specified fibre capacity — and, as demand fell away, began buying capacity from the same counterparties to whom they sold it. A United States House of Representatives Energy and Commerce Committee hearing in 2002, held under the title "Capacity Swaps by Global Crossing and Qwest: Sham Transactions Designed to Boost Revenues?", recorded that in the second quarter of 2001 reciprocal transactions accounted for thirty-two per cent of Global Crossing's reported cash revenue, and that without them the company's recurring adjusted earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation would have been negative forty-three million dollars rather than the positive four hundred and seventy-two million reported. Qwest reversed about nine hundred and fifty million dollars of swap revenue in September 2002, as reported by The Register and the Motley Fool at the time, and in 2004 settled fraud charges brought by the Securities and Exchange Commission for two hundred and fifty million dollars, the regulator having alleged that the company recognised more than three point eight billion dollars of revenue improperly between 1999 and 2002, according to Accounting Today's reporting of the settlement. The accounting argument at the heart of that matter was the non-monetary exchange rule described above: whether swapped capacity constituted an exchange of dissimilar assets, permitting revenue, or of similar ones, permitting none.

The third was individual misconduct in the recognition of specific contracts. In May 2004 Lucent agreed to pay a twenty-five million dollar penalty to settle with the Securities and Exchange Commission, without admitting or denying the allegations, over revenue the regulator said had been improperly recognised in fiscal 2000 — about one point one billion dollars in total, including one hundred and twenty-five million from a software licence arrangement with the carrier Winstar, as reported by Computerworld, NBC News and the Deseret News in May 2004. The regulator's staff indicated that the penalty itself reflected the company's conduct during the investigation rather than additional violations.

The lesson is not "circular structures are fraud". Most of the companies that extended vendor financing in that period were never charged with anything, and the largest destruction of value came from the first category, which broke no rule at all. The lesson is narrower and more useful. Where the structure failed catastrophically, the failure ran through the receivable. Where it produced enforcement, the enforcement ran through reciprocity — the cases in which the two legs of a transaction were with the same counterparty and offset each other. A reader who watches those two things is watching the right two things.

## What specifically to look for

Each of the following can be checked from public documents. None of them is evidence of wrongdoing. Each is a prompt to ask a question.

| What to check | Where it lives | What a change would mean |
|---|---|---|
| Receivables and days sales outstanding against revenue growth | Balance sheet and the receivables note | Revenue growing faster than cash collection is the oldest warning in the file |
| Notes receivable, financing receivables, contract assets and unbilled revenue | Balance sheet detail and the revenue note | Sales being converted into credit exposure rather than cash |
| Guarantees, backstops, residual-value guarantees, offtake and take-or-pay commitments | Commitments and contingencies note | Risk borne without an asset recognised; frequently larger than the equity |
| Accounting policy for consideration payable to a customer | Significant accounting policies note | Its appearance means the question has become live |
| Warrants or equity granted to customers | Share capital note, dilution reconciliation, subsequent events | Inducements that will reduce revenue as they vest |
| Related-party note | Its own note, or its absence | A stake crossing into significant influence changes what must be disclosed |
| Customer concentration | Segment and concentration disclosures | Concentration rising alongside an investment programme concentrates two risks in one name |
| Critical audit matters | Auditor's report | Tells you what the auditor found hardest, which is often the answer |
| Purchases from entities that are also customers | Commitments note and cost of sales discussion | Reciprocity is the pattern with the worst history |
| Gross versus net presentation, and any change to it | Revenue policy note | Moves headline revenue without moving profit |

Two of these deserve expansion.

**Cash, not revenue.** The most reliable discipline available to an outside reader is to stop reading the income statement and read the cash flow statement beside it. Revenue can be recognised in a period in which no cash arrives; a receivable is the difference. Where a supplier's operating cash flow tracks its reported profit closely over several years, financing of customers is not distorting the picture much, whatever the equity stakes look like. Where the gap widens persistently, something is being sold on terms that have not yet been settled in money. This is not a sophisticated technique. It is the first thing an accountant does and it survives every innovation in deal structuring, because cash is the one thing that cannot be recognised early.

**Follow the guarantee, not the stake.** Public attention concentrates on equity because equity has a headline number and a press release. In practice, the commitment that most changes a customer's behaviour is often the one that let it borrow: a promise to buy unsold capacity, a floor under the value of equipment, a support letter to a lender. Those obligations do not appear in a portfolio table, do not move with the share price, and carry an exposure that only becomes visible when it crystallises. Chapter fifty-six set out one disclosed example of that form. The general point is that the commitments note describes a different and often larger relationship than the investment note does, and a reader who reads only one of them is reading the smaller half.

## What none of this establishes

The accounts will not tell you what proportion of a supplier's revenue came from customers it has financed. No standard requires that disclosure, no company volunteers it, and no amount of reasoning from outside will produce it reliably. They will not tell you whether a price paid by an investee differed from the price paid by anyone else. They will not tell you whether an investment and a supply agreement were negotiated as one arrangement or two, which is the judgement on which the revenue treatment turns, and which is made by management and tested by the auditor before any reader sees a figure. Chapter sixty-one takes up what that testing looks like from the auditor's side.

Where a question has no answer in the public record, the answer is that it is not disclosed. Supplying a guess instead is not analysis, and the confidence with which circular structures are discussed in public is largely a function of how comfortable people have become with guessing.

Every figure in this chapter carries a date, and several of them are historical for a reason: the settled cases are the only ones where the full facts are on the record. The 2026 figures — the capital-expenditure totals, the private-credit numbers, the warrant that had not yet vested — are snapshots and will have moved. The current versions are in the most recent annual and quarterly filings of the companies concerned, in the revenue policy note, the receivables note, the commitments and contingencies note, and the auditor's report. Nothing in this chapter is investment advice, and nothing in it is a view on the merits of any security.

## The honest summary

Circular revenue is not a category of fraud. It is a category of reduced information. When a supplier funds its customer, the revenue it subsequently recognises is real, earned, delivered and correctly stated, and it tells an outside reader less about the strength of demand than the same revenue would from an unconnected buyer. That is the entire structural fact, and everything else in this chapter follows from it.

Two consequences follow for anyone reading these accounts. The first is that growth and asset values in such a supplier are correlated rather than independent, so good news arrives twice and must not be counted twice. The second is that the interesting disclosures are at the back of the report — the commitments, the guarantees, the receivables, the related parties — rather than at the front, where the revenue line is.

The telecommunications equipment industry demonstrated, twenty-five years ago, that this structure can operate for years with correct accounting and then fail through the receivable rather than through the revenue. It also demonstrated, in a smaller number of specific cases, what happens when reciprocity replaces trade. Those are different failures with different tells, and conflating them is the commonest error in the current discussion.

Chapter fifty-eight takes the comparison seriously and states both sides of it: the case that today's structures are meaningfully different from the vendor financing of the late 1990s, and the case that they are not. As in this chapter, the verdict belongs to the reader, and it should be reached with the filings open.
