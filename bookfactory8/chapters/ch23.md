# What Compute Means on a Balance Sheet

In Richland Parish, Louisiana, on a site of roughly two thousand two hundred and fifty acres, Meta is building a data centre campus called Hyperion: about four million square feet of planned buildings, a design capacity the company has expanded to five gigawatts, and a stated investment of more than fifty billion US dollars — up from a prior figure of roughly twenty-seven billion when the campus was first announced (Meta's published project description and CNBC, July 2026). Fifty billion dollars is more than the annual capital budget of most national governments. It is being spent on one campus, in one parish, by one company.

Now ask the question this book exists to ask: when that money is spent, where does it go in the accounts?

Almost none of it goes where a casual reader would look. It does not appear as a cost when it is spent. It lands on the balance sheet, as property, plant and equipment, and from there it is released into the profit and loss account in instalments, over lives that management estimates and auditors accept. Chapter four walked through that mechanism from the accounts side — the three statements, the footnotes, where the estimate hides. This chapter walks it from the loading dock. Part Three of this book is about the compute layer, the businesses that turn capital into rentable hours, and there is no way to understand any of them without first taking one of these buildings apart and sorting the pieces into the categories an accountant actually uses.

Because "a data centre" is not an asset. It is at least five very different assets wearing one building, and they age at wildly different speeds.

## Sorting the campus into asset classes

Walk the site from the fence inward and you pass through the categories in order of how long they last.

First the land, which lasts forever and is never depreciated at all. Then the shell — the structure, the roof, the slab — which is measured in decades. Then the power and cooling plant: substations, transformers, switchgear, generators, chillers, pipework, the equipment that turns a building into a machine for delivering electricity to silicon and taking heat away from it. Then the halls themselves, filled with racks of servers and accelerators — the most expensive contents and much the shortest-lived. Threaded through everything, the network: switches, optics, and cabling that tie tens of thousands of processors into one machine and connect that machine to the world.

Each class is capitalised — recorded as an asset rather than charged as a cost — and each is then depreciated over an assumed useful life that the company chooses, discloses, and revisits. The choice is different for each class, as it should be: concrete and silicon do not age alike. The table below sets out the anatomy in the form the rest of this chapter follows.

| Asset class | What it includes | How the accounts treat it | The life question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Land | The site | Capitalised; never depreciated | None — land does not wear out |
| Shell and buildings | Structure, roof, slab, fit-out | Depreciated over decades | Long, and recently getting longer |
| Power and cooling | Substations, transformers, generators, chillers, liquid-cooling plant | Depreciated over long lives, largely tied to the building | Long, but new cooling is tied to specific hardware generations |
| Servers and accelerators | CPUs, GPUs, memory, storage | Depreciated over roughly four to six years, by company | The contested estimate, and the biggest number |
| Networking | Switches, optics, cabling | Depreciated over medium lives, often alongside servers | Follows the accelerator cadence more than the building |

Before the tour, one boundary question, because everything else rests on it: what gets onto the balance sheet at all? The rule is old and simple in principle. A cost is capitalised when it buys something the company controls and expects to benefit from beyond the current period, and the directly attributable costs of getting the asset ready for use go in with it — delivery, installation, testing, and in the right circumstances the interest on money borrowed during construction. Routine repairs and maintenance are expensed as incurred. Electricity is expensed. The salaries of the people who run the site are expensed. In an ordinary business the boundary is dull. In a business assembling hundred-and-twenty-kilowatt racks by the thousand, it is a live judgement exercised constantly: replacing a failed compute tray inside a rack is plausibly a repair, and plausibly the substitution of one asset for another; re-plumbing a hall from air to liquid cooling is plausibly maintenance of a building, and plausibly an improvement that extends its life and belongs on the balance sheet with a life of its own. Each judgement moves cost between "charged now" and "charged over years". None of them moves cash at all.

One number tells you where the weight sits. Microsoft said in July 2026 that roughly two-thirds of its recent capital expenditure had gone to short-lived assets — principally CPUs and GPUs — rather than to buildings and infrastructure (company commentary, fourth-quarter fiscal 2026 earnings call, July 2026). Read that carefully. The public argument about data centres is conducted in pictures of buildings, but two of every three dollars are not buying buildings. They are buying the contents, and the contents carry the shortest lives and the most consequential estimates in the filing.

Hold that split in mind through everything that follows. The campus is mostly money that lasts; the bill is mostly money that doesn't.

## Land and shell: the part that lasts

Start with the slowest layer, because it is the easiest to account for and the easiest to misread.

Land is the one item on the entire campus that never touches the income statement. It is capitalised at cost and sits there. Whatever was paid for those acres in Louisiana will still be on Meta's balance sheet, at that cost, decades from now, unless the site is sold or written down. In an industry where every other number decays on a schedule, land is the exception, which is one reason operators talk so much about "land banks": acreage with power access is an asset that holds its accounting value by definition and — though the accounts will never say so — may hold its economic value better than anything else on the site.

The shell above it is depreciated, but slowly. Alphabet's annual filings put data centre and office buildings at seven to forty years (Alphabet Form 10-K for 2025, filed 2026). Microsoft said in July 2026 that from its fiscal 2027 it would carry data centre and office buildings at twenty-five years rather than fifteen (company commentary reported July 2026). A building on a twenty-five-year life sheds only four per cent of its cost into the profit and loss account each year. The shell is the calm part of the accounts.

It is not, any longer, the cheap part of the story it once was. Turner & Townsend's 2025 data-centre construction cost index recorded a five and a half per cent rise in the cost per watt of building a conventional air-cooled facility, and put the construction premium for comparable liquid-cooled capacity at around seven to ten per cent (Turner & Townsend data-centre construction cost index, 2025). Quartz, reporting industry cost data for the twelve months to November 2025, put the average data centre built in that period at five hundred and ninety-seven million US dollars, or nine hundred and sixty dollars per square foot, against three hundred and seventy-four million and five hundred and thirty-four dollars per square foot a year earlier (Quartz, 2026). Costs per building roughly doubling in a year is not inflation; it is the buildings themselves changing — bigger, denser, plumbed for liquid.

And here is the awkwardness an accountant should point out. The shell is the longest-lived asset on the site, and it is also the asset most specifically shaped by assumptions about the shortest-lived one. A hall is designed around a power density: how many kilowatts each rack position can be fed and cooled. Current rack-scale AI systems draw on the order of a hundred and twenty kilowatts each, against single-digit kilowatts for the ordinary servers of a decade ago (Nvidia's published specifications for its GB200 NVL72 system, reported from 2024). A hall built for one density does not gracefully accept the other. So a building carried in the accounts for twenty-five years is, physically, a bet on what the machines of the 2040s will need in power and cooling — made today, in concrete, by people who do not know what the machines of 2030 will need. Nothing in the depreciation schedule expresses that risk. It sits silently inside a long life and a low annual charge.

## Power and cooling: the part you queue for

The industry has started describing itself in a unit that does not appear anywhere in the accounts: the gigawatt. Hyperion is "five gigawatts". Meta's other flagship, a campus called Prometheus in New Albany, Ohio, was reported at a gigawatt, brought online at speed under tent-like structures with two to four hundred megawatts of its own behind-the-meter gas generation (trade reporting including Data Center Frontier, 2025). When a company measures its factories by their electricity intake rather than their floor area or their cost, it is telling you what the binding constraint is.

The balance-sheet translation of a gigawatt is the power and cooling plant: the substation that steps the grid down, the transformers and switchgear that distribute it, the generators that back it up, and the chillers, pumps and pipework that carry the heat away. These are long-lived assets in the accounting sense — they sit in the longer depreciation bands, closer to the building than to the servers — and they are utterly unglamorous, and they are currently among the hardest objects on earth to buy. Lead times for high-capacity power transformers in the United States were reported in May 2026 at as long as four years, with Wood Mackenzie modelling an estimated thirty per cent shortfall in power-transformer supply for 2025 (pv magazine USA, May 2026). A four-year queue for a transformer means the electrical spine of a campus must be ordered before the design of the machines it will feed is even public. Capital committed on that timetable is a forecast wearing a purchase order.

Cooling deserves its own line, because it has crossed a threshold. For most of the industry's history, cooling meant air: fans, ducts, chillers, a building-level concern. The current generation of accelerators has made it a rack-level concern, plumbed directly to the silicon. A Morgan Stanley bill-of-materials estimate reported in November 2025 put the liquid-cooling components of a single GB300 NVL72 rack at forty-nine thousand eight hundred and sixty US dollars, rising about seventeen per cent to some fifty-five thousand seven hundred for the next-generation NVL144 design (reported by Tom's Hardware, November 2025). Fifty thousand dollars of pipework per rack is a new kind of object for the accounts: physically part of the building's plumbing, economically part of a specific chip generation. Whether its life follows the building or the silicon is exactly the kind of classification question that sounds pedantic and moves reported profit.

How efficiently the plant runs has its own metric: power usage effectiveness, or PUE, the ratio of everything the site draws to what the computing itself consumes. A PUE of exactly one would mean no overhead at all. Google reported a fleet-wide average of 1.09 for 2025 (Google's published data-centre efficiency figures, 2025), meaning nine per cent overhead — a figure the rest of the industry does not uniformly match. PUE never appears in a financial statement, but it quietly prices every other line: the closer it gets to one, the more of that hard-won grid connection reaches revenue-earning silicon rather than the chillers.

For scale, the International Energy Agency's Energy and AI analysis put global data-centre electricity demand at around nine hundred and forty-five terawatt-hours by 2030, a figure introduced in chapter one along with the reasons to handle such forecasts carefully. The point here is narrower: electricity itself is never capitalised. The plant that delivers it sits on the balance sheet for decades; the power that flows through it is an operating cost, charged as incurred, forever. A data centre is a machine for converting one of those into the other.

## Servers and accelerators: the part that moves the profit and loss

Now the contents — the two-thirds.

The unit of purchase is no longer a server but a rack-scale system. Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 integrates seventy-two GPUs and thirty-six of the company's own CPUs into a single liquid-cooled rack drawing on the order of a hundred and twenty kilowatts; trade-press estimates during 2024 and 2025 put its price at around three million US dollars, with a spread of roughly two to three million across sources (TechRadar and other trade reporting, 2024 and 2025). Treat that price with the caution it deserves — Nvidia publishes no list price, and large buyers negotiate — but the order of magnitude is enough. A single rack now costs about as much as an entire small office building, occupies a few square metres, and is expected by its own maker to face a successor within about a year.

The other side of the same transaction was recorded, audited, in the seller's accounts: Nvidia's data centre revenue was one hundred and ninety-seven point three billion US dollars in its fiscal year to 25 January 2026 (company results announcement, February 2026). Nearly every dollar of that revenue became, on the day it was booked, an addition to some buyer's property, plant and equipment — an asset that will be released into the buyers' costs over the four, five or six years that follow. One company's income statement this year is the whole industry's depreciation schedule for the rest of the decade. There are few places in accounting where you can watch a cost of that size cross from one layer of an industry to another and change character on the way.

And this is the class where the useful-life estimate does almost all of its work, because this is where the money is and where the lives are shortest. What the large operators actually assume, as disclosed in their filings and announcements, is worth setting out side by side.

| Company | Assumed life for servers | Set when | Direction of travel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microsoft | Up to six years for server and network equipment | Assessment completed July 2022, effective fiscal 2023; raised from four (Microsoft disclosures, 2022, as reported by Computer Weekly) | Lengthened twice since 2020 |
| Alphabet | Six years | January 2023, raised from four; certain network equipment from five to six (Alphabet Form 10-K for 2023) | Lengthened twice |
| Meta | Five and a half years for certain servers and network assets | January 2025, up from four to five previously used (Meta disclosure, January 2025, reported by Yahoo Finance) | Lengthened |
| Amazon | Five years for a subset of servers and networking equipment | Effective 1 January 2025, cut from six, citing the pace of AI technology development (Amazon disclosures, 2025) | The one recent shortening |

Four sophisticated operators, buying broadly similar hardware from the same handful of suppliers, carrying it at four different assumptions, one of them moving in the opposite direction to the other three. Nothing in that table is improper; every entry was disclosed, reasoned and audited. But the table itself is the finding. If the economic life of this equipment were observable, these numbers would agree. They are estimates, and the spread between them is the honest measure of how much nobody knows.

## Networking: the part in between

Between the servers and the world sits the network, and it is easy to skip because it is a minority of the bill and photographs poorly. Skip it and you misread the industry's economics twice over.

An AI campus runs two networks. The front-end network connects the site to customers and to the rest of the operator's estate; it resembles the networking of any large cloud. The back-end network is different in kind: it stitches thousands of accelerators into a single machine, so that a model can be trained across all of them at once, and its bandwidth requirements scale with the ambition of the models rather than with customer traffic. It is built from switches and optical transceivers in extraordinary quantities, and it is bought, installed and — critically — refreshed on the accelerators' cadence, not the building's.

The scale is now visible in the seller's accounts again. Nvidia reported networking revenue of eleven billion US dollars in its fourth quarter of fiscal 2026 alone, up two hundred and sixty-three per cent year on year, driven by its NVLink compute fabric, Ethernet and InfiniBand platforms (Nvidia fourth-quarter fiscal 2026 results commentary, February 2026). Networking was for years the forgettable line in the industry's spending; it is now a business that would, on its own, rank among the larger equipment companies in the world.

The accounting treatment follows the physics. Network equipment is capitalised alongside the servers and carried at similar or slightly longer lives — Alphabet moved certain network equipment from five years to six in the same January 2023 assessment that moved its servers (Alphabet Form 10-K for 2023), and Amazon's January 2025 shortening covered networking equipment as well as servers (Amazon disclosures, 2025). The practical point for a reader is that "servers" in the useful-life footnote is usually shorthand for servers-and-network, and the two move together. When you think about whether a six-year life is realistic, you are also being asked to believe it for an optical transceiver sitting behind a chip generation that turns over annually.

## The depreciation assumption: the number that ties it together

Every class above ends at the same estimate, so state it plainly and give it the prominence it will keep for the rest of this book.

Assumed useful life is the number of years over which each asset class's cost is charged against profit. It is chosen by management, disclosed in a sentence, reviewed annually, and applied prospectively when it changes — no restatement of prior years, no catch-up charge, as chapter four explained. It is the single assumption through which this entire chapter's inventory — the shell, the transformers, the chilled pipework, the racks, the optics — becomes reported profit or the absence of it.

To see how hard the assumption works, take an illustrative rack, using the reported price above and round numbers for everything else; the arithmetic belongs to no real company. A three-million-dollar rack, no residual value, straight-line. On a four-year life it charges seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars a year against profit. On a six-year life, five hundred thousand. The difference is a quarter of a million dollars a year, per rack, produced by a sentence in a footnote. Now let the illustrative operator run ten thousand such racks — a fraction of a single large campus at current densities. The gap between the two assumptions is now two and a half billion dollars of annual reported operating profit. The racks are identical. The electricity bill is identical. The bank balance is identical to the cent. Only the estimate differs.

That is not a hypothetical mechanism; the disclosed effects at real companies are the same arithmetic at fleet scale. Meta expected its January 2025 move to five and a half years to reduce that year's depreciation by approximately two point nine billion US dollars (Meta disclosure, January 2025). Amazon's shortening in the same month ran the other way: the outturn for 2025 was an increase in depreciation and amortisation of about one point four billion US dollars and a reduction in net income of about one billion (Amazon's 2025 annual filing, as analysed by Hudson Labs). Chapter four set out the full decade of these changes and the public argument about them; chapter thirty-two will weigh the evidence on what these machines' economic lives actually are. The job of this chapter is only to fix the anatomy in place: the estimate attaches class by class — never depreciated at all at the fence line, forty years in the concrete, six years or five and a half or five or four in the racks — and the shorter the life, the bigger the money it governs.

Notice, finally, what the assumption cannot reach. It cannot reach the land. It cannot reach the cash, which left when the assets were bought. It cannot reach the electricity, which is expensed as it burns. It governs exactly the middle of the campus — the plant and the machines — which is to say, it governs the part of the business that the compute layer actually is.

## What to carry into the rest of Part Three

The chapters that follow examine the compute businesses one at a time: Microsoft's Azure, Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud and its own silicon, Oracle's second act, the new specialist operators, the megaprojects, the power problem, and then — once the companies are on the table — the depreciation question in full. Every one of those chapters stands on the anatomy laid out here, and each will keep asking the same short list of questions. What did the company capitalise, and into which classes? What lives has it assumed, class by class, and have they moved? What did any change do to reported profit, in dollars, per the company's own disclosure? And what did the cash do while the estimates were doing whatever they did?

A note on dates, as always. The figures in this chapter are snapshots: construction costs were rising as they were measured, transformer queues were lengthening, rack prices are negotiated and unpublished, and at least one major operator changed its useful-life assumption in each of the last several years. The current versions live in each company's most recent annual report — the property and equipment note, the useful-lives sentence in the accounting policies, and the capital expenditure line in the cash flow statement — and none of this, here or anywhere in this book, is advice to buy or sell anything.

What should stay with you is the shape. A data centre is a stack of clocks running at different speeds: land that never ticks, a shell counting in decades, electrical plant counting in decades that must be ordered years ahead, machines counting in low single digits, and a network that ticks with the machines. Reported profit depends on management's statement of what time it is on each clock. The rest of this Part is about the companies that own the clocks — and the number to watch, in every one of them, is the life they assume for the fastest one.
