# Reading a Chip Company's Accounts

Open the latest annual report of any listed semiconductor company and you are holding between one hundred and three hundred pages. Perhaps a dozen of them will answer the question you actually came with. The rest is legal boilerplate, governance tables and the auditor's engagement terms. The skill this chapter teaches is not reading faster. It is knowing which dozen pages, in what order, and what each number means once you find it.

This is the toolkit chapter, so it names no companies' figures. Everything here applies to the design houses, the foundries, the memory makers and the equipment suppliers alike, with the differences flagged where they matter. Later parts of the book apply this toolkit to real filings, with dates and sources attached. Nothing in this chapter, or in this book, is investment advice. It is instruction in reading.

## Where the numbers actually live

The filing you want depends on where the company is listed and where it is domiciled.

A US domestic filer publishes an annual report on Form 10-K and quarterly reports on Form 10-Q, with material events reported as they happen on Form 8-K. Inside the 10-K, three items carry almost all of the analytical content: Item 1A, Risk Factors, required under Regulation S-K Item 105; Item 7, Management's Discussion and Analysis, required under Item 303; and Item 8, the audited financial statements and the notes. Item 1, Business, is worth one careful read the first time and rarely again.

A foreign private issuer is a different animal. It files an annual report on Form 20-F, due within four months of its financial year end, and it is not subject to the US quarterly reporting rules at all. Instead it furnishes material information on Form 6-K as that information is released at home or to its primary exchange. If you are used to four evenly spaced US quarters, the rhythm of a 20-F filer will feel sparse, and the useful disclosure usually reaches you first through the home-market release that the 6-K forwards.

Two habits close that gap. Read the company's home-market filings directly, and learn the local calendar. Taiwan requires its listed companies to file operating revenue for the preceding month by the tenth day of the following month, under the Taiwan Stock Exchange's continuing obligations. No other major market demands revenue disclosure at that frequency. For an industry whose centre of gravity sits in Taiwan, that produces a monthly public read on demand roughly six weeks ahead of anyone's quarterly statement.

Two more places are systematically underused.

The first is the auditor's report, which most readers skip because they assume it says the same thing every year. Since Auditing Standard 3101, critical audit matters have been required in the auditor's report — for large accelerated filers, for financial years ending on or after 30 June 2019, and for most other filers for years ending on or after 15 December 2020. A critical audit matter is one communicated to the audit committee that relates to something material and involved especially challenging, subjective or complex auditor judgement. In plain terms: the auditor is naming the estimate that was hardest. In this industry that is frequently inventory valuation or revenue with variable consideration. When the critical audit matter changes from one year to the next, something has changed in the business, and the change is usually not advertised in the results release.

The second is the prior year's filing, read alongside this year's. The highest-yield technique in reading any annual report is to compare the risk factors line by line against the previous edition. Companies rarely delete a risk factor casually or add one for fun; each addition has been through the disclosure committee and counsel. A new paragraph about a single customer, a new sentence about a supplier's capacity, a newly specific description of a purchase commitment — these are the places where the business changes before the numbers do.

## Gross margin: the scoreboard of pricing power

Gross margin is revenue less cost of goods sold, divided by revenue. In a semiconductor business it is the most informative single line in the accounts, because it compresses four separate contests into one percentage.

The four are pricing and mix, yield, utilisation, and input cost. A company can improve gross margin by charging more for the same part, by selling a richer mix of parts, by getting more good die out of each wafer, by running its plant closer to full, or by paying less for wafers, substrates, memory and test time. Those are different achievements with very different durability, and the gross margin line does not distinguish between them. The MD&A usually does, in a paragraph most readers skim.

Where the cost sits matters enormously, and it differs by business model.

For a company that owns its plant, depreciation of that plant sits inside cost of goods sold. That single fact drives most of the industry's behaviour. Depreciation does not fall when demand does. A plant running at half its capacity earns half the revenue against nearly the same fixed charge, so margin collapses faster than sales. The reverse is also true, which is why a recovery in this industry looks violent in the margin line before it looks impressive in the revenue line.

Take an illustrative plant, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. Assume fixed costs of 1,000 a year, almost all depreciation and the staff needed to keep the tools running, and a variable cost of 200 for every 1,000 of revenue produced. At full output of 3,000 of revenue, cost of goods sold is 1,000 plus 600, gross profit is 1,400, and gross margin is roughly 47 per cent. Now cut output to 2,000 of revenue. Fixed cost is unchanged at 1,000, variable cost falls to 400, gross profit is 600, and gross margin is 30 per cent. Revenue fell by a third; gross margin fell by seventeen points. Nothing about the company's products, customers or engineering changed. Only the denominator did.

For a company that owns no plant, the wafer is a purchase rather than a fixed cost. Its gross margin measures a two-sided negotiation: what it can charge its customers, and what its foundry charges it. A fabless company reporting stable margin through a foundry price rise is telling you it passed the rise on, which is a statement about its bargaining position that no press release will make explicitly.

| What moved | Where you see it | How durable |
|---|---|---|
| Price per unit | MD&A narrative; rarely quantified | Durable while supply is short, not after |
| Product mix | Segment or product-line revenue split | Durable if driven by design wins |
| Yield | Almost never disclosed; inferred from margin with flat price | Durable — a permanent cost reduction |
| Utilisation | Sometimes disclosed by plant owners; otherwise inferred | Cyclical, reverses fully |
| Input cost | Cost of revenue discussion; supply agreements note | Depends who holds the contract |
| Sale of previously written-down inventory | Inventory note and MD&A, if disclosed | One-off, does not repeat |

That last line deserves its own paragraph, because it is the kind of thing only somebody reading the notes will catch. Under US GAAP, inventory is carried at the lower of cost and net realisable value, and when it is written down the reduced amount becomes a new cost basis. ASC 330 prohibits reversing the write-down even if conditions improve. So if a company writes inventory down in a bad year and then sells that same inventory in a good one, the parts leave the building carrying an artificially low cost, and the whole of the proceeds falls through to gross profit. Reported margin rises. Nothing about pricing power has changed.

Under IFRS the treatment is different: IAS 2 requires a previous write-down to be reversed, up to original cost, when the circumstances that caused it no longer apply. The reversal goes through profit in the period of recovery rather than through a flattered margin in later periods. Identical commercial facts, two accounting frameworks, two different-looking recoveries. When you compare a US filer with an IFRS filer through a downturn and out the other side, you are not comparing like with like unless you have read both inventory notes.

## Capital expenditure: a bet placed years before the revenue

Capital expenditure appears in the investing section of the cash flow statement, usually as purchases of property, plant and equipment. It is the most consequential number in the industry and the most misread, because it is a cash outflow today for a revenue line that does not exist yet.

The lag is not months. Published fab construction analyses in 2026 put the span from groundbreaking to volume production at roughly three to five years, and the Semiconductor Industry Association has made the same point in plainer language for years: capacity cannot be added quickly. Permitting and site work come first, then eighteen months or so of physical construction, then cleanroom commissioning and tool installation, then a yield ramp that takes many months more before the plant produces sellable volume at an acceptable cost. Money committed in one year is therefore a statement about demand three years out, made by people who cannot know. That is the sense in which capital expenditure in this industry is a bet. It is also why the industry's shortages and gluts are structural rather than a failure of management: the decision and the consequence are separated by longer than the cycle itself.

Two ratios turn the raw number into information.

The first is capital intensity: capital expenditure divided by revenue. It tells you how much a company must spend to stand still and grow. Design houses run low. Plant owners run high. Comparing the two on this measure is meaningless; comparing a plant owner against its own history is not.

The second is capital expenditure divided by depreciation. Depreciation is roughly what the company is consuming of its existing asset base each year. Spending materially more than that is expansion. Spending materially less is harvesting — running the existing base and letting it age. Neither is good or bad in itself, but the direction of travel usually tells you what management believes about the next three years more honestly than the outlook statement does.

Take an illustrative company, again with invented round numbers. It has 10,000 of gross plant, depreciated over five years, so depreciation is 2,000 a year. If it spends 3,500 on capital expenditure, it is adding capacity faster than it is consuming it, and you should expect depreciation to rise in future years, compressing gross margin until the new capacity is filled. If it spends 900, reported profit will look healthier in the short term precisely because it is spending less, and the constraint will show up later as an inability to supply. Neither of those futures is visible in the current year's profit figure. Both are visible in the cash flow statement now.

Capital spending also hides in places outside the capital expenditure line, and each hiding place is worth checking:

- **Prepayments to equipment suppliers.** Cash paid years ahead for tool slots, sitting in other assets or prepayments rather than in plant.
- **Capitalised interest.** Borrowing costs on assets under construction are added to the asset's carrying amount rather than expensed, which raises reported profit during the build and raises depreciation afterwards.
- **Right-of-use assets.** Leased buildings and equipment produce an asset and a liability rather than a capital expenditure outflow, so a leasing company and a buying company can look very different on a cash flow statement while operating identically.
- **Government grants.** Under IAS 20 a grant related to an asset may be presented either as a reduction of the asset's carrying amount or as deferred income released over the asset's life. The choice does not change cash but does change reported depreciation, and therefore gross margin, for years afterwards.
- **Customer advances.** Money received in advance to reserve capacity sits as a liability, not as negative capital expenditure, and it means the customer is financing the plant.

Assets under construction is a line worth watching on its own. It is capital spent but not yet depreciating. A large and growing balance is a queue of future depreciation waiting to be switched on, usually in the same period the company hopes to be recognising the revenue that justifies it. The assumed life over which all of it will be depreciated is a separate and much larger subject, and this book returns to it at length when it reaches the compute layer, where the assumption does the most work.

## Inventory and days: the earliest warning of a turn

Inventory is where a downturn shows up first, generally two quarters before it reaches the revenue line, because it is the physical record of the difference between what a company built and what it sold.

The note splits inventory into three buckets and the split matters more than the total.

| Bucket | What it is | What a rise usually means |
|---|---|---|
| Raw materials | Blank wafers, substrates, chemicals, purchased components | Building ahead of a planned ramp, or a supply agreement being fulfilled on schedule |
| Work in progress | Wafers in the line, and finished wafers held before packaging and test | Deliberate buffering, or output built before demand is confirmed |
| Finished goods | Packaged, tested, sellable parts | Products already made that nobody has yet bought |

The healthy pattern is a rise concentrated in raw materials and work in progress ahead of a stated ramp. The pattern that should slow you down is a rise concentrated in finished goods while revenue growth is flat or slowing. Held product is the most expensive form of optimism in this industry, because parts have short commercial lives and a written-down part can never be written back up under US GAAP.

The standard measure is days of inventory: inventory divided by cost of goods sold, multiplied by 365. It converts a balance into a period of supply, which makes companies of different sizes comparable and makes one company comparable with its own past. Two refinements make it more useful. Compare the growth rate of inventory against the growth rate of revenue over the same period; when inventory grows faster for two consecutive quarters without a stated ramp behind it, the company is building for demand it has not yet seen. And compare days of inventory against what management has guided for next quarter. Inventory is a forecast written in physical goods, and you can check it against the forecast written in words.

Then there is the inventory that never appears on the balance sheet at all, which in this industry is a great deal of it.

The first category is channel inventory. Under ASC 606, sales to distributors are generally recognised on shipment to the distributor, the sell-in model, rather than when the distributor resells to an end customer. Many companies previously recognised on sell-through. The consequence is that product sitting in a distributor's warehouse has already been recognised as revenue and has already left the manufacturer's inventory. Where distribution agreements carry price protection, ship-and-debit arrangements, stock rotation rights or rights of return, the manufacturer must estimate the eventual consideration and recognise that estimate up front, with returns shown as a refund liability and a corresponding asset for the right to recover the product. All of that is estimate. All of it is disclosed in the revenue recognition policy note, and none of it is in the headline.

The second category is purchase commitments — supply the company has agreed to buy but has not yet received. These are the mirror image of inventory risk and can be far larger. ASC 440 requires disclosure of unconditional purchase obligations that are non-cancellable or cancellable only in specified circumstances, including the fixed and determinable portion in aggregate and, where determinable, for each of the five succeeding financial years, together with the nature of any variable components. Note where this now lives. In November 2020 the SEC eliminated the tabular contractual obligations disclosure in Item 303 of Regulation S-K, on the reasoning that the financial statements covered it; calendar-year filers were required to comply from 2022. So the familiar summary table has disappeared from many MD&A sections. Material cash requirements from known contractual obligations remain part of the liquidity discussion, but the specific numbers now sit in the commitments note. If you are still looking for the table, you will conclude the commitments no longer exist. They do.

For a company that has pre-purchased capacity from a foundry or a memory supplier, the purchase commitments note is often the most important disclosure in the entire filing, because it quantifies what the company must pay whether or not the demand arrives.

## Customer concentration: the risk nobody puts on the front page

Under ASC 280, a public entity must disclose the fact that revenues from a single external customer amount to ten per cent or more of its total revenues, the total amount of revenue from each such customer, and which segment reports it. Two details in the standard shape what you actually see. Entities known to be under common control are treated as one customer, as are the federal government, a state or local government, and a foreign government. And the entity need not disclose the customer's identity.

So you will typically learn that Customer A represented some percentage of revenue and Customer B some other percentage, with no names. That is compliance working exactly as written. What it means for you is that concentration must be read as a structure rather than a roster.

The disclosure appears in up to three places, and they do not always agree in an obvious way. The segment note carries the revenue concentration. The financial instruments or credit risk note carries concentration in accounts receivable, which is concentration measured at a different moment and can reveal a customer whose purchasing has just accelerated or stalled. Item 1A carries the narrative version, and that is where a company will tell you, in the mandatory prose, that the loss of one or a small number of customers would materially affect it.

Three complications are worth internalising.

First, the disclosed customer is the entity that is billed, which is not necessarily the entity that decided to buy. Sales routed through a distributor or a contract manufacturer are attributed to that intermediary. One large disclosed customer may be a dozen unrelated end buyers behind a single assembler, in which case concentration overstates the risk. Equally, several disclosed customers may be serving one end buyer, in which case concentration understates it. The filing will tell you which arrangement applies only if it chooses to.

Second, geography is subject to the same problem. ASC 280 requires revenue attributed to the country of domicile and to all foreign countries in total, with material individual foreign countries shown separately, and it requires the entity to disclose the basis on which it attributes revenue. Companies commonly attribute on ship-to destination. Ship-to destination is where the goods were delivered, which in this industry is very often where they were assembled into something else, not where the end demand was. A large revenue figure attributed to a small country with a large assembly industry is a fact about logistics, not about customers. Read the basis-of-attribution sentence before drawing any conclusion from the geography table.

Third, concentration changes the meaning of every other number in the accounts. A supplier with one dominant customer has less room on price, which shows in gross margin; it may carry longer receivable days, which shows in the cash conversion cycle; and it may hold inventory that is specific to that customer's design, which cannot be resold if the relationship ends. When you find high concentration, go back and re-read the margin and inventory discussion with that fact in hand.

A related structure appears increasingly in this industry and deserves care rather than suspicion. A company may hold an equity investment in a customer, or supply a customer that is simultaneously an investor, or accept a prepayment from a customer that funds the capacity used to serve that customer. None of these is improper, and none of them implies anything about a particular company. But each of them means that some portion of reported revenue is connected to a relationship that is not purely arm's length, and each of them is disclosed — in the related-party note, in the equity investments note, or in the contract liabilities line. What to watch is whether the disclosure lets you separate the connected revenue from the rest. Where it does not, the honest conclusion is that you cannot tell, and the honest thing to write is that you cannot tell.

## R&D as a share of revenue

Research and development is the cost of remaining in business at the next process node or the next architecture. It behaves quite differently from every other cost in these accounts, because a company can cut it substantially this year with no visible effect until three years from now.

The accounting differs by framework. Under ASC 730 in US GAAP, research and development costs are expensed as incurred, with narrow exceptions for certain software costs. Under IAS 38 in IFRS, research is expensed but development costs must be capitalised once six criteria are met — technical feasibility, intention to complete, ability to use or sell, probable future economic benefits, adequate resources, and reliable measurement. The consequence is direct: two companies spending identically can report different profit and different assets purely because of where they are listed. When you compare an IFRS filer with a US filer on operating margin, check the intangible assets note before concluding anything.

Two ratios are useful. Research and development as a percentage of revenue is the conventional one, and it works for comparing a company against its own history. Research and development as a percentage of gross profit is more revealing for a design-led company, because development is funded out of gross profit rather than out of revenue, and a company whose margin has compressed is under pressure on development spending in a way the revenue-based ratio hides.

There is also a tax wrinkle that separates the reported expense from the cash. Under changes made by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, US taxpayers were required to capitalise research and experimental expenditure and amortise it — over five years for domestic research and fifteen for foreign — for tax years beginning after 31 December 2021. The legislation signed on 4 July 2025 introduced Section 174A, restoring an immediate deduction for domestic research and experimental expenditure for tax years beginning after 31 December 2024, with transition relief for amounts capitalised in 2022 to 2024 and with foreign research still subject to capitalisation. None of this changes the research and development line in the income statement. All of it changes cash taxes paid. If you have ever wondered why a company's cash tax payments diverged sharply from its effective tax rate in those years, this is usually most of the answer, and it will be explained in the income taxes note rather than anywhere more prominent.

The last item under this heading is stock-based compensation, which in chip design companies is large. It is an expense under ASC 718 and it is routinely excluded from the non-GAAP measures companies present alongside their statutory results. Companies may present non-GAAP measures, subject to Regulation G and Item 10(e) of Regulation S-K, which require the most directly comparable GAAP measure to be given equal or greater prominence and prohibit presentations that are misleading. An accountant's view of the exclusion is straightforward without being censorious. Stock-based compensation is not a cash cost in the year, which is a real and legitimate distinction. It is also a genuine cost, paid in ownership rather than currency, and the payment shows up in the diluted share count rather than in the cash flow statement. The place to check it is the share count over several years, together with the cash spent on buying shares back. A company repurchasing shares steadily while the count stays flat is converting cash into compensation, and it is doing so in a way that no single line of the income statement displays.

## Cash conversion

The cash flow statement is the least editable of the three statements. Judgement enters it, but it enters at fewer points, and the closing balance is checkable against a bank.

Start with operating cash flow against net income. The gap between them is made of non-cash charges — depreciation, amortisation, stock-based compensation, write-downs — and movements in working capital. In this industry, that gap is usually large and usually informative. Profit rising while operating cash flow falls means working capital is absorbing the difference, and in a chip company that most often means inventory or receivables. It is the same warning the inventory note gives you, arriving through a different door.

Then subtract capital expenditure. Free cash flow is not a defined term under either framework, and companies define it differently, so check the definition being used before comparing anyone with anyone. The reason to insist on it here is that operating cash flow flatters capital-intensive businesses: a plant owner's spending is classified as investing, so its operating cash flow can look magnificent while it consumes every cent of that cash and more on the plant that generates it.

The cash conversion cycle assembles the working capital picture into a number of days: days of inventory, plus days of sales outstanding, minus days of payables outstanding. Take an illustrative company with invented round numbers — 90 days of inventory, 45 days to collect from customers, and 60 days before it pays its own suppliers. Its cycle is 75 days. It funds two and a half months of operations out of its own pocket. Reduce inventory days to 70 and the cycle falls to 55, releasing cash without a single additional sale. That is why inventory discipline is a financing decision as much as an operational one, and why a company under strain will often show it first in the cycle rather than in the profit line.

Negative working capital is worth looking for specifically. A supplier scarce enough to be paid in advance is being financed by its customers, and the evidence sits in contract liabilities, deferred revenue or advance receipts on the balance sheet. In this industry that condition tends to arrive with shortage and leave with it, so the trend in that line is a demand signal in its own right, arriving one step ahead of revenue.

## A reading order

If you have forty minutes and a filing, spend them roughly like this. Read the cash flow statement first, all three sections, because it is the hardest statement to dress. Read the balance sheet second, with attention to inventory, assets under construction, and any liability that represents customer money. Read the income statement third, which is the reverse of most people's order and the right way round. Then the notes: segment and major customer concentration, commitments and purchase obligations, inventory, revenue recognition policy, income taxes. Then the auditor's critical audit matters, because that is a specialist telling you where the judgement was hardest. Then this year's risk factors against last year's. MD&A last, when you already know what happened and can see what management chose to emphasise and what it chose not to.

Two things the accounts will not give you, and it is worth knowing the boundary. They will not give you yield, which is the most commercially sensitive number in the industry and is disclosed by nobody. And they will no longer give you the industry-wide order signal that a previous generation of analysts relied on: SEMI ceased publishing its monthly North American book-to-bill report in 2017, with the December 2016 report the last, after participants stopped reporting orders. SEMI continues to publish a billings report, and the Worldwide Semiconductor Equipment Market Statistics report prepared with the Semiconductor Equipment Association of Japan carries billings and bookings by equipment segment and end-market region. Beyond that, backlog disclosure in individual filings is what remains.

Finally, the standing caution of this book. Every figure you extract using this toolkit is dated the day the filing was signed, and this industry re-prices weekly. The filing tells you what was true at a balance sheet date that has already passed. Take the method, not the number; when you need the number, go and get the current one from the current filing, and write down the date beside it.
