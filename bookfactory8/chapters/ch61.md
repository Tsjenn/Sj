# What the Auditors See

Late in the reporting cycle, in a meeting room that is usually the smallest on the floor, a handful of people spend an afternoon on a single estimate. There is a partner, a senior manager, someone from the technical department who has been brought in because the question is difficult, and, later in the day, the audit committee. Nobody in the room is arguing about whether a transaction happened. The invoices exist, the cash moved, the machines are installed and running. What they are arguing about is a number that nobody can observe: how long those machines will earn their keep, or what a minority stake in a private company is worth, or whether a contract signed with a company that is simultaneously a customer, a supplier and a shareholder produced one performance obligation or four.

That afternoon is the subject of this chapter. Not because the outcome is secret — it is published, in a form most readers skip — but because the *shape* of the argument tells you where a set of accounts is soft, and the shape is visible from outside if you know what you are looking at.

This chapter is written as general professional practice. It names no accounting standard, no auditing standard, no regulator and no country, because the judgements described here arise in every major reporting framework, under slightly different words, with the same underlying difficulty. What follows holds wherever the accounts are prepared.

One caution before starting. Nothing here alleges that any company has done anything improper. Every judgement described is a legitimate estimate that a competent management is entitled to make and a competent auditor is entitled to accept. The purpose is to show you where the estimating happens, so that you stop reading estimates as though they were measurements.

## What an opinion actually says

Begin with the thing most readers get wrong, because everything else follows from it.

An audit opinion does not say the accounts are correct. It says they are *fairly stated in all material respects* — that they present, within a tolerance, a view a reasonable reader would not be misled by. Two words there are doing heavy work.

*Material* is a threshold, set at the start of the audit and revised during it, below which an error is accepted as not worth correcting. It is calculated from something stable — profit, revenue, total assets — and reduced by judgement for the entity's specific risks. It is rarely disclosed. Its practical effect is that an audited figure carries a tolerance band, and in a company with hundreds of billions of dollars of assets that band is not small. Nobody has counted every server.

*Fairly stated* means within the range of defensible answers, not equal to the single right one. When an auditor accepts a six-year life for a class of equipment, the conclusion is not "six years is correct" but "six years sits inside the range the evidence supports, and the disclosure describes the basis honestly." Five might also have been acceptable. Seven might not. The audit tests whether the number is inside the fence, not where inside the fence it sits — and management, quite properly, chooses where inside the fence to stand.

This is why two companies in the same trade, with near-identical assets, can report materially different margins and both hold clean opinions. That is not a failure of audit. It is audit working as designed, applied to a question with no observable answer.

The second thing to understand is what an auditor is actually allowed to rely on. Evidence has a hierarchy, and it is worth keeping in your head as you read any note in this chapter:

| Strength | Kind of evidence | Example in this industry |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest | External, obtained directly by the auditor | Bank confirmations; a signed contract obtained from the counterparty; an observable market price in an active market |
| Strong | Physical, observed by the auditor | Attending a count of equipment; inspecting an asset in a facility |
| Moderate | Internal records with a robust external trail | Delivery notes matched to supplier invoices and cash |
| Weak | Internal records only | A management schedule of expected redeployment of hardware |
| Weakest | Management's intention and forecast | A board-approved five-year plan supporting a valuation or a going-concern conclusion |

Notice the direction of travel. The judgements that matter most in this industry — useful life, the value of a private stake, whether a cash-hungry business can fund itself — all sit at the bottom of that table. They are supported by the weakest class of evidence available, because there is no stronger class in existence. That is not negligence. It is the structural condition of auditing a business whose principal assets are a forecast about the future of a technology.

There is also the client relationship, which the profession manages with rules on independence, rotation and the approval of non-audit work, but cannot abolish. In the ordinary case it produces exactly what it should: a professional argument, conducted privately, that lands somewhere sensible. It also means that when you see an unusual disclosure — a lengthened explanation, a newly specific sentence, a matter named as difficult for the first time — you are usually looking at the visible residue of a private argument that management did not entirely win.

## The six places where judgement lives

Across the layers this book has described, the hard judgements cluster in six areas. None was invented by this industry. Each is stretched here to a degree older industries never demanded.

| Judgement | Why it is hard here | The weakest link in the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Useful life of hardware | Product generations arrive faster than the assumed lives; no deep second-hand market to observe | Management's forecast of redeployment and future demand |
| Revenue recognition on complex deals | Contracts bundle supply, capacity, credits, discounts and equity in one document | Allocating a single price across obligations with no standalone prices |
| Impairment of strategic investments | Stakes in private companies with no market and infrequent, investor-led pricing events | The last round's price, and whether it is a fair-value indicator |
| Purchase commitments | Very large, multi-year, non-cancellable obligations against uncertain demand | Whether a commitment has become loss-making, and when |
| Related-party disclosure | The same counterparty is customer, supplier and shareholder | Completeness — you cannot audit what you were never told about |
| Going concern for the cash-hungry | Large negative cash flow funded by rounds that have not yet been raised | Investor intention, which is not a contract |

Take them one at a time.

## Useful life of hardware

Chapter thirty-two set out what this estimate does to reported profit, and the arithmetic is not repeated here. What matters in this chapter is the narrower question: what can an auditor actually *test*?

Four things, and their strength varies enormously.

The first is the historical record of the fleet. If equipment bought six years ago is still installed, powered and carrying workload, that is real evidence for a six-year life. It is also backward-looking evidence about a generation of hardware that may not resemble the generation being capitalised today, which is the whole of the argument.

The second is the retirement history: what has actually been decommissioned, at what age, and what was written off when it went. This is the strongest evidence available, because it records events rather than intentions, and it is why early-retirement charges matter far more than their size suggests. A company whose fleet is young enough that nothing has yet reached the end of its assumed life has no such data point, and neither does its auditor.

The third is external corroboration. In most industries this is where an auditor turns: what do the machines fetch second-hand, what do independent valuers say, what do other operators assume? Here that evidence is thin. There is no liquid market in used accelerators, disclosed resale prices are rare, and peer comparison is circular — everyone is estimating the same unknown, so agreement proves only that they read the same coverage.

The fourth is management's forward plan: the capacity roadmap, the intended cascade of older hardware to less demanding work, the power and floor-space budget. Genuine evidence, and an auditor is right to consider it. Also, precisely, a forecast produced by the people whose reported profit depends on the answer.

From outside, read the wording of the disclosure and place it on a ladder.

The weakest rung is a bare range with no basis: equipment is depreciated over three to six years, and nothing further. That range is wide enough to cover a doubling of the charge and tells you nothing about where within it the company sits.

The middle rung states the life, states that it is reviewed, and says what the review considered — technology cadence, utilisation, redeployment, physical condition.

The strongest rung does one thing more: it gives you a number attached to a change. A company that revises an estimate and quantifies the effect has handed you the sensitivity of its own profit to its own judgement, and is easier to trust than one that revises silently, whichever direction the change went.

And one signal outranks all of the wording. If the useful life of equipment appears among the matters the auditor names as having required the most difficult judgement, the estimate was contested. If it appears in one year and not the next, something changed — either the evidence improved, or the estimate did.

## Revenue recognition on complex deals

The classic contract in an older industry was a box, a price and a delivery date. The characteristic contract in this one is a document of many pages in which a supplier agrees to deliver hardware over three years, the buyer agrees to purchase a minimum volume, the supplier takes an equity stake or grants a warrant, cloud credits move in the opposite direction, engineering support is provided at no separately stated charge, and a discount schedule is tied to volumes nobody can yet predict.

Every element of that is legitimate. All of it has to be turned into revenue, in the right amount, in the right periods, by judgements the accounts summarise in a paragraph. Four questions dominate, in the order an auditor asks them.

**How many promises are in this document?** A single contract may contain several distinct things the customer could have bought separately, and each is accounted for on its own timetable. Split it one way and revenue lands on delivery; split it another and it spreads across years of service. Nothing about the cash changes.

**What is the price, really?** Where consideration depends on future volumes, rebates, milestones or penalties, the amount to be recognised is an estimate of what will eventually be received, made now, constrained so that recognised revenue is not likely to reverse later. That constraint is a judgement about the company's own forecasting ability, assessed by the company.

**How is the price divided?** Once the promises are identified, the total must be allocated across them in proportion to what each would sell for standalone. In this industry many of the components have never been sold standalone — bespoke engineering support, priority access to constrained capacity, a bundled credit — so the standalone price is itself estimated, usually by building it up from cost plus a margin. Two estimates stacked on each other decide when revenue appears.

**Principal or agent?** Where a business resells capacity, or fulfils through a partner, the difference between reporting the gross amount and the net margin is enormous in revenue and nil in profit. The test turns on who controls the service before it reaches the customer — a judgement about contractual rights, not about size.

Take an illustrative contract, with round numbers chosen for clarity and belonging to no real company. A supplier signs a 1,000 arrangement: hardware over two years, three years of support, a volume rebate worth between nil and 100, and a commitment by the supplier to purchase 150 of the customer's services over the same period. Suppose the rebate is estimated at 40, so the transaction price is 960. If the purchase of the customer's services is judged a separate transaction at fair value, revenue of 960 is recognised and 150 of cost sits elsewhere. If it is judged consideration payable to a customer, the 150 reduces revenue and the same contract produces 810. Same cash, same commercial deal, a revenue difference of about fifteen per cent — decided by whether the supplier received a distinct good or service at a price it could demonstrate was fair.

Carry that away. Where money flows in both directions between two parties, the accounting question is not whether the arrangement is proper; it is whether the two flows are separately priced at something defensible. Chapter fifty-seven dealt with what circular flows look like from outside. This is what they look like from inside, on the day the file has to be signed.

From the disclosure, look for four things: whether the company describes its promises in terms specific to its actual contracts rather than in boilerplate; whether it discloses contracted revenue not yet recognised, and over what period; whether it says how it estimates variable amounts; and whether it discloses consideration paid to customers. A revenue policy note that could be pasted into any company's accounts unaltered is telling you the company would rather not be specific.

## Impairment of strategic investments

This is the area where this industry has moved furthest ahead of the machinery built to account for it.

A large operating company takes a minority stake in a private model developer. There is no market price. The stake is typically carried at what was paid, adjusted when an observable transaction in the same or a similar instrument gives a better indication, and written down when there is evidence it is worth less. Every one of those steps is judgement, and in this industry each has a specific difficulty.

The first difficulty is that the pricing events are infrequent and enormous. Anthropic closed a sixty-five billion dollar round at a nine hundred and sixty-five billion dollar post-money valuation in May 2026, briefly the most valuable private company in the world, as reported by CNBC in May 2026; OpenAI closed a one hundred and twenty-two billion dollar round in March 2026 at an eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollar valuation, per 2026 reporting from valueaddvc and Forbes. When a round like that prices, holders of earlier stakes may have an observable indication that moves a carrying value by a very large amount in one step. Between rounds, there is nothing.

The second is who sets the price. A round may be led or substantially funded by parties who are also the investee's largest suppliers or largest customers. Amazon has committed up to thirty-three billion dollars to Anthropic and Google up to forty-three billion, including ten billion in April 2026, per 2026 reporting; Amazon was reported as investing fifty billion dollars in OpenAI's March 2026 round. None of that is improper, and strategic investors have always paid for strategic reasons. But a price set by a party with a commercial relationship to the investee is a weaker indication of what a financial buyer would pay than a price set by an arm's-length one, and the accounts rarely tell you which kind of price is doing the work.

The third is asymmetry. The mechanism that moves a carrying value up on a new round also, in principle, moves it down on a lower one. Down rounds are less visible, more slowly disclosed and more likely to be structured — with liquidation preferences or ratchets that protect the new money at the expense of the old — so a headline valuation can hold while the economic value of an earlier, less protected holding falls. An auditor testing a carrying value against a headline round price has to ask what instrument was priced, with what rights, and whether the holding being valued shares them. It is one of the quietest and most consequential questions in this chapter.

The fourth is the effect on the holder's own results. Where a stake is measured at fair value through profit, changes in an unlisted valuation flow straight into reported earnings. Big Tech's stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic have been reported as distorting the corporate earnings picture of the companies holding them, per CNBC in August 2026. That is a reporting observation, not an allegation. A movement in a privately determined number can land in the profit of a listed company, and a reader who does not separate it will mistake it for trading performance.

Take an illustrative holder, with round numbers belonging to no real company. It invests 100 for a small stake. Eighteen months later a new round prices the investee three times higher, the holder writes its stake up to 300, and recognises 200 of gain. Nothing has been sold, no cash has arrived, and the 200 is the whole of that year's reported profit growth. If the next round prices flat but with preference terms ranking ahead of the holder's shares, the headline valuation is unchanged while the holder's economic position is worse. The accounts will show that only if somebody makes the judgement and can support it.

In the disclosure, look for whether gains and losses on investments are separated from operating results clearly enough to strip out; how unlisted holdings are valued and what triggered any remeasurement; the aggregate size of the holdings; and whether amounts through profit are realised or unrealised. Where you cannot separate them, the honest conclusion is that you cannot tell what the operating business earned.

## Purchase commitments

A commitment is a promise to spend money that has not yet been spent. It is not a liability on the balance sheet, because nothing has yet been received. It is disclosed in a note, and in this industry it is frequently the largest number in the accounts that is not in the accounts.

The scale is not hypothetical. Memory capacity was reported sold out through 2026 across major suppliers (industry analysis, 2026), and the way a buyer secures scarce capacity is by committing to take it. The sellers commit in the other direction: TSMC guided 2026 capital expenditure to fifty-two to fifty-six billion US dollars, above analyst expectations, in its January 2026 results, and SK Hynix announced a thirty-eight billion dollar investment in new memory plants, per CNBC in August 2026. Commitments are how a shortage is converted into a contract.

Three audit judgements arise.

**Completeness.** Commitments are found by asking, reading and searching — board minutes, legal confirmations, contract registers — rather than by testing a balance that must reconcile. No ledger is wrong if one is missed. That makes completeness the hardest assertion here, and it is why an auditor cares whether a company's contract approval process routes everything past the finance function.

**Cancellability.** A commitment that can be cancelled without penalty is nearly meaningless; one that cannot is close to a debt. Most sit between, with notice periods, tiered break fees or volume flexibility bands. The disclosure often uses a single word — "non-cancellable" — to describe a spectrum.

**Whether it has turned bad.** A commitment to buy at agreed prices becomes an accounting problem the moment the company expects to lose money on it, either because prices have fallen below the contracted level or because the demand it was signed for has not appeared. At that point a charge may be required before anything is delivered. Judging when that point has been reached is a forecast, made by the people who signed the contract.

Take an illustrative operator, again with invented round numbers. It discloses non-cancellable purchase obligations of 40 over four years, against annual operating cash flow of 12 and cash on hand of 15. Nothing about that is alarming on its own — the commitments buy the capacity that produces the cash flow. But the arithmetic is now visible: more than three years of current operating cash flow is pre-committed, and the commitment does not adjust if demand falls. That is the calculation the note exists to let you do, and it takes a minute.

Read the note for four things: the total, the maturity profile, whether cancellation terms are described, and whether the total moved sharply since last year. A single aggregate figure with no maturity split is disclosure at its minimum. A profile year by year, with categories named and cancellation terms described, is a company that expects you to do the arithmetic and is comfortable with the result.

## Related-party disclosure

The technical definition of a related party is narrower than most readers assume, and the gap between the technical definition and the commercial reality is where this industry lives.

Broadly, the frameworks catch parties that control or are controlled, parties under common control, parties in which the entity has significant influence or which have significant influence over it, joint ventures, and the individuals who direct the business and their close family. What they generally do *not* automatically catch is a party that is merely very important: a customer representing a large share of revenue, a supplier with no substitute, or a shareholder holding a modest percentage with no board seat.

So a company can be commercially entangled with another in every way that matters and still, correctly, report no related-party transactions with it. That is not a loophole; it is the definition working as written. The reader's job is to notice the difference between "no related-party transactions" and "no relationships that affect the numbers." Two judgements do most of the work.

The first is significant influence, which turns on the ability to participate in financial and operating policy decisions. Board representation, participation in setting policy, material transactions and interchange of personnel all point towards it; a percentage holding is an indicator, not the test. Where a company holds a stake below the customary threshold but has a board seat and a large supply relationship, the assessment is genuinely arguable, and the conclusion decides whether an entire category of transactions is disclosed.

The second is completeness, again. Related parties are identified from registers, board minutes, confirmations and enquiry, and every one of those sources depends on somebody inside the company having identified the relationship first. This is the one area in the chapter where the auditor's evidence depends fundamentally on being told.

What to infer from outside. A related-party note listing several relationships with descriptions and amounts is not a warning sign; it is a company that has looked. A note saying there were no related-party transactions, in a business you know to be tied to its investors as suppliers and customers, is a prompt to read the definition being applied and the equity investments note beside it. And the most useful comparison, as so often, is against last year: relationships appear and disappear from these notes as holdings and board seats change.

Where a company voluntarily quantifies revenue from parties in which it holds an interest — separately, with a number — it has given you something the frameworks may not require and the market cannot otherwise compute. That is the single most useful disclosure a company in this position can make, and its absence is itself information.

## Going concern for the cash-hungry

The final judgement is the one that sits underneath all the others, because it decides on what basis the accounts are prepared at all.

Accounts are prepared assuming the business will continue for at least the period the framework requires — around a year from the reporting date, give or take. If that assumption is doubtful, disclosure is required; if it fails, the whole basis of preparation changes and assets are restated to what they would fetch on a wind-down. There is no middle setting. The judgement is binary and its consequences are total.

For most of the companies in this book the assumption is comfortable, because they generate more cash than they spend. For the cash-hungry it is the hardest judgement in the file. A business spending heavily on capacity, funded by rounds rather than by operations, has a runway that can be calculated and an outcome that depends on whether the next round arrives.

Take an illustrative company, with round numbers belonging to no real company. It holds cash of 30. Its operations consume 20 a year. It has committed 25 of capital spending over the next twelve months, of which 10 is cancellable at short notice. On the face of it, the runway is short: 30 of cash against 20 of operating outflow and 15 of non-cancellable spending is a shortfall inside the year. The board's plan is a new round, discussions are advanced, and existing investors are supportive. That is the whole of the question. Investor support is not a contract. A signed and unconditional facility is evidence; an expression of intent is a forecast.

The response is graded. If the plan is credible and the mitigations sit within management's control — deferring the cancellable spending, slowing hiring — the assumption holds and nothing may need saying. If it depends on something outside management's control, the accounts should describe the dependency and the auditor's report should point to that description. In the most severe case the opinion itself is affected.

This is why the pace of private fundraising in this industry is not merely a market story. The rounds described earlier are, in accounting terms, the mitigation on which a going-concern conclusion rests for companies at every scale below the top. A funding market that stays open makes those conclusions easy. One that closes makes them hard, all at once and across many companies. Nothing here predicts which will happen; the point is that this judgement is the one place a market condition enters the accounts directly, as a yes or a no.

Three things are worth finding. Whether the accounts discuss the assessment at all, and how specifically. Whether the mitigating actions described sit within management's control or depend on third parties. And whether the auditor's report draws attention to the matter — because when it does, it is not adding new information; it is telling you that a disclosure already in the accounts deserved pointing at.

## Reading the disclosure rather than the number

Pull the six areas together and a general method appears, which works on any company in this industry and most outside it.

For every judgement, ask three questions in order. What is the range of defensible answers? Where within that range has management chosen to stand? And has the company given you enough to work out what a different choice would have done?

The third separates good disclosure from merely compliant disclosure, and it is answerable from outside. A company that states an estimate, explains its basis and quantifies the effect of a change has handed you the sensitivity. A company that states the estimate and stops has met its obligation and told you nothing. Both are unqualified. Only one is useful.

Then read the auditor's report properly, which almost nobody does. Most modern audit reports contain a section naming the matters that required the most difficult judgement in that year's audit — the terminology varies between frameworks, the function does not — and describing what was done about each. The value is not in the description, which is usually careful to the point of blandness. It is in the list, and in how the list changes. A matter appearing for the first time means the risk profile moved. A matter dropping off means the auditor concluded it was no longer among the hardest. Neither event is announced anywhere else.

Finally, note what an audit is not, because the limits are as important as the content. An audit is not a valuation, and an opinion says nothing about whether a company is worth what the market pays for it. It is not a forecast, and a clean opinion in one year implies nothing about the next. It is not a guarantee against fraud, particularly collusive fraud involving senior people and external parties, which is the kind an audit is least equipped to find. It does not test every transaction; it tests a sample, and designs the sample around where errors would matter. And it is conducted to a materiality threshold that is never disclosed, on a company whose most important assets are estimates about a technology that did not exist a decade ago.

None of that makes the opinion worthless. It makes it precisely what it says on the page: a professional conclusion that the numbers are fairly stated, in all material respects, on the basis described in the notes. The notes are where the basis is described. That is the entire argument of this chapter — the interesting information is not in the audited number, which is a point estimate inside a range, but in the disclosed reasoning that put it there.

One habit is worth taking from this chapter. When a figure in this industry strikes you as surprising, do not ask whether it is correct. Ask which estimate it rests on, find the note describing that estimate, and read what the company chose to tell you about how it was made. In an industry where the assets are machines with contested lives, the contracts are bundles with allocated prices, the investments are stakes without markets and the obligations sit in notes rather than on the balance sheet, that habit is worth more than any single number you will ever extract.
