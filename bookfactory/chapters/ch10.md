# Your Sleep Tracker Is Half Right

If you sleep with a tracker — on your wrist, under your mattress, or just a phone listening from the nightstand — you wake up every morning to a verdict. A number, usually. Perhaps a colour. Somewhere in your first conscious minute, a device tells you how last night went, as if you hadn't been there yourself.

The honest question is not "should you trust it?" — that invites a yes or no, and the truth is neither. The honest question is *which parts* to trust. Because your tracker is genuinely measuring some things, genuinely guessing at others, and presenting both in the same confident font. This chapter sorts the measurements from the decorations, as promised back in chapter one.

## What your tracker actually measures

Strip away the app's interface and every consumer tracker is working from a small set of raw signals: movement, sound, and — in wearables — heart rate and its variations. That's the whole toolkit. Everything on your morning screen is inferred from those.

And some inferences from those signals are on solid ground. Whether a body is moving or still, breathing noisily or quietly, running a resting heart rate or an active one — these are things an accelerometer and a heart-rate sensor can genuinely detect. From them, a tracker can do a reasonable job with three things:

**Timing.** When you got into bed, roughly when you stopped moving like a waking person, roughly when you started again. Trackers are decent clocks, and the clock questions — when did sleep start, when did it end — are the ones they answer best.

**Duration.** Total sleep time, give or take. A tracker will sometimes count a very still hour of lying awake as sleep — stillness is its main clue, and you can fool it by sulking motionlessly — but across a normal night, the tally is in the right neighbourhood.

**Fragmentation.** Long wake-ups in the middle of the night involve movement, heart-rate shifts, sometimes a trip down the hall. Those leave fingerprints a sensor can find. If your tracker says you were awake for forty minutes at 3am several nights running, that is worth believing.

The pattern behind all three: consumer trackers agree reasonably well with proper sleep measurement on the crude distinction — *asleep versus awake*. When your tracker speaks about that distinction, it is measuring, more or less. Remember that sentence, because the next section is about everything else.

## What it only guesses

Here is the part the marketing never leads with. The only true measurement of sleep stages — light sleep, deep sleep, REM — is polysomnography: a sleep-lab setup with electrodes on your scalp reading your brain's electrical activity directly, plus sensors for eye movement and muscle tone. Stages are *defined* by brainwaves. They are a brain phenomenon, scored from the brain.

Your wrist does not have access to your brainwaves. Neither does your mattress, and your phone's microphone certainly doesn't. What they have is movement and heart rate, which *correlate* with stages — heart rate tends to fall in deep sleep, the body is paralysed in REM — and from those correlations the tracker estimates what your brain was probably doing. Sometimes the estimate lands. Often it doesn't, and validation studies comparing consumer devices against the lab keep finding the same shape of result: good agreement on asleep-versus-awake, much weaker agreement on which stage you were in.

So when your tracker announces you got 43 minutes of deep sleep, that is not a reading. It is an educated guess dressed as a reading — a weather forecast wearing a thermometer's outfit. And when you feel terrible after a night your tracker scored as stage-perfect, or fine after a supposedly deep-sleep-free disaster, the discrepancy is not a mystery about your body. It is the error bars showing.

This is not a reason to throw the device away. It is a reason to read the stage chart the way you'd read a horoscope from a fairly perceptive friend: interesting, occasionally uncanny, not a basis for decisions.

## The score is a recipe, not a measurement

Then there is the sleep score — the single number, usually out of 100, that greets you at dawn.

Understand what that number is: a proprietary recipe. Each company takes the measurements and the guesses, weights them however its designers chose, folds in whatever else it fancies — heart rate, "restlessness," consistency — and bakes the lot into one digit-pair. The recipe is secret, differs between apps, and changes when the company updates it. Run two apps on the same night and you can get two different scores, because there is no true score they are both approximating. Nobody's brain produces an 81. An 81 is a design decision.

Which leads to the one rule that makes scores useful instead of tyrannical: **compare yourself only to your own average, in one app.** An 81 means nothing against a friend's 90 on a different platform — you're comparing recipes, not sleep. But your own 81 in a week of 74s, on the same device running the same recipe? That's a signal something changed, and worth a moment's curiosity about what. The score is meaningless as an absolute and modestly informative as a trend. Use it for the second thing only.

## When the tracker becomes the thing keeping you awake

Now the trap, because it's real enough that sleep clinicians gave it a name: **orthosomnia** — sleeping worse because of the pursuit of perfect tracked sleep.

It runs like this. A low score arrives. You feel worse than you otherwise would have — the number told you to. You go to bed that night *trying* to score well, which means monitoring, which means arousal, which — as the middle-of-the-night chapter established — is precisely the state that prevents sleep. Score drops further. Effort increases. The tracker, bought to improve your sleep, is now the stressor degrading it, and you have the strange modern experience of losing sleep over a gadget's opinion of your sleep.

The tell is emotional: if checking the morning number has started to feel like checking exam results, or if a bad score can bend your whole day, take a tracker holiday. Two weeks, device in a drawer. This is not quitting; it's the same logic as clock-blindness from the 3am chapter — removing a number that was generating arousal without generating help. Plenty of people sleep noticeably better on holiday from their tracker, which tells you exactly who was working for whom.

## A good diary, a bad judge

So here is the honest verdict, and the frame that keeps a tracker in its lane: **a tracker is a good diary and a bad judge.** As a diary — a record of when you slept, how long, how broken, drifting over weeks — it's genuinely useful, and more accurate than your own foggy recall. As a judge — grading nights, ruling on deep sleep, telling you how to feel — it is guessing beyond its evidence.

If you keep one, watch the diary numbers: total sleep time, sleep efficiency (the share of your time in bed actually spent asleep), and the consistency of your timings — the anchor, in other words, which the tracker is well equipped to witness. Watch trends over weeks and ignore single nights, because single nights are noisy in real labs, never mind on a wrist. And let how you feel by mid-morning outvote the score every time. You are the instrument the tracker is trying to approximate.
