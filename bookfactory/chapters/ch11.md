# Food, Exercise and Timing

By now you may have noticed a pattern: the previous chapters kept sending you away from the bedroom. The anchor wake time, the morning light, the caffeine cutoff — all daylight business. This chapter finishes that job. It covers the last three daytime levers people ask about: moving your body, feeding it, and the aisle of the pharmacy with the moon logos on everything. As ever, they are ranked — and the ranking may surprise you, because the one that works best is the one nobody can put in a bottle.

## Exercise: the sleep aid that actually earns the name

If a pill did what regular exercise does for sleep, it would be the best-selling sleep product in history. People who exercise regularly tend to fall asleep faster, spend more of the night in deep sleep, and wake less. And the effect arrives through more than one door at once, which is why it is so robust.

The first door is the one you already know from chapter two: sleep pressure. Physical effort is metabolically expensive, and a body that has done real work builds a more honest case for deep sleep than one that has commuted between chairs. The second door is temperature — exercise raises your core temperature, and in the hours afterwards it falls, and a *falling* core temperature is one of the body's strongest go-to-sleep signals. You get to ride the downslope. The third door may matter most of all if your problem is a busy mind: exercise reliably takes the edge off anxiety and low mood, and for a large share of bad sleepers, the thing keeping them awake is not a body that isn't tired but a head that won't idle. Exercise is one of the few tools that works on both.

Now for the caveat you have heard — "never exercise at night, it'll wreck your sleep" — because it needs honest downsizing. The rule is much weaker than its confidence suggests. For most people, moderate evening exercise does not harm sleep, and for plenty it is the only slot the day offers; an evening workout beats no workout by a distance, and this book will not pretend otherwise. The kernel of truth is smaller and more specific: a genuinely *intense* session finishing close to bedtime leaves you with an elevated heart rate, a raised core temperature and a dose of adrenaline, which is a poor state to lie down in. So the honest rule is not a curfew. It is a buffer: **after hard exercise, give yourself an hour or so to cool, shower and come down before bed.** After a walk, a stretch or an easy ride, you need nothing of the sort. Tune it by result, not by rule — you will know within a week which camp you're in.

## The late heavy meal

Food's main sleep effect is blunter and more mechanical than most of what this book covers, which makes it refreshingly easy to explain.

Digesting a large meal is work — warm, active, occasionally noisy work — and asking your body to do it at the same time as descending into deep sleep is asking it to do two jobs at once. But the bigger problem is plumbing. At the top of your stomach sits a valve, and while you are upright, gravity is on that valve's side: the meal stays down. Lie flat within an hour or two of a heavy dinner and gravity resigns. Stomach acid can seep the wrong way — that is reflux, and heartburn is its calling card. The sneaky part is that reflux doesn't need to wake you fully to cost you; it can fragment sleep into shallowness you never remember, so the bill arrives as an unexplained groggy morning. Rich, fatty, spicy and very large meals are the main offenders, alcohol relaxes the valve further, and if you already know heartburn by name, you know exactly which dinners summon it.

The fix is the same unglamorous tool this book keeps reaching for: **finish big meals two to three hours before bed.** That is not a fasting rule — going to bed properly hungry is its own sleep-wrecker, and a light snack in the evening is fine. The target is specifically the large, late, horizontal-within-the-hour dinner. Move the feast earlier, or shrink the late version, and this lever is fully pulled.

## Melatonin: a clock signal wearing a sedative's costume

Now the pharmacy aisle, starting with its most misunderstood resident.

Melatonin is a hormone your brain releases in the evening as darkness falls. Its message is not "shut down now." It is closer to a dusk announcement — *night is coming* — that helps set the circadian clock from chapter two. Taken as a pill, it does the same thing: it tells your clock what time it is. **It is a timing signal, not a sedative** — and that single sentence sorts every melatonin question you will ever have.

It means melatonin genuinely earns its keep where the clock itself is the problem: jet lag, shift work, a body clock drifted badly out of line with the life you need to live. Used there, at the right time, small doses can help shift the clock — and small is the operative word; more melatonin does not mean more sleep, because you cannot announce dusk *harder*. It also means melatonin is a poor tool for ordinary can't-switch-off insomnia: taken at your usual bedtime, when your clock already knows the time, you are announcing dusk to a body that heard the news hours ago. Some people feel a mild drowsy nudge; many feel nothing; nobody gets knocked out, because knocking out was never its job.

One more honesty note that applies to this whole aisle: in many countries melatonin is sold as a supplement, and supplements are loosely regulated — the actual dose in the bottle can differ substantially from the label, between brands and even between batches. That is not a reason for panic; it is a reason for the rule this book applies to everything you swallow: **talk to a pharmacist or doctor before taking it** — about whether it fits your situation, what dose makes sense, and what it does alongside anything else you take. That advice is free, it is personalised, and it is better than anything a paperback can responsibly print.

## Magnesium, herbal tea, and the honest case for ritual

Magnesium gets the short, plain version: the evidence that it improves sleep is thin. Such benefit as the studies suggest appears mostly in people who were short of magnesium to begin with — in which case the fix is fixing the deficiency, not a sleep hack. If you suspect that's you, that is a conversation for a doctor, not a shopping decision. Buying it *for sleep*, on current evidence, is buying hope.

Herbal teas — chamomile and its cousins — sit in almost the same evidential spot: as pharmacology, weak. And yet this book will not sneer at them, because they smuggle in something real. A warm, caffeine-free drink made the same way at the same hour every night is a *ritual* — a repeated cue that tells a well-trained body the day is closing. That is a genuine mechanism; it simply lives in the habit, not the herb. Drink the tea. Just be honest about what you're buying: the ceremony works, the chamomile is mostly a passenger.

Notice where this chapter ends up: the strongest item wasn't swallowed, the second-strongest was a scheduling change, and the pharmacy aisle mostly earned a shrug. That is the honest shape of it. But there is a boundary this whole book has been circling — the point where no habit, buffer or ritual is going to fix things, because the problem was never your habits. The next chapter draws that line in plain letters: the warning signs that mean your sleep needs a doctor, not a paperback.
