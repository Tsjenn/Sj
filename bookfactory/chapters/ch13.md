# The 14-Day Reset

Everything this book has argued now folds into two weeks. This chapter is the whole plan on a few pages: one change per day in week one, consolidation in week two. Read it once now, in daylight, rested — because the plan only works if it is written by the rested version of you and merely *executed* by the tired one. The 11pm version of you does not get a vote; you have both seen its judgment.

Three rules govern the whole fortnight:

- **One change per day.** Each day's task takes minutes. The restraint is the design — pile on five changes at once and you will not know what worked, and you will quit by Thursday.
- **A missed day means continue, not restart.** This is not a streak. Slept badly, skipped a step, lost a day to real life? Pick up where you left off. Restarting from day one is perfectionism wearing a planner's costume, and perfectionism is arousal.
- **Decide everything in advance.** Every step below is a decision made today so that no decisions are left for the dark.

## Week one: the big levers, in order of leverage

- **Day 1 — Pick the anchor.** Choose your wake time: the hour your worst weekday already forces on you, not an aspirational one. Set the alarm, then put it — or your phone — across the room or outside it entirely. The alarm is the decision; the morning version of you just executes it. Snooze reopens a negotiation you have already lost. This is the day the whole reset hangs on, which is why it comes first and why it is the only task today.

- **Day 2 — Morning light.** Up at the anchor, then light into your eyes within the hour: curtains first, outside if you possibly can, even briefly, even under cloud. The anchor works *through* light — getting up into dimness is only half the move. From today, this is simply what mornings are.

- **Day 3 — The caffeine cutoff.** Set your last-coffee time far enough from bed that the drug is mostly gone when you need it gone — early afternoon for most people — and hold it. Caffeine you can't feel is still there; the cutoff exists precisely because the effect is invisible from the inside.

- **Day 4 — The alcohol buffer.** Not prohibition — a buffer. Finish any drinking hours before bed and keep the dose modest, because alcohol is a sedative that sells you the first half of the night and quietly repossesses the second. If tonight involves a late glass, you have simply chosen the trade knowingly, which is the entire point of this book.

- **Day 5 — The bedroom pass.** One afternoon, done with your hands: cool (cooler than you think, warm covers), dark (blackout or an eye mask, tape over the LEDs), quiet (steady noise if your nights are punctured, silence if they aren't), and the phone out of the room for good — a cheap alarm clock takes its job. This is the day that costs money, and it costs less than a takeaway.

- **Day 6 — Dim down and unload.** From about an hour before bed: lamps not ceilings, screens down or away, nothing gripping. And the worry transfer — ten minutes with paper, earlier in the evening, writing down what is circling and the next small step for each. You are not solving anything tonight; you are showing your mind the list is held somewhere that isn't the pillow.

- **Day 7 — Hold, and start the log.** No new changes. Run days one to six as one ordinary day, and tonight start the log below. Holding steady *is* the task; the reset is a system now, not a list.

## The log (deliberately crude)

Each morning, three items, thirty seconds, on paper:

1. **Lights-out feel** — did sleep come easily, slowly, or badly? One word.
2. **Wakings, by feel** — none, some, or lots. Never by clock; you are not checking the time, remember.
3. **Morning freshness, 1–5.**

No clock data, no percentages, no scores. The log's job is to show a *trend* across fourteen days that memory — which keeps only the worst nights — will otherwise hide from you. Crude and honest beats precise and misleading.

## What week one honestly feels like

Now the warning this book owes you, because a book that promises honesty owes you warnings in advance: **days three to five commonly feel worse.** If your anchor moved earlier, you are getting up short of sleep while your bedtime hasn't yet drifted forward to meet it — genuinely, biologically underslept — and if you're also skipping the naps that used to patch the gap, the afternoon dip will make its case loudly. This is not the plan failing. The tiredness is the mounting sleep pressure that will pull your bedtime earlier night by night; most people feel the corner turn in the second week. Getting up at the anchor after a bad night is the move that makes tonight better. Trading it for a lie-in trades one groggy day for a drifting week.

## Week two: consolidation

- **Day 8 — The one-purpose bed.** From tonight, the bed is for sleep (intimacy excepted, as always). Reading, scrolling, working, worrying — all of it moves to a chair. You are retraining what the bed *means*, and the training runs every night from here.

- **Day 9 — Arm the 3am playbook.** Lay the trail tonight: blanket and boring book on a chair, dim lamp ready, the commitment renewed — no clock-checking, up after roughly twenty minutes of churning *by feel*, back to bed at sleepy, not bored. Fair warning: early on, this rule makes rough patches feel *more* disrupted — you are out of bed more, and it is annoying. That is the protocol working, not failing; you are refusing to practice wakefulness in bed.

- **Day 10 — One nap, done right.** If you want naps in your life, run the experiment properly: early afternoon, short — set an alarm — and never as a rescue for a bad night. Log how the evening goes. A short early nap borrows little from the night; a long or late one is a payday loan against it. Decide from your own data whether naps stay.

- **Day 11 — Demote the tracker.** If you wear or run one, tonight it becomes a diary, not a judge: glance weekly for trends, ignore nightly scores, and let your morning freshness rating outrank anything the graph claims. If the tracker makes you anxious, retiring it entirely is a legitimate move, not a failure.

- **Days 12 and 13 — The weekend test.** The lie-in is where resets go to die, so it gets two days: wake within an hour of your anchor, light on schedule, and if you're genuinely wrecked, use the day-10 nap rather than a late morning. A nap dips into tonight's pressure; a lie-in moves your clock. Only one of those heals by Monday.

- **Day 14 — Review, against the right standard.** Read the log. The standard is **better most nights, not perfect every night.** Falling asleep somewhat faster, fewer remembered wakings, mornings trending from 2 toward 3-and-a-bit — that is the reset working, and it keeps compounding for weeks after this page. What you are *not* looking for is fourteen unbroken nights; nobody gets those, including the people who claim to. And the standing boundary: if a fortnight of honest effort has moved nothing — or your nights involve heavy snoring, gasping, or dread that follows you into the day — the answer is a doctor, not a stricter fortnight.

Two weeks in, you have something better than a streak: a system that survives ordinary life. The next chapter is about keeping it — what maintenance actually requires (less than you fear), which rules can safely loosen, and how to catch a drift before it becomes a relapse.
