# Keeping It: The Maintenance Mindset

Thirteen chapters ago you were promised no miracles, and here at the end the promise holds: if you've run even half of this book, what you have now isn't perfect sleep. It's something better — a working relationship with sleep, where you know which levers matter, which numbers to ignore, and what to do at 3am. This final chapter is about keeping that relationship through the thing that breaks every system eventually: real life.

## Why streaks fail and elastic survives

The fitness-app model of habit — the unbroken streak, the chain you must not break — is precisely wrong for sleep, and it's worth understanding why before life tests you.

A streak treats one bad night as damage to a perfect record. But you now know one bad night is *nothing* — a single short night is recovered almost entirely by one or two ordinary nights afterward, because sleep pressure does the heavy lifting automatically. What actually damages sleep is the *response* to the bad night: the lie-in that slides the anchor, the panic that raises the stakes, the "my streak is ruined so why bother" collapse that turns one bad night into a bad month. Streak-thinking manufactures exactly that collapse. It makes the first slip catastrophic, and catastrophizing at bedtime is — as chapter seven taught you — arousal wearing a costume.

Elastic thinking makes the opposite bet: the system will bend, regularly, and its whole job is to snap back. Holidays will happen. Illness will happen. A baby, a deadline, a wedding, a World Cup in the wrong timezone — all coming for your anchor eventually. The measure of your sleep system was never whether it bends. It's how fast it returns.

## The two-night rule

So here is the entire maintenance protocol, small enough to remember at your worst:

**After any disruption, give yourself two nights of the full system — anchor, light, cutoffs, boring bed — before you conclude anything.**

Not one night: the first night after a disruption is often noisy regardless (an overnight flight, a hangover, a newborn handoff), and judging the system by it is judging a bridge in an earthquake. And not a week of drift: two nights of the full pattern is almost always enough for the machinery — pressure, clock, association — to find its groove again. Jet lag and shift changes take longer, but the move is the same: plant the anchor in the new reality and let the system walk to it.

The two-night rule also quietly handles the guilt problem. You don't owe sleep an apology or a perfect record — you owe it two honest nights. Anyone can do two nights. That's the point.

## The whole book on one page

Cut this out, photograph it, stick it inside a cupboard door. Ranked, as promised, by leverage:

1. **The anchor.** Same wake time every day, weekends within an hour. This one rule carries half the book.
2. **Morning sky.** Outside light within an hour of waking, ten to twenty minutes. Cloudy counts.
3. **Caffeine cutoff** eight to ten hours before bed. Keep the morning cup.
4. **Alcohol finished** three hours before bed. Never *as* a sleep aid.
5. **The room:** cool, dark, quiet, boring. Phone charges elsewhere. One-off fixes, tape included.
6. **Evenings dim,** content calm. The worry transfer on paper, early, in a chair.
7. **Bed is for sleep.** Everything interesting happens somewhere else.
8. **At 3am:** no clock, ever. Twenty minutes by feel, then up — dim lamp, boring book, back at sleepy.
9. **Naps:** twenty minutes, before mid-afternoon, or not at all.
10. **Trackers are diaries, not judges.** Watch duration and your own trend; ignore the grade.
11. **Bend, then two nights back.** No streaks. No apologies.
12. **Snoring with gasps, months of bad nights, dread at 3am — doctor.** That's strength, not failure.

Notice what's absent: nothing to buy, nothing to believe, nothing that breaks when a hotel room or a house guest breaks your routine. Twelve lines, all mechanism, all yours now.

## Allowing, not achieving

One last idea, and it may be the one that matters most in five years.

Every failed approach to sleep in this book's opening chapter shared a single error: they treated sleep as a performance — something to attempt, optimize, score, and win. And sleep, almost uniquely among the things adults care about, cannot be won. It is not an achievement. It is a biological event that happens *to* you, reliably and on schedule, the moment you stop preventing it.

Which reframes what these fourteen chapters actually were. Not a training program to get better at sleeping — you already knew how; you've known since before you could talk. They were a course in *removing the obstacles*: the drifting clock, the bright evenings, the afternoon caffeine still parked in your receptors at midnight, the bed taught to mean worry, the 3am arithmetic, the grade delivered each morning by an app guessing at your brainwaves. Obstacle removal is humbler work than optimization. It's also the only work that was ever needed.

So this is the maintenance mindset, in the end: keep the path clear, bend when life bends you, come back within two nights, and then — this is the hard part and the whole part — *let it happen*. You don't chase sleep. You make it welcome, turn out the light, and allow it.

Goodnight. You know what to do now — which is, at last, almost nothing.
