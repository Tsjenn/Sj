# When It Isn't Habits: Red Flags That Need a Doctor

Chapter one made a promise: when your sleep problem stops being a habits problem and starts being a medical one, this book would say so in plain letters. This is that chapter. Everything else in these pages lives in wellness territory — timing, light, caffeine, the bed's meaning, the 3am playbook. Those levers are real and they move most bad sleep. But there are sleep problems that no amount of good habits will fix, because they are not caused by habits, and the most dishonest thing a sleep book can do is keep selling you chapters when what you need is an appointment.

So here is the line, drawn as clearly as I can draw it. Nothing in this chapter is a diagnosis — a book cannot examine you, and this one won't pretend to. What a book *can* do is describe the patterns that deserve a professional's eyes, so you stop wasting months politely troubleshooting something that was never yours to troubleshoot.

## The loud-snoring pattern: no exceptions

Start with the big one, because it is common, serious, and routinely missed.

The pattern looks like this: **loud snoring, plus pauses in breathing or gasping and choking that someone else has witnessed, plus sleep that doesn't refresh no matter how long it lasts, plus real daytime sleepiness** — the kind where you fight your eyelids in meetings or, worse, at traffic lights. That cluster is the classic signature of sleep apnea: the airway repeatedly narrowing or closing during sleep, so the body has to half-wake, over and over, to breathe. You usually don't remember those wakings. You just live the consequence — a full night in bed that lands like half a night.

If that pattern sounds like your nights, the move is screening with a doctor. **No exceptions.** Not "once I lose some weight" — apnea occurs in slim people too. Not "I'm too young for that" — it doesn't check birthdays. Not "my partner exaggerates" — a witnessed pause is exactly the observation a doctor wants to hear, and your bed partner is often a better historian of your night than you are, for the honest reason that they were conscious for it.

Why the urgency, stated soberly: breathing interruptions put repeated stress on the heart and blood vessels, untreated apnea is linked with high blood pressure and cardiovascular strain, and the daytime sleepiness it causes is a genuine accident risk — on the road above all. None of that is said to frighten you; it is said because the other half of the sentence is genuinely good news. **Apnea is treatable, and treatment works.** People who get it handled routinely describe waking up feeling rested for the first time in years. The gap between "untreated and slowly wearing down" and "treated and transformed" is one conversation wide. Have the conversation.

## When sleeplessness itself becomes the condition

Everything this book teaches about insomnia — the wind-down, stimulus control, the 3am playbook — works on ordinary bad patches and habits hardening in the wrong direction. But there is a point where sleeplessness crosses into clinical territory: **it has lasted months, it happens most nights, and it is damaging your days** — your mood, your focus, your functioning. That combination has a name — chronic insomnia — and it deserves more than a paperback.

Here is what an honest book tells you about what comes next, because it surprises most people. The internationally recognised first-line treatment for chronic insomnia is not a pill. It is **CBT-I — cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia** — a structured programme, usually a handful of weeks, delivered by a trained professional. And if you have read chapters three through eight, some of it will feel familiar, because those chapters are cousins of its components: the fixed wake time, the bed-means-sleep rule, getting up instead of churning, dismantling the performance pressure. Cousins — not the thing itself. The clinical version is systematic, personalised, supervised, and adjusted week by week to *your* nights. If self-help versions of these tools haven't shifted a months-long problem, that is not evidence they're wrong; it is evidence you've earned the full-strength version with a professional in your corner.

And sleeping pills, honestly: they exist, doctors prescribe them, and for short-term crises — a bereavement, a brutal stretch — they can be a legitimate bridge. But they are short-term tools with real limitations: the sleep they produce isn't quite the same as natural sleep, the body adapts, and they treat the night without touching the machinery that broke it — which is why the pill conversation belongs *after* the CBT-I conversation, not instead of it. What they are not, ever, is a self-prescription. Borrowing a family member's tablets, or improvising with whatever the pharmacy or the internet offers, is exactly the move this chapter exists to talk you out of. If you think you need chemical help, that is a doctor conversation — a real one, where the trade-offs get said out loud.

## The creeping legs, and three more patterns worth naming

**Restless legs.** If evenings bring a creeping, crawling, hard-to-describe urge to move your legs — worse when you're resting, briefly relieved by movement, reliably sabotaging the drowsy slide into sleep — that pattern has a name, restless legs syndrome, and it is one of the most missed and most treatable things in all of sleep medicine. People carry it for years assuming it's fidgetiness or stress. It isn't a character trait. One thing worth knowing walking in: iron status is a known factor, and checking it is a standard early step a doctor will take. This is a short appointment with a real chance of a large payoff.

Three more, briefly, all doctor territory:

- **Wakings with gasping, a pounding heart, or dread** that follows you into the day — chapter eight flagged these, and this is where they were pointed. Normal cycle-surfacing doesn't feel like that.
- **Heavy daytime sleepiness despite adequate hours.** If you genuinely give sleep seven to nine hours, most nights, and still fight to stay awake through the day, something is eating your nights from the inside — and finding out what is a professional's job.
- **Any sleep problem travelling with low mood that colours your days.** Sleep and mood run on shared machinery and drag each other in both directions. A doctor can look at the whole picture; a sleep book, honestly, cannot.

## How to open the conversation

Many people who clear every bar in this chapter still don't go — not from stubbornness, but because they don't know what to say, and "I sleep badly" feels too small to book an appointment over. So here is the script. Take it word for word if you like:

> *"I'm concerned about my sleep and I'd like it looked at properly. For [weeks/months], I've been having [loud snoring and my partner has seen me stop breathing / trouble sleeping most nights, and it's affecting my days / a crawling urge to move my legs in the evenings / severe sleepiness during the day even with a full night in bed]. Could we discuss screening, or a referral to someone who specialises in sleep?"*

That's it. Pattern, duration, daytime impact, request. Bring your bed partner's observations if you have them. If insomnia is the issue, asking *"is CBT-I available to me?"* by name tells the doctor precisely what you already understand.

And drop, here and now, any idea that this is admitting defeat. You have spent eleven chapters taking your sleep seriously — recognising a pattern that outranks self-help and putting it in front of the right professional is the *same skill*, applied at the boundary. It is the strongest move in this book. The person who books the appointment is not the one who failed; they're the one who read the map correctly.

If nothing in this chapter described your nights — if your problem is the ordinary, frustrating, fixable kind this book was built for — then everything you've read is about to get its final assembly.
