---------- DAY 1 ----------

In one trading session, Nvidia lost about 589 billion dollars of market
value.

No fab closed. No product failed. Headcount barely moved.

That day taught me most people — including many finance people — have
never been told what a market capitalisation actually is. It is the
last traded price multiplied by every share in issue. It is a price, not
a pile of money, and it is set at the margin by whoever traded last.

27 January 2025. Reported by CNBC and Bloomberg the same day.

If you hold tech shares in a fund, you own a piece of that number.


---------- DAY 2 ----------

Reported profit is partly an assumption. Here is the one that matters
most right now.

A data centre is full of servers. Those servers are capitalised as
assets and depreciated over an assumed useful life. Extend that assumed
life from four years to six, and the annual depreciation charge falls.
Reported profit rises.

Not a cent of cash has moved. Nothing has been sold. The machines are
the same machines.

Several large operators extended those assumed lives over the past few
years, disclosed it properly in the notes, and reported the effect.

The question nobody can answer yet: does AI hardware actually age the
way general-purpose servers did?


---------- DAY 3 ----------

Two of the most important companies in artificial intelligence publish
no audited accounts at all.

Anthropic closed a round at a 965 billion dollar valuation in May 2026.
OpenAI at 852 billion in March 2026.

A private valuation is not a market price. It is the price of the last
shares sold, on terms you cannot see, often with preferences attached
that mean the headline number overstates what ordinary shares are worth.

We quote those figures next to Apple's and Microsoft's as though they
were the same kind of number. They are not.


---------- DAY 4 ----------

The most important company in the world is one most people cannot name.

TSMC manufactures the leading-edge chips for Nvidia, Apple, AMD,
Broadcom and Qualcomm. Not some of them. Effectively all of them.

Its 2026 capital expenditure guidance was raised to 60–64 billion
dollars. That single company's spending decision sets the ceiling on
how much AI compute the world can have in 2029, because a fab takes
about three years to build.

Capacity is decided years before demand shows up. That is why this
industry has never escaped its cycle.


---------- DAY 5 ----------

Amazon and Google are both major investors in Anthropic.

They compete directly. They both put capital into the same laboratory,
alongside compute agreements, without either taking control.

Once you start drawing these lines, the same companies appear three
times in one diagram: as supplier, as customer, and as shareholder.

None of that is hidden. All of it is disclosed. But it is disclosed in
three different places, on three different timetables, and almost
nobody assembles it.


---------- DAY 6 ----------

Why memory shares moved so much, in plain terms.

DRAM was for decades the worst business in semiconductors — a
commodity, brutally capital-intensive, with a cycle that destroyed more
capital than it made. Dozens of makers became three.

Then AI accelerators turned out to be limited by memory bandwidth, not
raw arithmetic. High-bandwidth memory solves that. It is hard to make,
yields poorly, and has to be qualified by the customer.

A commodity became a specified, contracted, sold-out component.

Whether that breaks the cycle or merely lengthens it is the open
question. Every protection in that list has an expiry date.


---------- DAY 7 ----------

A supplier that holds equity in its own customers is not doing anything
improper. But it is worth understanding what it does to the numbers.

If a supplier invests in a customer, and the customer buys the
supplier's product, both companies report growth. The cash has moved in
a circle. Revenue recognition rules exist precisely for this, and
disclosure requirements exist precisely for this.

The point is not suspicion. The point is that a circle carries less
information than a straight line, and you should know which one you
are looking at.

The telecom equipment industry learned this in 2001.


---------- DAY 8 ----------

The constraint on AI has already moved twice, and most commentary is
still on the first one.

It was chips. Then it was memory. Then advanced packaging.

It is now becoming electricity — interconnection queues, transformer
lead times, and whether a site can actually get power this decade.

Profit follows the binding constraint. Whoever owns the current
bottleneck earns the rent, until the bottleneck moves.

Watch where it goes next. That is the whole game.


---------- DAY 9 ----------

The hardest numbers in any set of accounts are the ones nobody can
observe.

Not the transactions — those have invoices. The estimates. How long a
machine will earn its keep. What a minority stake in a private company
is worth. Whether a contract with a company that is simultaneously
customer, supplier and shareholder created one performance obligation
or four.

Every decisive judgement in this industry rests on the weakest tier of
evidence available to an auditor.

That is not a scandal. It is the job. But it should change how you read
a headline profit figure.
