# Working With Long Documents

A tender pack arrives at four in the afternoon. Two hundred pages and change, with appendices, a schedule of rates, and a covering email asking for a view by nine the next morning.

The obvious thing to do is what most people do. The whole document goes in, and one instruction goes after it: *summarise this and tell me the key risks.*

What comes back is good. That is the trap, and it is worth sitting with for a moment before we go any further. The summary is well organised. It names the actual sections. It picks up the payment terms, the liquidated damages provision, the insurance requirements, and it describes them accurately. Spot-check a dozen claims against the source and you find no invention at all.

And it has not mentioned a clause about two-thirds of the way in that changes the commercial shape of the whole thing.

Not misread it. Not summarised it clumsily. Simply not mentioned it — and nothing anywhere in that fluent, accurate, entirely honest answer indicates that there is anything to mention.

That is the specific problem of long documents, and it is a different animal from the one in Chapter 4. Hallucination is a wrong thing present. This is a right thing absent. A wrong thing present can be caught by checking. An absence has no shape at all, and you cannot proofread your way to noticing something that is not on the page.

## Why "summarise this 200-page document" is the wrong instruction

Three things go wrong at once, and they compound.

**Attention is uneven.** Chapter 5 made the general case and it is worth restating in its sharpest form here: a large context window guarantees that a lot of material *fits*, not that all of it is *weighed equally*. A model handed two hundred pages and a one-line request will attend more reliably to the beginning, to the end, and to whatever is structurally prominent — headings, opening paragraphs, anything that looks like a summary already. The clause buried in the middle of a dense schedule is exactly the material that is least likely to be well attended and, in professional documents, disproportionately likely to be the one that matters. Drafters do not put the awkward provision on page one.

**"Summarise" does not tell it what counts as important.** Chapter 9 called this the beige problem, and long documents make it worse, not better. Faced with two hundred pages and no criterion, the model produces what a summary of a document *of this type* usually contains. For a contract that means parties, term, payment, termination, liability. Those are genuinely the usual headline items. They are also, for that reason, precisely the items you were least likely to miss on your own.

**Completeness is not something the process can promise.** Go back to the mechanism. The model predicts the next token from what is in front of it. There is no step in that loop that maintains a checklist, no register of sections visited, no moment where the system asks itself whether anything has been left out. When you ask for "the key risks", you get a list of risks. You do not get *the* list, and no amount of insisting will change that, because insisting is just more text in the sequence.

Then comes the detail that makes it dangerous rather than merely limited. **The output length barely changes with the input length.** Ask for a summary of twenty pages and you get perhaps four hundred words. Ask for a summary of two hundred pages and you get perhaps four hundred words. The compression ratio has silently increased tenfold and nothing in the answer tells you so. It reads exactly as complete as the short one did.

You cannot tell what it skipped. That single sentence is why this chapter exists, and why the fix is a process rather than a better prompt.

## First, know which job you are doing

Before you touch the document, answer one question: is this a summary task or an extraction task? They look similar and they are not remotely the same job.

**A summary is compression, and it is tolerant.** You want the sense of the thing. If it captures nine of the ten themes and phrases the eighth slightly loosely, you have still been well served. Nobody is going to audit your understanding of the general drift. Losing detail is not a defect of a summary; it is the definition of one.

**An extraction is completeness, and it is unforgiving.** Every payment obligation. Every deadline. Every clause that mentions termination. Every commitment made in the meeting. Here, nine out of ten is not ninety per cent right — it is wrong, because the item you missed is the one that turns up later with someone's name attached to it. There is no partial credit in extraction. A list of obligations that is missing one obligation is a false document, and it is more dangerous than no list at all, because it looks finished.

Most professional work with long documents is extraction wearing the language of summary. "Give me a summary of the lease" almost always means "tell me every obligation that falls on us, every date, and every number." Say the second thing. The whole discipline that follows only makes sense once you have admitted which of the two you are actually doing.

## The pipeline

The reliable method is not a cleverer prompt. It is five stages, in this order, and the order is doing real work.

**Split by section. Summarise each. Extract to a table with references. Synthesise from what you built. Verify against the original.**

Why that order. Each stage produces something short enough that the next stage can genuinely attend to all of it — you are converting one impossible attention problem into five manageable ones. Synthesis comes fourth because it should operate on a compact, structured, referenced object rather than on two hundred pages. And verification comes last because it is the only stage that goes back to the source, and it is only affordable at all if stage three carried the references that make each check a two-second lookup.

Skip a stage and you get the failure mode that stage prevents. Skip splitting and you get uneven attention. Skip the table and you get prose you cannot check. Skip references and verification becomes a search, which means it becomes something you stop doing by Thursday.

### Stage one: split by the document's own structure

Not by page count. Never by page count.

Page-count splitting cuts clauses in half, separates a definition from the obligation it governs, and lands a table's header in one chunk and its numbers in another. Documents already come with a structure their authors built deliberately — parts, sections, schedules, annexes, agenda items, chapters, speaker turns. Use it. The contents page is your map, and the first useful thing you can do is turn it into a work list.

Two practical habits make this work.

Give every chunk its full heading path when you paste it — *Section 8: Payment / 8.3 Retention* — so the model knows where in the document it is standing. And carry a short **standing header** into every chunk: the parties, the key defined terms, the dates, and anything from the definitions section that changes what ordinary words mean in this document. A clause on page 147 is often unreadable without a definition on page 12, and the model has no way to go and look it up. You supply it or it guesses.

If a section is still too large, split at the next real boundary down. If a document genuinely has no structure — a badly scanned bundle, a raw transcript — impose one first, by date, by speaker, by document within the bundle, and record what you did.

### Stage two: summarise each section

Here is the sectional prompt. Notice how much of it is about what to preserve rather than what to compress:

    You are reviewing one section of a longer agreement on behalf of the
    party receiving the services. Below is Section 8 in full, with the
    defined terms that apply to it.

    [standing header: parties, defined terms, key dates]
    [the section]

    Working only from the text above, produce:
    1. Two sentences on what this section does.
    2. Every obligation it places on either party, quoted, with the
       clause number.
    3. Every figure, percentage, date and deadline it contains, quoted
       exactly as written, with the clause number.
    4. Every cross-reference it makes to another section or schedule.
    5. Anything ambiguous or unusual, flagged as such rather than
       resolved.
    If this section contains none of a category, write "none" for that
    category. Do not summarise anything outside the text above.

Three things in there are load-bearing. Quoting rather than paraphrasing means a figure arrives as a quotation you can check, not a number the model produced. The cross-reference list tells you where this section reaches into the rest of the document, which is how you catch the definition that changes everything. And "write none" gives the model an approved, low-effort way to report absence — without it, an empty category is indistinguishable from a category it did not consider.

### Stage three: extract into a table with references

Now collapse the section summaries into one structure. Named columns, one row per item, and a reference column that is not optional:

    From the section summaries below, build a single table of every
    obligation that falls on us.

    Columns, exactly these: Obligation (quoted or closely paraphrased) |
    Who it binds | Trigger or deadline | Financial consequence if missed |
    Clause reference | Section summary it came from.

    Rules: one obligation per row. If a row has no clause reference,
    do not include it — list it separately below the table under
    "no reference found". Do not merge similar obligations. Do not add
    anything that is not in the summaries.

    [the section summaries]

The reference column is the most valuable thing in this chapter, so it is worth being direct about why. It converts verification from a search into a lookup. Checking twenty extracted figures against a two-hundred-page document is an afternoon if you have to find each one, and about four minutes if each row tells you where to go. A control you can perform in four minutes is a control you will actually perform.

It has a second effect, and I will state it honestly because it is easy to oversell. Requiring a reference for every row makes fabrication harder work than accuracy, and things that are harder work are less probable continuations. It reduces invention. It does not eliminate it — a reference can itself be wrong, pointing to a real clause that says something else. So when you verify, check both halves: does the clause exist, and does it say what the row claims? That is the Three-Check Rule from Chapter 4 applied to a table.

### Stage four: synthesise

Only now do you ask for the thing you actually wanted — the risk note, the briefing, the summary for the partner. And you ask for it from your table and your section summaries, not from the original document.

This is the stage people are tempted to do first, and doing it last is what makes it defensible. Every claim in the synthesis traces to a row, and every row traces to a clause. You have built a chain of custody for a document, which is an unglamorous phrase for the thing that lets you answer "where did this come from?" three months later.

Be clear-eyed about what has happened, though: at this point the model is summarising a document *you* produced, so anything wrong upstream is now inherited and invisible. Which is exactly why there is a fifth stage.

### Stage five: verify against the original

Not against the summaries. Against the source.

Verify **every** figure that survives into the deliverable — all of them, without exception, because figures are what readers test first and what damage you most. Sample-check the rest: pick five rows at random, and pick them badly on purpose, from the dullest schedule rather than the interesting section. Then read the deliverable once as a hostile stranger, asking of each claim: which row is this, and which clause is that?

## The "did it find them all?" problem

Everything above improves quality. None of it answers the question that should still be nagging at you: *how do I know the list is complete?*

Honestly, you do not — not from the list itself. A model returns a good list without any guarantee that it is a whole list, and asking "is that all of them?" gets you "yes, that is all of them", which is a continuation, not a check. Completeness has to be established by some route other than asking.

Four routes, and they cost little:

**Inventory first, then tick.** Before any summarising, ask for a plain inventory of the document from its contents page and headings: every section, in order, with its heading and page range. Check that inventory against the actual document yourself — it is quick, because you are comparing headings, not reading. Then require that the final table accounts for every section, including the ones with nothing in them.

**Count, and make it count.** Ask how many clauses a section contains, or how many action items a transcript holds, before asking what they are. Then require the extraction to produce that many rows. A mismatch between the count and the table is a signal you would otherwise never have seen.

**Search for the words the answer hides behind.** You know them for your document type: *shall*, *must*, *no later than*, *indemnif*, *terminat*, *penalty*, *notify*. Plain text search in the original file, no model involved, and compare the hit count against your table. This is the cheapest completeness control available and the most neglected, largely because it is not clever.

**Run it twice and compare.** Extract the same section in two separate conversations with no shared history. Where the two lists agree you have some comfort; where they differ you have a specific place to look. Disagreement is informative even though agreement proves nothing.

None of the four proves completeness. What they do is convert an unbounded unknown into a bounded one — and being able to say what you checked, and how, is most of what professional defensibility consists of.

## The document you are not allowed to share

Sometimes the file cannot go anywhere near an assistant. Chapter 8 draws those lines and they do not move for convenience.

The pipeline helps more than you would expect here, in two ways. First, granularity: you may be permitted to work with one anonymised schedule when the full file is out of the question, and stage one has already cut the document into pieces small enough to redact properly. Second, the generic-framework approach from Chapter 8 fits this work perfectly. Rather than sending the document out, describe the *type* of document generically and ask for the checklist:

    You are a commercial reviewer with long experience of facilities
    agreements. I cannot share the document. Working only from this
    generic description — a three-year services agreement with a
    volume-based fee, a service credit regime, and a change control
    procedure — list the twenty provisions most often missed by a
    reviewer under time pressure, and for each, the words to search for
    in the document. Table, two columns, no preamble.

You then do the reading yourself, with a search list a careful colleague would have taken an hour to produce. The thinking arrives; the document never leaves.

## Comparing two long documents

If you take one practical recommendation from this chapter, take this one: difference-finding is among the highest-value things these systems do, and most people never ask for it.

The reason it works so well is structural. Comparison is bounded — the answer must come from one of the two texts in front of it, so there is much less room for invention than in open summarising. It is checkable, because every claimed difference points at two specific places you can look. And it is genuinely miserable work for a human, which is the sweet spot from Chapter 3: holding two dense versions side by side and noticing that "within thirty days" became "within thirty business days" is exactly the task where attention fails after twenty minutes and the machine does not get bored.

    You are comparing two versions of the same clause set. Version A is
    the version we approved. Version B is what came back from the other
    side.

    [Version A, clause by clause]
    [Version B, clause by clause]

    Produce two separate tables.
    Table 1 — Substantive changes: Clause | What A said (quoted) |
    What B says (quoted) | What changes in practice | Who it favours.
    Table 2 — Wording changes with no effect on meaning: Clause | Note.
    Then list, separately: clauses present in A but absent in B, and
    clauses present in B but absent in A.
    If you are unsure whether a change is substantive, put it in
    Table 1 and say why you were unsure.

The two-table split is the whole point. It forces classification instead of a flat dump of every altered comma, and it puts the burden of doubt in the right place — uncertain changes get escalated, not quietly filed under "cosmetic".

The same shape works across documents that are not versions of each other. A contract against its schedule of rates. A policy against the procedure that is supposed to implement it. A tender response against the tender's requirements list. Ask what is in one and unmatched in the other, in both directions, and you will find the gaps that ordinary reading does not surface. One honest note: for exact text comparison, a proper document comparison tool is more reliable and does not get creative. Use the model when the wording has changed and the question is what it *means*, not when the question is which characters moved.

## Transcripts, and the thing you must not do with them

Transcripts deserve their own warning, because they carry a risk the other document types do not.

**Speaker attribution is a guess.** The labels in a transcript — Speaker 1, Speaker 2, or a name your meeting tool has attached — come from an audio system separating voices, not from anything that knows who was in the room. Cross-talk, similar voices, a person moving away from the microphone, someone joining late on a different device: all of these scramble attribution routinely. Then a language model summarising that transcript treats the labels as fact, because they are the only facts it has.

The result is the most quietly dangerous output in this chapter: a clean, confident action list in which a commitment is attached to a named person who may not have made it.

So the rule is absolute, and it is short. **Never attribute a commitment, a concession, or a decision to a named person on the strength of a transcript alone.** Check the recording. To make that cheap, require a timestamp on every extracted item, so confirming who said something is thirty seconds of audio rather than a hunt. And where it matters, use the oldest control in professional life: circulate the action list and let people confirm their own items. That is not a workaround for AI. That is what good minutes always did.

## Which move first

| Document type | The right first move | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Contract or agreement | Extract the defined terms and the clause list; carry definitions into every chunk | Every figure and date against its clause; that each cross-reference points where it claims |
| Annual report or accounts | Split narrative from financial statements; treat the statements as extraction, never as summary | Every number against the statement page; that the figure's label matches; totals recalculated outside the model |
| Policy or procedure manual | Build a section inventory from the contents page before reading anything | Which version you were given; whether a later section overrides an earlier one |
| Meeting or interview transcript | Split by agenda item; extract commitments with speaker and timestamp | Speaker attribution against the recording; that a "decision" was a decision and not a suggestion |
| Tender or bid document | Extract the requirements into a numbered compliance table first | That every numbered requirement has a row; mandatory versus desirable wording; submission deadlines |
| Case file or evidence bundle | Build a chronology with a source page for each event | Dates and sequence against the underlying documents; what the bundle does *not* contain |

Read down the last column and the pattern from Chapter 1 shows up again. Where you brought the material, you are checking for drift and omission. You are never checking whether the model knew — it did not, and it did not need to.

## Do this today

Take a long document you already know well. That is essential: the exercise only teaches you something if you can mark the answers.

1. Ask for a one-shot summary of the whole thing, the lazy way. Keep it.
2. Now build a section inventory from the contents page and check it against the document yourself.
3. Run the sectional summary prompt on three sections — including the dullest schedule you can find.
4. Build the extraction table with the reference column, then verify every figure in it. Time yourself. Notice how fast the references make it.
5. Put the one-shot summary beside the table and ask the only question that matters: what is in the table that the summary never mentioned?

Whatever you find in step five is the size of the gap you have been living with.

You now have a method for the largest documents you will meet, and with it most of the craft in Part Two: how to write a prompt, how to be specific, how to use examples, how to fix a shape, how to make it think, how to make it critique, how to hold a voice, how to survive two hundred pages. Which means it is time to stop building and start diagnosing — because you will still get bad answers, and the difference between a frustrating tool and a reliable one is usually the ten seconds after a disappointing reply.
