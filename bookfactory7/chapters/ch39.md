# Insurance

Ask a claims handler what the job actually consists of and the answer, more often than not, is reading. A file arrives with a medical report, an engineer's report, two statements, a chain of emails, a schedule, a set of endorsements and a policy wording running to sixty pages. Somewhere inside all of that sits one question — is this loss covered, and if it is, for how much — and the answer usually turns on a single sub-clause and a single date.

The complaint is rarely about the reading itself. It is about the order of it. By the time the picture has been assembled, half the allotted time on the file is gone, and the part that actually required judgement gets the smaller half.

That is the shape of the opportunity in insurance, and it is also the shape of the danger. This is an industry built almost entirely out of documents and promises. Documents are the machine's home ground. Promises are not — because a promise to a customer is a decision about a person, and decisions about people are the one thing in this chapter you must not hand over.

Everything below assumes you have read Chapter 3, which gives you the four zones, and Chapter 8, which governs what may go into a tool at all. Insurance files are made of exactly the categories Chapter 8 tells you to treat most carefully: names, policy numbers, addresses, dates of birth, bank details, and — in health, life, travel and personal injury work — medical information about people who never chose to interact with an AI tool.

One more thing before we start. What follows is general professional information, not legal, regulatory or actuarial advice. Insurance is among the most heavily supervised industries there is, and the rules differ by jurisdiction, by line of business, and by whether you are an insurer, a broker, a managing agent or an adjuster. Check your own regulator, your own conduct rules, and your own firm's policy before you change anything.

## What this work actually consists of

Strip the industry to its working parts and you get five kinds of daily labour.

**Underwriting.** Deciding whether to take a risk, on what terms, and at what price. In practice that means reading proposal forms, broker submissions, surveys, loss runs and prior policies, then applying an appetite and a rating basis, then recording why. Much of the reading is repetitive. Almost none of the deciding is.

**Claims.** Receiving a notification, gathering evidence, establishing the facts, applying the wording to those facts, reserving, negotiating, settling or declining. The judgement is concentrated in two places — what actually happened, and what the wording actually says about it — and both are buried in paperwork.

**Policy servicing.** Endorsements, renewals, cancellations, mid-term adjustments, certificates, and the very large volume of ordinary correspondence that surrounds them. Low glamour, high volume, and the place where customers form most of their impression of you.

**Complaints.** A customer who is unhappy, a file to reconstruct, a regulated timetable, and a response that has to be accurate, fair, and comprehensible to someone who is not in the trade. Complaint handling is where poor documentation earlier in the chain comes home.

**Compliance and reporting.** Conduct rules, product governance, financial crime obligations, data protection duties, regulatory returns, and the narrative sections that accompany them. This work is judged not only on being right but on being evidenced.

Look at that list as a language problem and something jumps out. Four of the five are dominated by reading, comparing, explaining and recording. That is where a language model earns its keep. The fifth activity inside each of them — deciding — is where it must stop.

## The value map

The honest map for insurance follows straight from the zones in Chapter 3. What changes is the consequence column, because in this industry a wrong output does not merely embarrass you. It can land on a customer.

| Where it sits | The work | Why it lands there | What you must do about it |
|---|---|---|---|
| High value, low risk | Summarising a claims or underwriting file you supplied; drafting routine correspondence; turning existing procedures into training material and knowledge-base articles | Compression and transformation of material already in front of it | Spot-check every figure, date and name that travelled into the output |
| High value, low risk | Comparing two policy wordings you supplied, clause by clause | Difference-finding across two supplied texts | Confirm the differences are real, and that it found the ones that matter |
| High value, higher risk | Triage support; coverage-question research grounded in the wording; complaint response drafting; regulatory narrative drafting | Useful and genuinely time-saving, but touches a customer outcome or a regulator | Human decision on every output, recorded, with the reasoning owned by the human |
| High value, higher risk | Fraud-pattern flagging as a prompt to an investigator | Surfaces questions worth asking; cannot establish anything | Never treated as a finding; never disclosed to the customer as a reason |
| Lower value | Free-form brainstorming about your own book of business; asking it to recall market practice or a competitor's wording | It knows nothing about your book, and market claims arrive reference-shaped | Supply the documents, or do not ask |
| Do not go here | Automated declines, repudiations or underwriting refusals; final coverage opinions; pricing decisions; any decision about a person taken without a human | Accountability cannot be delegated, and the customer is owed a real reason | Keep human, always |

The pattern is the same one from Chapter 1. Where you bring the material, the machine is strong. Where you ask it to be the material — what the policy usually says, what the market normally does, what this claimant is probably up to — it is generating something answer-shaped, and in insurance that is a fair-treatment problem long before it is an accuracy problem.

## Ten use cases, with what to prompt and what to check

| # | Use case | What to prompt | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Claims file summarisation | Attach the file. Ask for a chronology, the parties, the loss described, the sums claimed, and the open questions — every line tagged with the document and page it came from | Each cited reference actually says what the summary claims; dates and amounts against source |
| 2 | Claims triage support | Ask it to classify against your written triage criteria and explain which criterion each file meets. Never ask which claims to decline | That the stated criterion genuinely applies; sample the ones it ranked lowest, not just the highest |
| 3 | Policy wording comparison | Supply both wordings. Ask for a clause-by-clause table of differences with both texts quoted verbatim | Quotations character by character against the originals; ask separately what it found in one document with no counterpart in the other |
| 4 | Underwriting file summarisation | Attach submission, loss runs and surveys. Ask for the risk described, the exposures, what is missing from the submission, and the questions a referral would ask | Every figure; that "missing" items are genuinely absent rather than overlooked |
| 5 | Customer correspondence drafting | Give the facts, the outcome already decided by a human, the reading age and the tone. Ask for a draft | That it states only decided facts; that it invents no timescale, entitlement or next step |
| 6 | Complaint response drafting | Supply the complaint, the file chronology and your findings. Ask for a response addressing each point raised in the customer's own order | That every point is answered; that findings are yours, not its; that it concedes nothing you have not decided to concede |
| 7 | Coverage-question research support | Paste the relevant sections of the actual wording. Ask which clauses bear on the question and what each says, with quotations, plus what the wording does not address | That every quotation exists in your document; that no reasoning rests on what such policies "usually" say |
| 8 | Fraud-pattern flagging | Ask it to list features of the file that an investigator would want explained, phrased as questions, with the evidence for each | That each flag is grounded in the file; strip out anything resting on where someone lives, their name, or their background |
| 9 | Training and knowledge-base building | Supply your procedures and wordings. Ask for scenario-based exercises, plain-English explainers and internal Q and A drafts | Technical accuracy by a subject expert before publication; that examples are illustrative and labelled as such |
| 10 | Regulatory and reporting narrative | Supply the data from your systems and the points to make. Ask it to write the narrative around figures it must not alter | Every figure against the system output; that no assertion of compliance has been invented |

Three of those deserve a longer word.

**Triage support means support.** The useful version sorts a queue by what your own written criteria say and shows its working, so a handler starts the day with the files most likely to need attention. The dangerous version is the same tool with the output renamed a recommendation and nobody reading the file underneath. The distinction is not technical. It is whether a human still forms and records the view.

**Coverage research is the one to be strict about.** Ask a model whether a claim is covered without supplying the wording, and it will answer — fluently, in the voice of an experienced practitioner, from a residue of every policy-shaped document it ever absorbed. The answer may even be usual market practice. Your customer did not buy usual market practice. They bought the words in your document, including the endorsement that changed one of them last renewal.

**Fraud flagging carries the heaviest ethical load in this chapter.** A model can notice that a claim was made shortly after inception, that two documents describe the same event differently, or that a supporting invoice has no reference number. Those are questions for an investigator. They are not findings, they are never evidence, and they must never reach a customer as a reason for anything. Chapter 59 goes further into how bias enters; here it is enough to say that any feature which is a proxy for who someone is, rather than what happened, has no place in the prompt or the output.

## Three worked prompts

These use the six-part frame from Chapter 9: role, task, context, constraints, format, example. All names, figures and references below are invented for illustration. Before you run anything like this on a live file, settle the Chapter 8 questions first — which tier you are on, and what has been redacted.

Comparing two policy wordings, clause by clause:

    ROLE: You are an experienced policy wording technician in a general
    insurance business. You work only from the documents in front of you.

    TASK: Compare Document A (our current wording) with Document B (the
    proposed replacement wording) and produce a clause-by-clause table of
    substantive differences.

    CONTEXT: Both documents are attached. Document A is the wording in
    force for this product; Document B is a draft revision. The table will
    go to product governance and to the claims technical team, who need to
    know what changes for a customer at claim stage. They are technical
    readers. They do not need the drafting history.

    CONSTRAINTS:
    - Quote the actual text of both clauses, verbatim, in the table. Do not
      paraphrase the wording itself; paraphrase only in the "effect"
      column.
    - Ignore purely typographical, numbering and formatting differences.
      Say in one line at the end that you have done so.
    - If a clause exists in one document with no counterpart in the other,
      give it its own row and mark the missing side "no equivalent found".
    - If you are unsure whether a difference is substantive, include it and
      mark it "uncertain — for human review". Do not decide it for me.
    - Do not comment on whether either wording is compliant, fair, or
      market standard. That is not this task.
    - Use nothing outside the two attached documents. If you find yourself
      relying on what policies of this type usually say, stop and mark that
      row "outside the documents".

    FORMAT: A table with columns — Clause reference (A) | Text (A) |
    Clause reference (B) | Text (B) | Effect of the change on a claim |
    Substantive or uncertain. Below the table, a short list headed "Clauses
    with no counterpart".

    EXAMPLE of the depth I want in the effect column: "The qualifying
    period before cover attaches lengthens from seven days to fourteen. A
    loss occurring in the second week after inception, previously covered,
    would not be."

What to check before that table goes anywhere: pick five rows at random and read both quotations against the originals. Then, separately, run the comparison the other way round — B against A — because "what is in A that is missing from B" and "what is in B that is missing from A" are different questions, and a model will often answer the first well and the second thinly.

Summarising a claims file with source references:

    ROLE: You are a senior claims technician preparing a file note for a
    colleague who has not seen this claim before.

    TASK: Produce a factual summary of the attached claim file.

    CONTEXT: The file has been redacted: the insured is "the policyholder",
    the claimant is "the claimant", and identifying details have been
    removed. The reader is an experienced handler who will make the
    coverage decision themselves. This note exists to save them the first
    reading, not to reach a conclusion for them.

    CONSTRAINTS:
    - Every factual statement must carry a reference in the form
      [document name, page]. If you cannot give a reference, do not make
      the statement.
    - Do not express a view on whether the claim is covered, whether the
      claimant is credible, or what the outcome should be.
    - Where two documents in the file conflict, record both versions and
      mark the conflict. Do not resolve it.
    - Do not convert, total or recalculate any monetary amount. Quote
      amounts exactly as they appear.
    - If something a handler would expect is absent from the file — a
      report, a statement, a schedule — list it under "Not in the file"
      rather than assuming it exists.
    - Plain professional English. No adjectives about the parties.

    FORMAT:
    1. One-paragraph description of the loss as notified.
    2. Chronology table: Date | Event | Source reference.
    3. Sums claimed, itemised, each with its source reference.
    4. Points of conflict between documents.
    5. Not in the file.
    6. Open questions for the handler, as questions.

    EXAMPLE of a chronology row: | 14 March | Claimant reports water
    ingress to the ground floor | [Notification form, p.1] |

Then verify by sampling. Take four references at random and open the pages. You are testing one thing only: does the cited page actually say what the summary says it says. Do that for the first ten files you run and you will have a real sense of how far the tool can be trusted on your material — which is worth more than anyone's assurance, this book's included.

Drafting a complaint response, in compressed form:

    ROLE: You are a complaints handler in a regulated insurance business,
    writing to a customer.

    TASK: Draft a response to the attached complaint.

    CONTEXT: The findings are mine and are set out below; the chronology is
    attached. The customer complains about three things, in this order:
    [list]. On point one we accept the service fell short. On points two
    and three we do not, for the reasons given below. The customer is not
    an insurance professional.

    CONSTRAINTS:
    - Address the three points in the customer's own order, using their own
      words for what they complained about.
    - State only the findings I have given you. Do not add findings, do not
      soften them, and do not concede anything I have not conceded.
    - Where we accept we fell short, say so plainly in the first sentence
      of that section. No throat-clearing.
    - Do not state any timescale, entitlement, referral right or next step
      that is not in the material I have supplied. Leave [TO CONFIRM] where
      one is needed.
    - Explain the relevant policy term by quoting it and then restating it
      in plain English.
    - No jargon, no defensive tone, no blaming the customer.

    FORMAT: A letter. Short paragraphs. One heading per complaint point.

    EXAMPLE of the register I want: "You told us you called on three
    occasions and were not called back. We have listened to those calls.
    You are right, and I am sorry."

Note what the constraints are doing. They are not making the model honest — nothing does that. They are making the sentences you must check visibly different from the sentences you supplied, which is the difference between reviewing a draft and rewriting it.

## The risks, taken seriously

| Risk | How it actually shows up | The control |
|---|---|---|
| No real reason for a decision | A customer asks why, and the file records an output rather than a rationale | The human decision-maker writes the reason in their own words, on the file, before the letter goes |
| Automated adverse outcomes | A decline, repudiation or refusal issued without a person forming a view | A hard rule: no adverse customer outcome without human decision and human sign-off |
| Bias through proxies | Postcode, name, language, occupation or claims-history shorthand quietly standing in for who someone is | Strip such features from prompts; test outputs for them; escalate patterns rather than explaining them away |
| Ungrounded coverage opinions | Confident reasoning about what the policy says, produced without the policy | Never ask a coverage question without pasting the actual wording and endorsements in force |
| Personal and health data exposure | Medical reports, dates of birth, bank details pasted into an unapproved tool | Chapter 8 in full: know your tier, redact by default, work on extracts |
| Unreconstructable decisions | Months later, nobody can say what the model was given or what it produced | Keep the prompt, the material, the output and the human's reasoning together in the claim record |

Four of those deserve to be said in full sentences rather than table rows.

**A customer affected by a decision is entitled to a reason, and "the model suggested it" is not one.** This is not a technology principle. It is the oldest principle in regulated conduct, and it long predates any of this. If your reason cannot be written in a sentence a customer could understand, and defended by a named person, you do not yet have a decision. You have an output.

**Never automate a decline, a repudiation, or an underwriting refusal.** Automating the paperwork around a decision a human has made is fine and often excellent. Automating the decision itself is where insurance goes badly wrong, and it goes wrong at scale and in one direction, because a system does not have a bad Tuesday — it has the same flaw on every file it touches until someone notices.

**Bias creeps in through proxies, not through intent.** Nobody writes a prompt that says treat these customers less favourably. What happens is subtler: a triage prompt that mentions the neighbourhood, a fraud prompt that includes a name, a summary that carries forward a previous handler's adjective. The model does not know which features are permissible in your jurisdiction. You do. Review prompts as carefully as you review outputs, because a prompt is applied to everyone.

**You must be able to reconstruct how a decision was reached.** Supervisors, ombudsmen and courts all ask the same question after the fact, and they ask it years later. If AI assistance touched a customer file, the record needs to show what was supplied, what came back, what the human concluded, and why. Build that into the workflow at the start. Retrofitting a record you did not keep is not possible.

## A 30-day starting plan

**Week 1 — settle the ground rules and pick one thing.** Confirm which tool is approved and at which tier, and what may be entered. Write a one-page internal rule covering redaction, the no-automated-adverse-outcomes line, and what gets kept on file. Choose exactly one use case, and choose a boring one: claims file summarisation or wording comparison. Not triage.

**Week 2 — build the prompt and a baseline.** Write the prompt in the six-part frame. Collect ten real, redacted files that a person has already worked on, so you know the right answer. Time how long a handler currently takes on the first reading. Write that number down before you see any AI output.

**Week 3 — run the ten and count the failures.** Run all ten. For each, check every reference and every figure against source. Record what went wrong, not what went well: the invented page number, the merged date, the missing conflict between two documents. Fix the prompt one constraint at a time, then re-run all ten from the start.

**Week 4 — put it in front of two people and decide.** Give the prompt and the checking routine to two colleagues, one enthusiastic and one sceptical. Watch what they do with it, especially where they stop checking. Then decide honestly: keep it, fix it, or drop it. Write a short note recording the decision and the evidence, and file it where your governance function can find it. If you keep it, book the review date now.

## How to measure it honestly

Resist the temptation to produce a number that flatters the project. Measure four things, all of them yours.

**Time, measured properly.** Compare the same task before and after, on comparable files, with the checking time included — because checking is part of the work now. If handlers were spending forty minutes on a first reading and now spend fifteen reading a summary and eight verifying it, that is your honest figure. If the saved time simply fills with more files, say so; it is still a result, just a different one.

**Quality, scored against criteria you wrote first.** Take a sample of outputs and score them against a short rubric — factually accurate, correctly referenced, right tone, nothing invented. Score the pre-AI equivalent too. Without a baseline you have an opinion, not a measurement.

**Error rate, counted rather than felt.** How many outputs in your sample contained a wrong reference, a wrong figure, or an assertion not supported by the file? That single number tells you more about whether to expand than any enthusiasm in the room. Track it over time, because prompts drift and material changes.

**Customer-facing outcomes, watched but not claimed.** Complaint volumes, complaint upheld rates, reopened claims, requests for explanation. Watch them. Do not attribute movements to your project without evidence — too many things move at once in an insurance operation, and a claim you cannot support is exactly the habit this chapter is asking you to avoid.

And record what did not work. A pilot that saved nothing on underwriting summaries but a good deal on wording comparison is a genuinely useful finding, and far more credible to a board than a project where everything succeeded.

## Do this today

Ninety minutes, and you will know more than any vendor demonstration can tell you.

1. Take two versions of a policy wording you already know well — this year's and last year's, or two variants of the same product. Nothing confidential is needed; your own published wordings will do.
2. Run the clause-comparison prompt above, unchanged.
3. Check five rows against the originals, character by character.
4. Now run it the other way round, B against A, and see what appears that did not appear the first time. That difference is the chapter's whole lesson about completeness.
5. Finally, in a fresh conversation with no documents attached, ask a coverage question about that same product. Read the answer carefully. It will be fluent, plausible, and built from no policy in particular — which is precisely the answer you must never let near a customer.

That last step is worth doing once, properly, because it inoculates you. Everything in this chapter that sounds like caution is really just that experience, written down.
