# Finance and Accounting

It is day four of the close and the numbers have stopped moving.

The trial balance is final, the accruals are posted, the intercompany differences are down to something you can live with. What is left is the part that always takes longer than anyone plans for: the pack. Nine pages of commentary, a variance page for each of three regions, a paragraph explaining why the gross margin moved, a note on the two provisions the auditors will ask about, an email to the sales director who wants to know why his cost line looks worse than he remembers, and a board paper due Thursday that currently exists as four bullet points and a sinking feeling.

None of that is arithmetic. The arithmetic finished on Tuesday. What is left is explanation — turning a set of correct numbers into something a reader can act on, in a register that suits them, by a deadline that does not move.

That is language work. It is also, for most finance teams, where the hours actually go.

This chapter opens the industry playbooks, and every one that follows uses the same seven-part shape: what the work actually consists of, where the value sits, ten concrete use cases with what to prompt and what to check, worked prompts in the six-part frame from Chapter 9, the risks particular to the sector, a thirty-day starting plan, and an honest way to tell whether any of it helped. Read this one even if you do not work in finance. The pattern transfers.

One line before we start, and it is meant. **This chapter is general professional guidance, not accounting, audit, tax or regulatory advice.** Your standards, your regulator, your firm's methodology and your employer's policy govern you. Nothing here relaxes any of them.

## What this work actually consists of

Ask a finance professional what they do and you will get the org chart answer: reporting, control, planning, analysis. Watch a month instead, and the shape is different.

A large part of the month is a **cycle you cannot reschedule**. Close runs on the same days regardless of what else is happening, and the work compresses into a fortnight-shaped spike: journals, reconciliations, review, pack, commentary, distribution. The second half of the month is the part people mean when they say they never get to the value-added work, and it is routinely eaten by the first half of the next one.

A second large part is **explanation on demand**. A budget holder wants to know why their line moved. A director wants a number reconciled to a different number that was produced for a different purpose. A lender wants a covenant calculation restated. Almost none of these questions is hard; nearly all of them require assembling context, writing carefully, and being right.

A third part is **evidence and documentation**. Reconciliations that must show what the difference is, not merely that it exists. Process notes that are two reorganisations out of date. Audit requests that arrive in a list of forty items, each needing a file, a sentence, and someone to chase.

A fourth part is **judgement** — genuinely hard, genuinely yours. Is this provision adequate. Does this arrangement meet the recognition criteria. Is the forecast the business gave you plausible or aspirational. Should this go to the audit committee now or next quarter.

And threaded through all of it is **coordination**: emails, chasing, re-explaining, the same question answered four times in four registers.

Notice the proportions. The judgement is the part that requires you and cannot be delegated to anything. The other four parts are drafting, restructuring, extraction, and correspondence — which is to say, the sweet spot of Chapter 3 sitting in the middle of your week wearing a finance costume.

## The value map

Chapter 3 sorted tasks into four zones by asking whether you are transforming material you supplied or asking the machine to be the source of truth, whether you can verify quickly, and what an unnoticed error costs. Here is that map applied to a finance function.

| Finance activity | Zone | What AI genuinely does here | What it must not do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Close commentary and reporting narrative | Sweet spot | Turns your figures and your causes into readable, consistent prose | Supply the causes, or restate figures from memory |
| Restructuring a pack for a different audience | Sweet spot | Re-registers the same content for board, bank, budget holder | Change what the content means while changing its tone |
| Extraction from long documents (agreements, policies, prior packs) | Sweet spot | Pulls terms into a table you can check | Guarantee it found everything |
| Drafting emails, memos, and stakeholder replies | Sweet spot | First draft in seconds, in your register | Send anything unread |
| Process notes and desk instructions | Sweet spot | Turns your walkthrough into a documented procedure | Invent the steps you forgot to describe |
| Explaining a standard or concept to you | Good | Clear teaching of well-known ideas; better questions to ask | Be the authority you rely on |
| Structuring a policy assessment or a forecast narrative | Good | Frameworks, option lists, counter-arguments | Reach the conclusion for you |
| Analysis over your numbers | Caution | Describes patterns you point it at; drafts the interpretation | Calculate, total, rank by amount, or restate a figure |
| Reconciliation and completeness work | Caution | Triages differences, drafts explanations, groups exceptions | Confirm that a reconciliation is complete or correct |
| Accounting policy conclusions and sign-off | Danger | Prepares the paper the preparer and reviewer then own | Be the judgement, the reviewer, or the signature |

Read the right-hand column downwards. Everything in it is a thing a finance professional is paid to be accountable for. That is not a coincidence — it is the boundary, and it holds for every playbook in this part of the book.

## Ten use cases

Each of these is ordinary work. For each, what to ask for and what to check before it leaves your hands.

| # | Use case | What to prompt | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Month-end close commentary | Attach the pack; supply what actually happened this month; ask for commentary in a fixed structure, material items only, inferences flagged | Every figure against the pack; that no cause was invented; that nothing material was silently dropped |
| 2 | Management reporting packs | Ask it to convert an agreed structure into consistent narrative sections, one per business unit, same order and depth each month | Consistency against last month's pack; that unit-specific facts did not migrate between sections |
| 3 | Variance analysis narrative | Give it the variance table and your list of drivers; ask for one row of commentary per material variance, cause first | Sign and direction of every variance; that "favourable" means what your convention says |
| 4 | Reconciliation support | Paste the difference listing; ask it to group items by likely type, propose the test that would confirm each, and list what is unexplained | The grouping against the items themselves; that "unexplained" was not quietly filled in |
| 5 | Budget and forecast drafting | Ask for the assumption schedule structure, the questions to ask each budget holder, and the narrative once your numbers are fixed | That no assumption value was authored by the model; that the narrative matches the model's outputs |
| 6 | Accounting policy interpretation | Paste the actual text of the standard and your fact pattern; ask for the criteria, how each is met or not, and what evidence is needed | Every reference against the real standard; that the fact pattern was not improved in the retelling |
| 7 | Board paper drafting | Supply the decision, the options, the numbers and the recommendation; ask for the house paper format, one page | That the recommendation is yours; that no figure appears which is not in the appendix |
| 8 | Audit-request response drafting | Give the request list and your file inventory; ask for a covering response per item, flagging what is outstanding and who owns it | That nothing is described as provided which is not; scope of what you are asserting |
| 9 | Process documentation | Walk it through the process in rough speech; ask for a numbered procedure with controls, inputs, outputs and exceptions marked | That every step is real; that no control was invented to make the document look tidy |
| 10 | Finance-team and stakeholder communication | Give the facts, the audience and the outcome you want; ask for a short message in plain professional English | Tone against the relationship; that no commitment was made on your behalf |

Five of those deserve a sentence more.

**Reconciliation support** is the one people expect to be impossible and find useful. You are not asking it to reconcile. You are asking it to look at eighty difference lines and say: these forty look like timing, these twenty look like a coding error in one cost centre, these fifteen share a counterparty, these five are unlike anything else. That is classification against criteria you supplied — sweet spot work — and it turns an evening of sorting into a review of someone else's sorting. The reconciliation is still yours. The completeness assertion is still yours.

**Accounting policy interpretation** is only safe grounded. Ask a bare assistant what a standard requires and you will get something standard-shaped, confidently numbered, and occasionally wrong in exactly the way Chapter 4 described. Paste the actual paragraphs and the task changes character entirely: it is now reading a document in front of it. Even then, the output is a paper for a human to challenge, not a conclusion.

**Audit-request drafting** carries a specific trap. The model will happily write "as requested, please find attached the supporting schedule" for an item where nothing is attached, because that is what such sentences look like. Every assertion in a response to an auditor is a representation. Read them as representations.

**Process documentation** is the highest-value use most finance teams never try, because writing procedures is nobody's favourite afternoon. Describe the process aloud, transcribe it, and hand the transcript over. What comes back is eighty per cent of a desk instruction. The twenty per cent you correct is the part where you were vague — which is itself worth knowing.

**Stakeholder communication** is where the daily minutes hide. Not one dramatic saving: forty small ones, each a message you would have rewritten three times because it had to be firm without being rude.

## Three worked prompts

The variance commentary prompt was built line by line in Chapter 9; rather than repeat it, here are three others in the same six-part frame. Everything in square brackets is yours to replace, and nothing confidential should go in until you have settled the question in Chapter 8.

**One: an accounting policy note, grounded in the standard.**

    You are a technical accounting manager preparing a file note for
    review by a partner.

    Task: assess whether the arrangement described below meets the
    criteria in the standard text I have pasted, and draft the file note.

    Context: [describe the arrangement in generic terms — the parties by
    role, the obligations, the timing, the consideration, and what the
    business believes the answer is and why.] The reviewer is
    technically strong and short of time. The note supports [the
    treatment in the [PERIOD] accounts].

    Constraints:
    - Use only the standard text I have pasted. Do not cite any
      paragraph, standard or interpretation that does not appear in it.
    - Where the pasted extract does not address a point, write "not
      addressed in the extract provided" rather than reasoning from
      general knowledge.
    - Do not state a conclusion as settled. Present the criteria, the
      arguments each way, and what evidence would resolve it.
    - No figures unless I supplied them.
    - Plain professional English, no more than 700 words.

    Format: (1) The arrangement in five lines. (2) A table with columns
    Criterion, Met / not met / unclear, Basis in the extract, Evidence
    needed. (3) "Points for the reviewer" — three bullets, the weakest
    parts of the analysis first.

    Example of the register I want: [paste four lines from a file note
    your firm was happy with.]

Notice the two constraints doing the heavy lifting: the grounding rule that forbids anything outside the pasted text, and the instruction to present rather than conclude. Together they turn a dangerous request into a checkable one.

**Two: a board paper first draft.**

    You are a finance director drafting a paper for a board that
    includes two non-executive directors without a finance background.

    Task: write the first draft of a one-page decision paper.

    Context: the decision is [state it in one sentence]. The options are
    [A], [B] and [C]. My recommendation is [X] because [two reasons].
    The board already knows [what they were told last time]. The
    financial effect is set out in the appendix I have attached; do not
    restate any figure from it in the body except the three I list here:
    [figure, label; figure, label; figure, label].

    Constraints:
    - The recommendation is mine and must be stated as management's, not
      hedged into a menu.
    - No figure anywhere in the body other than the three given above,
      quoted exactly as written.
    - No jargon a non-financial director would have to look up; if a
      technical term is unavoidable, define it in the same sentence.
    - Name the two most likely objections and answer them in one
      sentence each. Do not pretend the risks are smaller than stated.
    - One page. Under 500 words.

    Format: Purpose (2 lines) / Background (4 lines) / Options and
    assessment (short table) / Recommendation (3 lines) / Risks and
    mitigations (2 bullets) / Decision requested (1 line).

The figures instruction is the one to copy into every finance prompt you write. Restricting the model to three figures, quoted exactly, converts a whole class of risk into something you can verify by eye in ten seconds.

**Three: reconciliation exception triage.**

    You are a financial controller reviewing an unreconciled difference
    listing.

    Task: group the items below into likely categories and propose one
    test for each category.

    Context: this is the [balance sheet account] reconciliation at
    [PERIOD END]. Normal causes in this account are [timing between
    systems, mis-coded cost centres, unposted receipts, FX retranslation
    — amend to your own]. The account is [material / immaterial] to the
    result and is reviewed by [role].

    Constraints:
    - Do not calculate, total, or rank anything by amount. I will do the
      arithmetic.
    - Every item must appear in exactly one group, and every item must
      appear. Do not summarise the listing.
    - Items you cannot place go in a group called "Unexplained" — do not
      guess a cause to empty it.
    - Do not state that the reconciliation is complete or correct.

    Format: one table, columns Group, Item references, Likely cause,
    Test that would confirm it. Below the table, one line: how many
    items are in "Unexplained".

That last constraint is not decoration. Left to its own devices a model will tidy the awkward group away, because tidy tables are the likelier continuation. Naming the residue and asking for its size makes the awkward part visible, which is the only part that needed your attention anyway.

## The risks that bite in this sector

### The arithmetic warning, unmissable

**The model drafts the commentary. The spreadsheet does the maths.**

If you take one sentence from this chapter, take that one. Chapter 3 explained the mechanism: predicting a plausible next token is not the operation of adding up. In finance the consequence is sharper than elsewhere, because the failure does not look like a failure. It looks like a number.

Three ways it actually shows up:

**Drift.** You supply a figure and the model restates it in prose one digit different, or rounds it in a way that no longer ties to the pack. Close is worse than wrong; close survives a glance.

**Silent recalculation.** You give it revenue and cost; it helpfully mentions the margin percentage. Nothing computed that. It was generated to look right.

**Sign and convention.** Favourable and adverse, positive and negative variances, credits shown as brackets or as minus signs — conventions differ between systems and packs, and the model will follow the most common convention in its training rather than yours, then write a confident sentence in the wrong direction.

The control is not vigilance. Vigilance fails at eleven at night on day four. The control is structural: **let the spreadsheet own every number, and forbid the model to type any figure you did not give it.** Better still, have it write the narrative with placeholders — `[REVENUE_MOVEMENT]` — and populate them from the model of record. Where an assistant can genuinely run code or read the file and calculate, that is a different arrangement and a real one; the professional habit is simply to know which is happening. If nothing computed, nothing was computed.

### The rest

| Risk | Why it is worse in finance | The control |
|---|---|---|
| Confidential employer or client data | Draft accounts, forecasts, deal terms and board papers are exactly the never-paste categories | Establish your deployment tier before you paste; redact to role labels and scale indicators; work on extracts |
| Price-sensitive information | Unreleased results for a listed entity carry market-abuse obligations sitting on top of confidentiality, enforced far more aggressively | Treat pre-release numbers as untouchable outside an approved, contracted environment; when in doubt, do not |
| Invented causes | Variance commentary is a genre that demands a cause, so cause-shaped text appears whether or not a cause was supplied | Supply the real drivers; require inferred explanations to be flagged in brackets |
| False completeness | "Have I missed anything" reads as assurance and is not | Use it as a prompt to think; completeness assertions remain yours |
| Standards and citations | Paragraph references look authoritative and are generated, not retrieved | Ground in pasted text; verify every reference in the actual standard |
| Consistency across periods | A slightly different framing each month reads as a change in the business | Keep one saved prompt and one example row; change it deliberately, not accidentally |
| Over-smooth prose | Fluent narrative can glide over a real problem the numbers are showing | Read the pack first, then the commentary; never the other way round |
| Audit trail | You may need to show how a figure or conclusion was reached | Keep the prompt, the source file, and the human review evidence with the working papers |

### The duty that does not move

No AI output is a substitute for the preparer's or the reviewer's judgement. A pack you did not read is not a pack you prepared. A policy note you did not challenge is not a note you reviewed. If your name is on it — as preparer, reviewer, approver, or signatory — the accountability is entirely yours, and the fact that a draft arrived quickly is not a fact anyone will find interesting afterwards.

Say so out loud in your team, early. The failure mode in finance functions is not people trusting the machine too much on hard questions. It is a reviewer skimming a well-written page because well-written pages feel reviewed.

## A thirty-day starting plan

Thirty days, one hour a week of deliberate practice, no budget required beyond whatever tool you are already approved to use.

**Week 1 — one task, done properly.** Pick the single piece of writing you resent most. For most people it is commentary or a recurring stakeholder email. Build it in the six-part frame, run it four times, and keep the version that worked. Do not tell anyone yet. Before you start, write down honestly how long that task took you last month.

**Week 2 — build the two assets.** First, a saved prompt file with your three or four most repeated tasks, each parameterised with placeholders written as notes to yourself. Second, a short standing instruction: who you are, what entity and sector, your house register, your figure rule (no number the model did not receive), your uncertainty rule (say "not in the pack" rather than estimating). Everything downstream gets better once these exist.

**Week 3 — put your material in front of it.** Move from typing context to attaching it: the pack template, last month's approved commentary, the chart of accounts descriptions, the process notes. If your setup supports a persistent project workspace, this is the week to build one. Then try the harder ground: one reconciliation triage, one policy note grounded in pasted standard text. Run the Three-Check Rule on both — numbers against source, sources against existence, reasoning as a chain — and note what you found.

**Week 4 — one workflow and one measurement.** Choose the single task from weeks 1 to 3 that repaid the effort most, write it up as a one-page procedure a colleague could follow, and agree with your manager where the human checkpoint sits. Then time the same task again, the same way you timed it in week 1, and write both numbers down. Take the honest result to your team, including the parts that did not work.

Two things not on the plan. Do not start with the close itself — the close is the worst possible week to be learning anything. And do not start with a tool procurement exercise; you are trying to find out whether this helps your work, which is a different question from which product to buy.

## How to tell whether it actually worked

This book refuses to tell you what savings to expect, because nobody honestly can and the figures in circulation are marketing. What you can do is measure your own work, which is better evidence anyway.

**Time your own tasks, before and after.** Pick three recurring tasks. Record how long each took across a few real instances before you changed anything — that baseline is the part everyone skips and the part without which nothing else means anything. Then record the same three tasks the same way afterwards. Include the time you spent prompting, re-prompting and verifying, because that is part of the new method.

**Count errors and where they were caught.** How many figures did review find wrong? How many statements had to be corrected before issue? Compare like with like: a pack has a normal error rate already, and the question is whether the new method changed it, in which direction, and whether the errors moved to a later and more expensive point in the process.

**Count rework.** Review comments per pack, drafts per board paper, rounds per email. Rework is a good proxy for quality precisely because nobody has to judge anything subjectively to count it.

**Ask the reader.** Do the people who receive the commentary find it more useful, less useful, or the same? Two questions in a corridor is a legitimate measurement and often more informative than a stopwatch.

Now three honest cautions about what those numbers mean.

**Time saved is not money saved.** An hour freed on day four of close is money only if the hour is genuinely reallocated to something valuable, or if it is an hour you would have billed or paid overtime for. If it simply becomes an hour of a slightly calmer afternoon, that is a real benefit to a human being and a poor line in a business case. Say which one you are claiming.

**Beware the flattering comparison.** Do not measure your best assisted run against your worst manual one, or a fresh, well-rested attempt against a task you did at midnight. Same task, same conditions, several instances.

**Watch the confounders.** Close cycle times move for a dozen reasons — a system change, a quieter month, a new starter finding their feet. If close came down by a day, ask what else changed before you attribute it.

Measured honestly, most finance teams find the gain concentrated in a narrow band: drafting, restructuring, and correspondence, done many times a month. That is not a disappointing answer. It is a durable one, and it is defensible in front of the sort of person who asks for evidence.

## Do this today

Forty minutes, and you will finish with something you keep.

1. Open last month's pack and mark, in the commentary, every sentence that is explanation rather than calculation. That proportion is your opportunity.
2. Write down how long the commentary took, and how long the accompanying emails took. This is your baseline; you cannot recreate it later.
3. Take one section and build it in the six-part frame, including the two finance-specific constraints: no figure the model did not receive, and "not in the pack" instead of an estimate.
4. Run it on redacted or generic material, and run the Three-Check Rule on the output.
5. Write one line at the bottom of the saved prompt recording what you had to fix. That line is next month's constraint.

Then a fair warning about where this goes next. Everything in this chapter assumed you are preparing the numbers — that you own the record, know what happened, and are explaining your own work. Change one thing about that position, and put yourself on the other side of the table, examining someone else's numbers with an obligation of scepticism and a file that must stand up years later, and every use case here needs re-testing against a stricter standard.

That is audit, and it is the next chapter.
