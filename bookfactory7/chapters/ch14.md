# The Critique Loop: Making AI Grade Its Own Work

It is possible to improve a proposal four times in a row and end up with something worse than the first version. It happens more often than anyone admits.

The draft is decent. Two pages, a clear offer, a price, a timetable. You read it, feel the vague dissatisfaction everyone feels reading their own draft, and type the sentence that comes most naturally in the world:

    Make this better.

Back came two and a half pages. Every sentence had been polished. Three new adjectives had arrived. The timetable now had an introductory paragraph explaining that the timetable was indicative. He read it, felt the same vague dissatisfaction, and typed it again. And again.

By the fourth pass the proposal was four pages long, contained the phrase "leverage our deep expertise," hedged the price with "typically," and had lost the one sentence in the original that had actually said something specific about the client's problem. Nothing had gone wrong in any single step. Each version was a reasonable response to the instruction it was given. The instruction was the problem.

That is the failure this chapter exists to prevent, and the technique that prevents it is the most reliable quality tool in the whole book — provided you understand exactly what it can and cannot do.

## Why "make it better" produces length instead of quality

Go back to the mechanism. The model predicts the next token given everything in front of it. When you say *make this better*, what is in front of it is a competent draft and an instruction containing no standard whatsoever.

So ask yourself what text most plausibly follows a competent draft and a request for improvement. Not a shorter draft: shortening requires deciding what is expendable, which requires knowing what the document is for. Not a restructured draft: restructuring requires a view about what the reader needs first. What plausibly follows is *more of the same thing, slightly more elaborately expressed* — because in the enormous body of writing these systems learned from, "improved" versions of documents are, overwhelmingly, longer and more ornate versions.

"Better" is not a direction. It is an approving noise. The model cannot measure against it, because there is nothing there to measure against, so it does the only thing available: it produces more writing of the kind already present. Length is what improvement looks like when nobody has said what improvement means.

Now give it a standard.

    This proposal will be read by a finance director who has three
    competing proposals on her desk and eleven minutes. Judge it against
    four criteria: (1) can she tell in the first paragraph what she is
    buying and what it costs; (2) is every claim about our capability
    something we could evidence if challenged; (3) is there anything she
    would have to ask us before she could decide; (4) is anything here
    that she would skip. Score each out of five, quote the specific text
    that lost marks, and say nothing about tone.

That is a different request in kind. There is now something to compare the text against — four named properties, an audience with a stated situation, and an explicit exclusion so it does not spend its answer on prose style. The model can hold the draft and the criteria in the same context and generate an assessment of one against the other. That is language work, and language work is its home ground.

This is the whole insight of the chapter, and it is worth stating in one line: **a model cannot improve towards a standard you have not named, but it is remarkably good at measuring against one you have.**

## The four moves

The loop has four moves, and the fourth is not optional.

**DRAFT.** Get a first version, using everything from the last few chapters — a real prompt with role, task, context, constraints, format, and where it helps, a plan before the work.

**CRITIQUE.** Ask for an assessment against named criteria. Not "is this good" but "does it do these five specific things, and where does it fail."

**REVISE.** Feed the critique back with instructions about what to change *and what to leave alone*.

**VERIFY.** Check the facts, figures, and sources against the actual source material. Yourself.

Most people do the first and the third and skip the second, which is how you end up in the "make it better" spiral: revising with no diagnosis. A few people do all of the first three and stop, which is more dangerous, because a document that has been through a critique loop feels finished in a way that a first draft does not — and feeling finished is exactly the state in which unverified figures go into board packs.

## Writing criteria that bite

The quality of the loop is set entirely by the quality of the criteria. Vague criteria produce vague critiques: ask whether a document is "clear, persuasive and professional" and you will get three paragraphs confirming that it is fairly clear, quite persuasive, and broadly professional. That is not a critique. That is a compliment with structure.

Criteria that bite come from one question, and it is worth a card above the desk: **what would a real reviewer in this field actually object to?**

Not what would a writing teacher object to. What would the partner who signs this off, the client who receives it, the regulator who might read it, the colleague who has to act on it — what would *they* say in the margin? You know the answer, because you have received those margins for years.

For a proposal, the real objections are: I cannot tell what this costs; you have not told me what happens if it overruns; this claim is unevidenced; you have described your process rather than my outcome.

For a variance commentary: you have restated the number instead of explaining it; you have given a reason without a magnitude; you have not said whether it recurs next month.

For a policy: a member of staff could read this and still not know what to do on Monday; the exceptions are not stated; nobody is named as the person to ask.

For a client letter: you have not answered the question they asked; you have used four terms of art without defining them; you have not said what you need from them and by when.

Notice what those have in common. They are all *falsifiable*. Someone can look at the document and say yes or no, and point at the place. "Be more persuasive" cannot be checked. "Every capability claim has evidence we could produce if challenged" can be checked, claim by claim, and the checking produces a list.

Three rules for writing them.

**Make each criterion a test, not a virtue.** "Concise" is a virtue. "No sentence over 25 words and no paragraph over four sentences" is a test. "Well evidenced" is a virtue. "Every number is attributed to a named source in the text" is a test.

**Name the reader and their situation.** Almost every criterion depends on who is reading and what they are trying to do. A reader with eleven minutes and three competing documents generates completely different objections from a reader who has asked for detail and will read every line.

**Say what not to comment on.** Left alone, a critique will drift to the easiest surface — wording, tone, transitions — because that is what most critique-shaped text in the world is about. If you want structural and factual objections, exclude the surface explicitly, as the proposal prompt above did with "say nothing about tone."

## The hostile reviewer

Once you have criteria, there is a second prompt that earns its place in any professional's library, and it works on a different axis. Criteria ask "does this meet the standard?" The hostile reviewer asks "where would this be attacked?"

    You are the most sceptical partner in the firm, the one who finds the
    hole in everything and enjoys it. You are reading the draft below with
    no goodwill towards it.

    Find the three weakest claims in this document. For each: quote it
    exactly, say precisely why it is weak — unsupported, overstated,
    ambiguous, or contradicted elsewhere in the document — and say what
    the strongest counter-question would be.

    Then name the single thing in this draft most likely to be challenged
    first, and what we would need to have ready when it is.

    Do not suggest rewording. Do not tell me what is good about it. If a
    claim is fine, do not include it to fill the list.

Three parts of that are load-bearing, and it is worth knowing why rather than copying it blindly.

**"The three weakest"** is a forced ranking, and forced rankings are the antidote to the model's default helpfulness. Asked for "any weaknesses," it will produce a balanced list including things it does not really think are weak, because balance is the conventional shape of feedback. Asked for the three weakest, it has to order them, and ordering is where the useful judgement lives. I usually read the first one carefully and the third one sceptically.

**"Quote it exactly"** anchors the critique to the actual text. Without it you get commentary about the document in general — "the section on delivery could be stronger" — which tells you nothing you did not already suspect. With it you get a sentence you can look at and either defend or delete.

**"Do not tell me what is good about it"** matters more than it looks. Unprompted, a critique will open with two paragraphs of praise, because that is how feedback is conventionally structured in the writing these systems learned from. Those paragraphs are not information. They are a genre convention, and worse, they are pleasant to read, which means they colour how you receive the criticism that follows.

Be warned about one thing: a hostile reviewer prompt will sometimes manufacture objections. Asked for three weaknesses, it will find three, even in a document with one. This is not a fault to be fixed, it is the price of the instruction — and it is a cheap price, because you are reading the objections with your own professional judgement engaged. You are not obliged to accept any of them. An objection you dismiss in five seconds has cost you five seconds. The one you dismiss and then think about again in the car is why you ran the prompt.

## The completeness check

The hostile reviewer attacks what is on the page. The completeness check asks about what is not — a genuinely different question, and usually the one that catches the more expensive errors.

    Below is a draft [document type] for [named audience and their
    situation].

    List what is missing that this reader will expect to find and does not.
    For each item: what is absent, why this particular reader would expect
    it, and where in the document it should sit.

    Then list any question this document raises in the reader's mind and
    does not answer.

    Rank both lists by how likely the omission is to stop the reader
    acting. Do not comment on anything that is already present.

Omissions are hard for people to spot in their own work, and the reason is straightforward: you know what you meant, so the missing piece is present in your head while you read. It is not present on the page. This is precisely the sort of gap a reader who has only the page will hit immediately — and the model, reading only the page, is in the reader's position rather than yours.

"Where in the document it should sit" is a small addition that saves a lot of time. An omission with a location is a paste; an omission without one is a rewrite.

## Why a fresh conversation critiques better

Here is the refinement that costs nothing and improves the loop more than any prompt wording.

**Do not ask the chat that wrote the draft to critique the draft. Open a new conversation, paste the draft in cold, and critique it there.**

The reason follows from the mechanism, and it is not a claim about the model having feelings about its work. When you ask for a critique in the same conversation, the context contains the draft *and* everything that led to it: your original brief, its plan, its reasoning, and the draft presented as its own successful completion of a task. The critique is then generated as a continuation of a conversation in which that document is the agreed good outcome. What plausibly follows? Modest, affirming feedback with minor suggestions. The conversation has a direction, and the critique is generated along it.

A fresh conversation contains one thing: a document, and instructions to assess it. There is no history in which this document was a triumph. There is no prior commitment to the choices it made, because from that conversation's point of view the document arrived from nowhere and might have come from anyone. The critiques I get this way are noticeably sharper, and they are sharper on structure and substance rather than on wording.

There is a second benefit. In a fresh conversation you can lie about the provenance, and you should:

    A colleague has sent me the draft below and asked for a frank review
    before it goes to the client.

That single line changes the register of what comes back, because reviewing a colleague's work is a different genre from admiring your own. It is also, in a sense, true — the draft did come from somewhere else.

This is the manual version of a pattern Chapter 26 covers properly, where independent reviewers are separate participants by design. You do not need any of that machinery to get most of the benefit. You need a second window and thirty seconds. It is one of the highest-value-per-effort habits in this book, and almost nobody does it, because opening a new chat feels like starting over rather than what it actually is — getting a second opinion from someone who was not in the room when the decision was made.

## Make it a rubric, so it is repeatable

If you do a piece of work regularly — and most professional work is regular — write the criteria down once and reuse them.

A rubric is nothing more than your named criteria in a fixed order, with a fixed output shape. Its value is not that any single critique is better. It is that the critiques become *comparable*: the same five things get examined every time, in the same order, so you can see whether last month's weakness has been fixed, and so the quality of the review does not depend on how carefully you happened to word the prompt on a Thursday afternoon.

Ask for the output as a table with a fixed set of columns — criterion, verdict, the specific text that failed, what would fix it. A fixed shape makes the critique skimmable, makes it obvious when a criterion has been quietly skipped, and stops the assessment from becoming an essay. Chapter 12 made this argument about output shape generally; it applies with particular force to critiques, which sprawl if you let them.

Keep it to five or six criteria. A rubric with fifteen produces a critique nobody reads, and a critique nobody reads is worth precisely nothing.

## Revising without wrecking it

Now the move where the proposal in the opening story went wrong. Feeding a critique back and saying "fix all this" is how you get over-correction — and over-correction has three recognisable forms.

**Hedging creep.** The critique says a claim is unsupported. The revision does not remove the claim or evidence it; it softens it. "We will reduce your close by three days" becomes "we would typically expect to see improvements in close timelines." Nothing is now false, and nothing is now worth reading. Run three critique rounds without guarding against this and you will produce a document that cannot be attacked because it no longer says anything.

**Collateral damage.** The critique flags paragraph four. The revision rewrites paragraphs one through seven, because it was asked for a revised document and generating a revised document means generating all of it — and everything generated is generated afresh. The sentence you loved was not protected by being good. It was simply not mentioned.

**Bloat.** Every point in the critique gets addressed by *adding* something, because addition is the path of least resistance. Five objections, five new paragraphs, and a document forty per cent longer that is no clearer.

The fix is to revise selectively and say so, explicitly, in the instruction:

    Below is the draft, then the critique of it.

    Revise the draft, but only as follows.

    Fix points 1, 3 and 5. Ignore points 2 and 4 — I disagree with them.

    Rules for the revision:
    - Do not change any sentence that the critique did not criticise.
      Leave the opening paragraph and the pricing table exactly as they are.
    - Do not soften any claim to fix it. Either evidence it, make it
      specific, or delete it.
    - The revised version must be no longer than the original. If you add
      something, cut something.
    - At the end, list what you changed, point by point, so I can check.

    Do not rewrite for style.

Every line of that exists because of a failure I have watched happen. Naming what to keep is the most important of them — good writing does not survive a revision unless something protects it, and the only thing that protects it is you saying so. Requiring the change list at the end is second, because it converts your review from re-reading the whole document to checking a short list, which takes a minute instead of ten and is the difference between actually checking and telling yourself you did.

And note "Ignore points 2 and 4 — I disagree with them." You are the reviewer of the review. Some of the critique will be wrong, or right about a general principle and wrong about your situation. Accepting all of it uncritically is just a slower way of not exercising judgement.

One more discipline: **stop after two rounds.** The first critique catches real problems. The second catches what the first missed and what the revision broke. The third is where the document starts converging on a bland average — every distinctive choice sanded off because distinctive choices are the ones that attract objections. If round three is producing changes you like, you probably had the wrong criteria, not an unfinished document.

## The ceiling, stated honestly

Everything above improves reasoning, structure, completeness and expression. It is genuinely valuable and it is also strictly bounded, and the boundary deserves to be stated exactly, because this technique is the one most likely to be oversold.

**A model cannot catch what it does not know.** If your draft says the threshold is one hundred thousand and the threshold is fifty thousand, a critique against your criteria will not notice — unless the criteria happen to make it check, and it has something to check against. It will assess whether the claim is clearly expressed, well placed, and consistent with the rest of the document. A confidently stated wrong number is clearly expressed, well placed, and consistent. It passes.

**It cannot verify facts it never had.** Chapter 4 made this point about generation and it applies identically to critique: assessing a claim against a document you supplied is checkable work, and assessing a claim against the world is not, because the world is not in the context. Asking "is this accurate?" without supplying the source invites exactly the reference-shaped answer that chapter warned about — a confident assessment produced by the same process that produces confident claims.

**A critique loop polishes the reasoning and leaves the errors in place.** This is the sentence to carry out of the chapter. Three rounds of critique will make a document with a fabricated citation more coherent, better structured, more persuasive, and more likely to be believed — with the fabricated citation still in it, now surrounded by better prose. The loop can raise the quality of the argument around a false fact. It has no mechanism for noticing that the fact is false.

Which is exactly why VERIFY is the fourth move and why it cannot be delegated to the same machine. The Three-Check Rule from Chapter 4 — numbers against source, sources against existence, reasoning as a chain — runs *after* the loop, against the actual material, done by you. The critique loop makes the third check easier and does nothing at all for the first two.

| A critique loop CAN fix | A critique loop CANNOT fix |
|---|---|
| Structure and order of argument | A figure that is simply wrong |
| Claims that are vague where they should be specific | A citation that does not exist |
| Missing sections a reader will expect | Anything about your client, firm, or file it was not given |
| Questions the document raises and does not answer | A rule, rate, or threshold that has changed |
| Internal contradictions between two passages | A misreading of a document it was not shown |
| Unsupported assertions — by flagging them for you to evidence | Whether the evidence you then supply is sound |
| Wrong register or wrong length for the audience | Whether the recommendation is right for this client |
| Jargon a lay reader will not follow | Whether you are professionally allowed to say it |
| Weak openings, buried conclusions, padding | Its own blind spots — it cannot audit what it does not know |
| Reasoning that does not follow from the premises | Premises that are false |

Read the right-hand column and you will see one thing running through it: everything there requires contact with something outside the text. That is the boundary. Inside the text, the loop is strong. Outside it, the loop is blind — and it will be blind fluently, which is the part that catches people.

## Do this today

Twenty minutes, on something real that has not yet gone out.

1. Take a draft you produced with AI help this week. Before anything else, write down five criteria a real reviewer in your field would judge it against. Make each one a test with a yes or no answer, not a virtue.
2. Open a **new** conversation. Paste the draft in with no history and no mention of where it came from. Ask for the assessment against your five criteria, with the failing text quoted.
3. In the same fresh chat, run the hostile reviewer prompt: the three weakest claims, quoted, with the counter-question for each.
4. Then the completeness check: what is missing that this reader expects.
5. Decide which points you accept and which you reject. Write that decision down — it is the professional judgement in the exercise.
6. Revise selectively, naming what to keep and requiring a change list.
7. Now run the Three-Check Rule against the actual sources. Every figure, every reference. Note how many of the errors you find here were things no round of critique had mentioned.

Step seven is the one that teaches the chapter. Most people finish it with a document that is markedly better argued than the one they started with, and one or two factual problems that survived every round of improvement untouched — sitting in prose that had, by then, become good enough to make them entirely convincing.

Save your five criteria. Next time, they are a rubric.

There is one kind of criticism this loop handles badly, and you will have noticed it if you ran the exercise: ask about voice and you get generic advice about tone, because "sounds like us" is not a standard anything can be measured against — it is a thing you recognise and cannot easily state. Which is the next problem to solve, because a document can be structurally sound, complete, and factually verified, and still read as though it came from nobody in particular.
