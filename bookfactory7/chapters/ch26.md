# Many Hands: Multi-Agent Patterns

Picture the end of a long session. You and an assistant have been working on a document for an hour — a tender response, a policy note, a memo that will go to the board on Thursday. It has been genuinely useful. The draft is on its fourth revision, the structure finally makes sense, and it reads well.

So you ask the obvious question. *Is this any good? Anything you would change?*

And you are told that the draft is strong, that it covers the key points, that the structure works well for the audience — with perhaps a small suggestion about the third paragraph.

Now try something slightly different. Open a new conversation. Paste in two things only: the finished draft, and the original brief you started from. Not the hour of discussion. Not your running commentary about what you were trying to achieve. Then ask it to find three specific things that are wrong.

It will find three things. Often one of them is real. Occasionally one of them is the thing that would have embarrassed you in the room.

Same technology. Same draft. The only difference is that the second conversation had not spent an hour helping to build it.

## The one thing worth knowing

Most of what gets written about multi-agent systems is about architecture — orchestrators, workers, message passing, frameworks with confident diagrams. Almost none of it is necessary for a professional sitting in front of a browser with three tabs open.

What is necessary is one finding, stated plainly:

**The single most reliable quality improvement available to an ordinary user is a second, independent pass that has not seen the first one's reasoning.**

Not a bigger model. Not a cleverer prompt. Not the setting that thinks for longer. Those all help, sometimes considerably, and Chapter 13 is about the last of them. But they help in the way that a sharper pencil helps. A second pass that arrives cold is a different kind of help altogether, and it is available to anyone, today, for the cost of opening a new window.

Everything else in this chapter is variations on that sentence.

## Why independence is the active ingredient

It is worth being precise about why "check your work" fails inside the same conversation, because the reason is the mechanism from Chapter 1 rather than anything psychological.

When you ask an assistant to critique the draft it has just produced, its context contains the whole preceding conversation: your brief, its plan, its reasoning, its earlier attempts, your approvals, its explanations of why it did what it did. All of that is text sitting on the desk. And the model's only operation is to predict what comes next given everything on the desk.

What comes next, after a transcript in which a case has been carefully built, is not a demolition of that case. Human writing does not work that way, so neither does the prediction. The most probable continuation of a long collaborative build is a warm, mildly qualified endorsement with one cosmetic suggestion. You did not get a review. You got the ending that the transcript implied.

There is a second effect underneath it, subtler and more important. Everything the model produced earlier is now context it is reasoning *from*. Its own summary of the source document, its own interpretation of your ambiguous instruction, its own decision that a particular clause was not relevant — those are no longer choices under examination. They are facts on the desk, indistinguishable in kind from the material you supplied. A conversation cannot go back and doubt its own foundations, because its foundations are what it is standing on.

A fresh conversation has none of that. It sees the output and the brief, and nothing else. It has no investment in the interpretation that produced the draft, because it never saw the interpretation. It reads the words on the page the way your reader will read them — which is the only way that matters, since your reader will also not have seen your hour of discussion.

This is not a claim that the second pass is more honest. It has no mechanism for honesty; Chapter 4 settled that. It is a claim about what is in the context, which is a claim about what will be predicted. That is a much smaller and much more dependable thing to rely on.

None of it makes the same-conversation critique loop from Chapter 14 worthless. That loop is cheap, it runs in seconds, and it catches the surface faults — the missing section, the wrong length, the tone that drifted. Keep doing it. But know its ceiling, and know that stepping over the ceiling costs one new tab.

## Pattern one: fan-out

**What it is.** Split a large job into pieces that do not depend on each other, do each piece separately, then combine the results in a final pass.

Twelve supplier contracts to review for one specific risk. Six topics to cover in a briefing note. Forty customer complaints to categorise. Done in one conversation, quality decays as the context fills: the twelfth contract gets a thinner reading than the first, and by then the earlier ones are exerting a quiet gravitational pull on how the later ones are described.

**How to do it by hand.** Open a conversation per piece — or reuse one window and start a genuinely new conversation each time. Give each the same instruction, changed only in what it is looking at. Ask each for output in the same shape: the same columns, the same headings, the same fields. Then open one more conversation, paste all twelve results in, and ask for the combination: the common themes, the outliers, the contradictions between pieces.

That final combining pass is not optional, and people skip it.

**When it is worth the extra minutes.** When the job is wide rather than deep, when the pieces genuinely do not need to know about each other, and when you would otherwise be asking one conversation to hold more material than it can attend to properly.

**What it does not fix.** Consistency. Twelve separate readings will use twelve slightly different vocabularies, apply your criteria with twelve slightly different thresholds, and disagree about borderline cases — and nothing in the process will tell you that, because each piece looks fine on its own. The defences are to specify the output shape rigidly, to include your definitions in every piece's instruction rather than assuming them, and to treat the combining pass as a real piece of work: *where do these twelve disagree with each other, and on what.*

## Pattern two: reviewer

**What it is.** A fresh conversation, given only the output and the original brief, asked to find what is wrong with it.

This is the pattern from the opening of the chapter, and it is the one to learn first. If you adopt nothing else from this chapter, adopt this.

**How to do it by hand.** New conversation. Paste the brief. Paste the output. Nothing else — no history, no explanation of your intentions, no note about which bits you are proud of. Then ask for specific faults, in a specified number.

The wording of that last part carries more weight than anything else in the pattern. *Is this good?* returns yes, always, because that is the shape of the answer that follows a polite question about a competent-looking document. *Find three specific problems* returns three specific problems, because the instruction has made the number the thing to satisfy rather than your approval.

A reviewer prompt worth keeping:

    You are reviewing a document written by someone else. You did not
    write it and you have no stake in it.

    Below is the brief it was written to, and the document.

    Find the three most serious problems with it. For each: quote the
    exact words at fault, say what is wrong, and say what it would take
    to fix it. Rank them by how much damage they would do if the
    document went out unchanged.

    Then answer separately: what does the brief ask for that the
    document does not deliver at all?

    Do not summarise the document. Do not tell me what works.

    BRIEF:
    [paste]

    DOCUMENT:
    [paste]

That last instruction — *do not tell me what works* — earns its place. Without it you will get four hundred words of appreciation before the criticism, and appreciation from a system with no stake in your work is worth nothing while costing you attention.

**When it is worth it.** Anything going to a client, a board, a regulator, or a court of public opinion. Anything you will not be present to explain. Anything where you have been staring at the words long enough to stop seeing them.

**What it does not fix.** Facts. A reviewer that has only the brief and the draft can tell you the argument in paragraph four does not follow from paragraph three; it cannot tell you the figure in paragraph four is wrong, because it has never seen your source. Checking claims against sources is a different pattern, and it is the fifth one.

## Pattern three: pipeline

**What it is.** Stages, in order, where each stage's output is the next stage's input. Extract, then structure, then draft, then check.

The instinct with a hard task is to write one enormous prompt that describes the whole job. It rarely works well, and when it fails you cannot tell which part failed. A pipeline breaks the same job into steps that are individually simple, individually inspectable, and individually fixable.

**How to do it by hand.** Take a common piece of professional work — turning a folder of documents into a comparison you can act on. Four conversations:

1. **Extract.** Give it one source document and ask only for the facts, in a fixed structure: the named fields, one row per item, a note where the document is silent. No analysis, no prose.
2. **Structure.** Give a new conversation the extracted material from all the sources and ask for it in one table with consistent categories. Still no analysis.
3. **Draft.** Give a new conversation the table and the brief, and ask for the memo. It never sees the original documents, which is the point: it can only write what the table supports.
4. **Check.** Give a new conversation the memo and the table, and ask which statements in the memo are not supported by the table.

Each stage is checkable by eye in a minute. And when the final memo is wrong, you can see exactly where it went wrong — in the extraction, in the structuring, or in the writing — which is something the one-giant-prompt approach can never give you.

**When it is worth it.** Recurring work, work with volume, and anything where you will be asked *where did this number come from*. The stage boundaries are where your audit trail lives.

**What it does not fix.** Errors introduced early. A pipeline propagates its own mistakes with great efficiency: a misread field at stage one becomes a confident sentence at stage four, and stage four has no way of knowing, because the table is the only truth it was shown. Sample-check between stages, not only at the end. Chapter 16 makes the same point about long documents, and for the same reason.

## Pattern four: judge panel

**What it is.** For a decision with no obviously right answer, generate two or three genuinely different options from different starting stances, then judge them against criteria you wrote before you saw any of them.

The failure this addresses is specific and common. Ask for options and you get one real option plus two straw men — a recognisable pattern where the second and third exist to make the first look sensible. That is not a menu. That is an argument in fancy dress.

**How to do it by hand.** Three moves, in this order, and the order is the whole trick.

First, before anything else, write down what a good answer looks like. Four or five criteria, in your own words, on paper or in a note. Do this while you have no options in front of you, because criteria written after the options are simply a description of the option you already liked.

Second, generate options in separate conversations, each with a different stance built into the instruction. Not *give me three approaches* but three conversations: *design the most cautious version of this, assuming the regulator reads it first*; *design the fastest version, assuming we must be live in six weeks*; *design the version that costs least to maintain in three years' time*. Different stances produce genuinely different shapes. One conversation asked for three shapes produces one shape in three coats of paint.

Third, judge. A new conversation, the criteria you wrote first, the options stripped of any label that says which was which.

A judging prompt worth keeping:

    Below are my criteria, written before I saw any of these options,
    and three options labelled A, B and C.

    Score each option against each criterion out of five, and give one
    sentence of justification per score that refers to something
    specific in the option.

    Then: name the single strongest argument against your highest-
    scoring option, and say what would have to be true for a different
    option to win.

    Do not tell me the options are all strong in different ways. Rank
    them.

    CRITERIA:
    [paste]

    OPTIONS:
    [paste]

**When it is worth it.** Choices you will have to defend later — a structure, a supplier, a policy position, a way of pricing. The judging pass has a second benefit that has nothing to do with the machine: writing criteria before seeing options is a discipline most professionals endorse and almost nobody practises, and this pattern forces it.

**What it does not fix.** Your criteria. If the thing that actually matters is missing from your list, every option will be scored against the wrong test, thoroughly and with justifications. The scores are also not measurements — they are a structured opinion, and treating a four-out-of-five as a number you can add up is exactly the false precision this book keeps warning about. Read the justifications. Ignore the totals.

## Pattern five: verifier

**What it is.** An adversarial pass whose job is to refute rather than approve. Here is a claim and the source it came from; find every statement the source does not support.

The reviewer asks *is this good writing*. The verifier asks *is this true of the document in front of you*, which is a narrower question and a far more answerable one. It is the only pattern in this chapter that reaches the failure mode from Chapter 4 directly.

**How to do it by hand.** New conversation. The output and the source material — the actual contract, the actual report, the actual policy. Then an instruction that gives it no route to approval.

A verifier prompt worth keeping:

    Your only job is to find unsupported statements. Assume the summary
    below contains errors; your task is to locate them.

    Go through the summary sentence by sentence. For each factual
    statement, one of three verdicts:

    SUPPORTED — quote the exact words from the source document that
    support it.
    NOT SUPPORTED — the document does not say this.
    CONTRADICTED — the document says something different; quote it.

    Every figure, date, name, and defined term gets its own line.
    If you cannot find supporting words, the verdict is NOT SUPPORTED.
    Do not reason from what is likely to be true.

    List the NOT SUPPORTED and CONTRADICTED items first.

    SOURCE DOCUMENT:
    [paste]

    SUMMARY TO CHECK:
    [paste]

The quotation requirement is what makes this work at all. A verdict on its own is another prediction. A verdict attached to quoted words from the source is something you can check in seconds, and — crucially — something that is much harder to produce when the support does not exist.

**When it is worth it.** Any summary of a document that someone will rely on without reading the original. Any set of figures lifted out of a source. Any place where an invented specific would matter.

**What it does not fix.** Everything outside the source. A verifier confirms that the output matches the document; it cannot tell you the document is out of date, that a later amendment supersedes it, or that the whole framing is wrong. It also cannot catch what was left out unless you ask separately — omission is invisible to a sentence-by-sentence check, so add the question directly: *what does the source treat as important that the summary does not mention at all?*

## The five, side by side

| Pattern | What it catches | Cost | When to skip it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan-out | Thin coverage, missed items in volume, fatigue at the twelfth document | High — one pass per piece, plus a combining pass | Small jobs; anything where the pieces genuinely depend on each other |
| Reviewer | Weak argument, missing section, drifted tone, the thing you stopped seeing | Low — one extra conversation | Internal notes, throwaway drafts, anything you would not have proofread anyway |
| Pipeline | Compound failures in complex work; tells you *where* it broke | Medium — several passes, more handling | One-off simple tasks; nobody will ask where the number came from |
| Judge panel | Fake choice, unexamined preference, criteria invented after the fact | Medium to high — several generations plus a judging pass | Decisions that are reversible, low-stakes, or already obvious |
| Verifier | Unsupported figures, invented specifics, claims the source does not make | Low to medium — one pass, plus your reading of the quotes | Output containing no factual claims; material you are going to read in full yourself anyway |

## Keeping the passes actually independent

The patterns are simple. Keeping them honest is where people quietly undo the benefit, usually out of helpfulness. Four rules.

**Paste the output, never the reasoning.** The reviewer gets the finished thing and the brief. It does not get the plan, the earlier drafts, the explanation of why you chose the structure, or the assistant's own account of what it did. Every one of those is an argument, and an argument in the context is a thumb on the scale.

**Start a new conversation, not a new message.** "Now review that critically" in the same thread is the thing this whole chapter exists to replace. The scroll is the problem. A new message does not clear it.

**Do not tell the reviewer what you were hoping for.** *I think this is quite strong, but have a look* is a complete instruction to agree with you. So is *I'm worried the tone is too formal* — you will now get tone feedback and nothing else, whatever else is wrong. Say what the document was for. Say nothing about what you want to hear.

**Where it matters, use a different assistant entirely.** Two products from different providers share fewer habits than two conversations with the same one. This is a real improvement and a modest one; do not overestimate it, for reasons in the next section.

One practical note: independence has a cost in convenience, and the cost is copying and pasting. That is genuinely the whole of the labour. Anyone who tells you the manual version of this is impractical is comparing it to an automated system they are selling, not to the twenty minutes it actually takes.

## The honest limits

This section is the important one, and it is longer than usual on purpose, because these patterns are easy to oversell and the overselling has already begun.

**They do not make the system truthful.** Nothing in this chapter changes the mechanism from Chapter 1. A verifier is a language model predicting text about whether other text is supported. It is better at that than the same model is at criticising its own recent work, for reasons of context rather than character. It is not an authority. When it says SUPPORTED and quotes a sentence, your job is to look at the quoted sentence — which is exactly why the prompt demands quotations rather than verdicts.

**They do not create knowledge that was never there.** If nobody in the chain knows your firm's actual policy, no number of passes will discover it. Four conversations discussing a document none of them has seen produce four confident accounts and no information. Independence multiplies the value of good grounding; it does not substitute for it, and Chapter 29 is where that lever properly lives.

**Agreement is not evidence.** This is the one that catches sophisticated people. When three passes agree, it feels like corroboration — three independent witnesses telling the same story. They are not independent in the way witnesses are. They are built in similar ways on overlapping material, and they share blind spots systematically: the same common misconception, the same plausible-but-wrong convention, the same assumption about which jurisdiction you are in. When they are wrong together, they are wrong in the same direction and with matching confidence, which reads exactly like corroboration and is nothing of the kind. Agreement is weak positive evidence at best. **Disagreement, however, is genuinely informative** — when two independent passes reach different conclusions, something is worth your attention, and that is the more useful signal of the two.

**More passes cost more, and the returns fall away sharply.** Every pass is time, money and handling. The first independent pass delivers most of what there is to get. The second adds something. The fourth adds very little beyond the pleasant sensation of thoroughness, and each additional pass is another opportunity for a confident wrong suggestion to talk you out of something that was right. Two passes is usually the whole benefit. Anyone running six should ask what they are actually buying.

**Review can make things worse.** Accepting every suggestion is not quality control, it is dictation. Some reviewer findings are wrong; a few are confidently, specifically wrong. You remain the editor, which means the pattern is *read the finding, form a view, decide* — not *apply*. If you cannot tell whether a criticism is right, that is a signal about the material, not permission to accept it.

**And none of this transfers accountability.** Four passes and a judging table make a decision look thoroughly examined. Your name is still the one on it. Chapter 63 has the list of things that stay human whatever the process around them looks like.

## A word on the automated versions

Everything in this chapter exists in tooling. Frameworks that fan work out to parallel workers and gather the results; systems where one model's output is piped into another as a checking step; evaluation harnesses that run a judging model over hundreds of outputs and score them. Chapter 24's working assistants can be instructed to do a version of it inside a single session, running a review pass over their own output before handing it back.

Those are real and they matter at volume. If you are processing four thousand items, a hand-run reviewer is not the answer.

But notice what the automation buys: scale, not insight. The quality improvement comes from the independence of the passes, and you get that with three browser tabs. For the professional volumes most readers deal in — a document, a decision, a dozen contracts — the manual version delivers nearly all of the benefit, needs nothing installed, and has the considerable advantage that you read every finding yourself instead of trusting a pipeline you cannot see.

Start by hand. Automate the pattern only once you have run it enough times to know what its findings look like when they are wrong.

## Do this today

Thirty minutes, on something real that has not yet gone out.

1. Take a document you finished this week and are reasonably pleased with. Being pleased with it is the point.
2. Open a new conversation — not a new message in the old one. Paste in the original brief and the finished document, and nothing else.
3. Run the reviewer prompt from earlier in this chapter. Ask for three specific problems, ranked by damage, each with the offending words quoted.
4. Read the three findings and decide, one at a time: right, wrong, or arguable. Write the decision down. You are calibrating a tool, not receiving instructions.
5. If the document contains figures or claims drawn from a source, run the verifier prompt as well, with the actual source pasted in. Check every quoted line yourself.
6. Note how long the whole thing took. It is almost certainly less than you expected, and that number is what determines whether you ever do it again.

The reviewer pass is the one to make a habit. The others you will reach for when the work is worth it — and knowing which surface, which pattern and how much machinery a given job deserves is the question that closes this part of the book, which is where we go next.
